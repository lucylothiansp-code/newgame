## The People of Cradlewrack, Continued

The first half of the highland cast is the Attendance and the things it must answer for. The second half is the country that has to live under the answers: the veiled houses that own the springs, the nurses and scriveners and tallymen who keep the books, the people at the border and the people who break it, a queen who has been pregnant longer than most of her subjects have been alive, and a door on a hill that has been opening for six hundred and forty-one years. Many of them have never met. All of them can feel the interval in their bellies.

#### Who They Are

She is carried, not walked. The Duchess Morwen moves through her house in a high-backed wicker chair borne on poles by four Veil-Wardens in madder livery, and she is preceded by a smell before she is preceded by anything else: warm salt, iron, and the sweetish lanolin the nurses work into the membrane each morning to keep it supple. The membrane covers her from the crown to the collarbones, a grey-pink sheath the thickness of a wet glove, glistening, veined with fine red threads like the inside of an eggshell held to a candle. Through it her face is a pale oval with two darker hollows and a darker mouth, and when she speaks the veil dimples inward over the lips and bellies out again. Her voice comes through it wet and muffled, as if spoken from the far side of a curtain in a warm room. She has never felt wind on her cheek. She has never been kissed except through it.

She is tall, narrow-shouldered, gaunt in the way of the Course-struck, as if everything she eats is taken for someone else, and the house dresses her in loose undyed robes sewn on each morning and cut off each night, because nothing she wears will stay fastened. Her hands are long and pale and nearly always still. Under the robe her belly is never flat for more than a week. Servants learn quickly that she hears everything, that she turns her blurred face toward a lie the way a dog turns toward a dropped plate, and that when she lifts one finger the room is to be silent, because she is looking forward.

The Veiled Council listens to her in silence. Commoners who see her litter on the Tull road cross the lane and knock three times on the nearest tree. To her own servants she is courteous, distant and exact, and she remembers the names of their children, and she does not ask after the ones that went to the Low Wall, because she already knows.

#### Their Story

Iphigene was born in 591 in the deep chambers of Morwen Hall, veiled, as every true Morwen is, and the Attendant who delivered her wept, because the caul was the thickest she had ever seen and the infant beneath it was breathing through it, unharmed, the membrane rising and falling like a second chest. She was the only child of Duchess Ottoline Morwen and Ottoline's cousin-consort; the Cutwrights' locked volume records the union in its shorthand and the house has never asked for a key. She was raised in the Veiled Galleries, taught letters by touch and Lore by recitation, and began to foresee at six, when she told her nurse not to take the north bridge and the north bridge fell in the next pang.

Her mother went to the deep chambers in 609, crowning at the breast, and Iphigene, at eighteen, took the house. In 611 she married her cousin Casimir in the bricked room of the Hall, as the custom is, and was broken out at dawn into a house that cheered. She bore her first child in 613. Over the next twelve years she bore nine times as women bear, and the Second Opinion was asked for each. Three were permitted. Two of those went to the deep chambers before their naming. The third is the Lady Isolde Morwen, now sixteen, whose caul came away whole in the Attendant's hands at birth, and who is therefore, by every law of the house, not a Morwen at all.

The Course took Iphigene in 625. Since then she has carried almost continuously, six or eight deliveries a year, each a few weeks long, each ending in the deep chamber with two nurses, a basin, and a covered dish carried out before she can turn her head. She has never asked what they were. In Plenty of 640, during the hardest pang of the year, the veil went red: not the membrane itself, which is as grey-pink as ever, but everything she sees through it, the forward-blur that has been her sight all her life suddenly flushed and running like light through a closed eyelid. In Carving 641 the other four houses of the Veiled Council reported the same. Their prophecies, which once disagreed about everything, have been unanimous since.

#### Their Place in the Land

House Morwen owns eleven of the warm springs of the upper Tull and half the clay-pits west of Kest; the potters of Rudge dig Morwen clay and the salt-boilers of the ravines boil Morwen water, and every lack they earn passes through the Hall's factors before it reaches them. The Duchess sits first on the Veiled Council, which advises the Attendance on prophecy and decides, in practice, the price of salt. Orla Kest consults her before every Lying on a noble descent. The Rim Road Company buys Morwen cauls. The Cutwrights' College keeps the Morwen genealogy sealed at her request and at her expense.

Her power is real and narrow. She cannot command the Attendance, which answers to no one, and she cannot overrule the King Within, though she can ask the Chamberlain to frame a question so that the kick falls her way. She cannot leave the Hall without a litter, four wardens and a day's preparation. Her real currency is foresight: once a season she dictates to her scribe a list of what will happen in the highlands before the next Unbricking, and the list is copied and sold, and the houses that buy it plan their harvests and their marriages by it, and they are rarely wrong to. Lately the list is short and every line of it is red.

Her routine is the routine of a body that is always beginning. She wakes before dawn in the Veiled Gallery, is washed and oiled, eats a little clay-bread and posset, and receives petitioners through a gauze screen until noon. In the afternoons she is carried along the third-storey bridges to the Foretelling Room, a round chamber with no windows and one long couch, where she lies on her back with her scribe beside her and looks forward. At dusk the nurses come to see how near she is. On delivery nights the house is sealed and the bridges drawn in.

#### What They Carry

- **The silver dish** — a shallow Morwen dish, black with age and engraved with seven doorways around the rim and an eighth, chiselled smooth. From it, at every naming, the house eats the child's caul raw. She has eaten from it three times for her own children. She keeps it by her bed and does not let the nurses polish it.
- **The Red Ledger** — her book of foretellings, dictated since 609, in a scribe's hand. The early pages are full of weddings and floods. Since Plenty 640 every entry begins with the same four words: *When the door opens*.
- **A horn speaking-cup** — a cone of polished ram's horn she holds to her veil to make her voice carry in Council. Without it she can be heard only by someone leaning close enough to feel the membrane's warmth.
- **A cruet of spring-salt water** — for wetting the veil. Dry, it cracks at the edges and bleeds, and the cracks open again each pang. Her own springs supply it. Lately it tastes, she says, of someone else's mouth.
- **The strong-room key** — the key to the Morwen strong-room, in which a locked chest was found open in 588 with a damp, curled thing inside and the lock still turned. She keeps the key on a cord at her throat. The strong-room has been bricked since 588, so the key opens nothing, and she knows that, and wears it anyway.
- **A child's shoe** — one soft kid shoe, the size for a child of two, embroidered with the Morwen door. She will not say whose. The house assumes it was one of the two who went to the deep chambers. It was not; it was never worn; she had it made in 626 for a child she foresaw, and has never been delivered of.
- **A scrap of the caul of Isolde** — the membrane that came away from her daughter at birth, dried and kept in a silk envelope. By house law it should have been eaten. She did not let them.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

She wants the house to continue, veiled and thick-blooded, as it has for three hundred years, because that is the only shape of duty she has ever been taught. She wants, much more quietly, to see her own face once, unblurred, before the end. She fears the red. She fears that the woman she sees standing in the door in the ground with her arms spread is herself, and that she will go there gladly, as Vey stood in the doorway of the Old Lying-In and waited.

**Secret (she knows it):** she has foreseen the day the Dilation opens. She has not told the Council the date. She has told Orla Kest, in a sealed letter, and Orla wrote back one line: *Then I will be in the room.*

**Secret (she does not know it):** the nurses do not destroy what she delivers. On Casimir's orders they carry it down by the servants' ladder to a closed cart, and the cart goes east to the Assemblers' Barn, paid for in Second Table coin. Three of the Parts in Herself were born of Iphigene's body, including the eye that sits nearest the figure's heart. When the Duchess turns her blurred face toward the Sill in the Foretelling Room, Herself, twenty miles away, turns hers. The red she sees is partly the lamplight of the Barn.

#### Ties

- **Lord Casimir Morwen** — her cousin and consort. She married him because the Cutwrights' ledger said so and has never once been able to see his lies, because he never stands before her when he tells them.
- **Lady Isolde Morwen** — her uncauled daughter, sixteen, kept on the lower floors. Iphigene loves her and cannot be seen to. She has foreseen Isolde leaving the Hall and has not forbidden it, which is the only time the Want has ever let her go.
- **Orla Kest** — the Midwife-Paramount, her ally and the only person she treats as an equal. They have attended each other's worst nights.
- **Queen Dowager Sibyl Aubrey** — her great-aunt by two lines. The Queen visits by litter once a year and the two women sit in the dark together, both carrying, and do not speak.
- **Factor Merriam Toll** — buys Morwen cauls. Iphigene suspects the trade has grown larger than the house's own accounts can explain.
- **Ambrose, the Eldest Infant** — tutors Isolde. He is the only person who has told the Duchess what he sees when he looks at her veil, and she has asked him not to again.
- **The Reckoners** — have killed three Caul-line nobles in their veils this decade. She has foreseen a fourth and has not said who.

#### In Play

Player characters meet the Duchess as petitioners at the gauze screen, as hired hands for some task the house cannot be seen to do, or as the unlucky subject of a foretelling she speaks aloud in their presence. She wants from them what she cannot do herself: to go where she cannot be carried, to ask what she cannot be heard asking, and, if she trusts them, to find out what the nurses do with the covered dish. She is courteous, slow, and absolutely truthful, which is its own kind of weapon. She will never lie to a character. She will simply foretell something about them and let it come.

- **The Foretelling.** At a Council supper the Duchess lifts one finger and says that tomorrow one of the characters will open a door that should have stayed shut. The GM must make it happen. The house then hires them to make sure it is the right door.
- **The Covered Dish.** Isolde Morwen, sixteen, comes to the characters secretly and asks them to follow the nurses' cart. She wants to know what her mother has been delivered of for sixteen years. The cart goes to the Barn, and the Assemblers will kill to keep the arrangement.
- **The Fourth Veil.** A Reckoner is in Kest with a list, and the Duchess's name is on it. She knows. She asks the characters not to stop the Reckoner but to stop him from reaching Isolde, whom the Reckoner has mistaken for the heir, because her face is bare and he has never seen a Morwen face.

Under threat she is still; her wardens act. To kindness she responds with a gift of foresight, which is rarely welcome. Toward the god she is resigned and, lately, curious. If nobody intervenes, she will be carried to the rim of the Dilation on the day she has foreseen, she will have her chair set down at the edge, and she will stand up out of it without help for the first time in sixteen years.

### Dolour Pym — the Wet-Nurse

#### Who They Are

She is enormous, and soft, and slow, and warm as a bread-oven, and she smells of milk so strongly that cats follow her along the Lying-In road and sit outside her door. She wears a vast shawl of grey nursing-wool knotted under one arm, open at the breast in the way of every wet-nurse in the highlands, and the shawl moves. Nobody who has spent an hour in Dolour's cottage forgets the moment they understood that the shawl moves even when she is still. There are small mouths on her. Some are at the breast and some are not. Several have not let go in years, and her skin around them has grown up in soft pink collars like the rim of a crowning, and she moves through her low dim rooms with the care of a woman carrying eggs in her apron, turning sideways at doorframes, singing.

Her voice is low and tuneless and never stops. She sings the counting-song, the lambing-song, the Tally read aloud, the names of the dead in the Stillyard, anything with a rhythm. Her hands are huge, cracked, and gentle. Her eyes are pale blue and slightly unfocused, as though she is always listening to something in another room. She has no fear of anything that is born. She has a great deal of fear of people with ledgers.

#### Their Story

Dolour Pym was born in 583 in a carter's house below the Lying-In hill and was a big, quiet, unremarkable girl who wanted only to marry and bear. She married a carter, Hugh Pym, in 607. In 611 she was delivered of her first child at home, with the parish Attendant present as the law requires, and the Attendant asked the Two Questions and answered them, and carried a bundle out of the room tied in the knot that cannot come undone. Dolour never saw what it was. Her milk came in on the third day and never stopped.

It did not stop in a week, or a month, or a year. Hugh left in 613. By 614 the Attendant who had taken her child came back with a basket and a hard face and asked if Dolour would take a birth the Lying-In had permitted and could not bear to look at. She did. It was the first. The Lying-In has sent her something every season since: the permitted births no mother will hold, the old-born that cannot feed from a cup, the Parts the Book allows to stay for study, and, in 627, a born-grown woman of forty who had come out mute and starving and would take nothing but the breast. Dolour has nursed, by her own count, three hundred and twelve. She has outlived most of them. Of those she still has, eleven live in her cottage, and four of them have never let go.

The Attendance moved her to a cottage built against the outer wall of the Lying-In in 619, so that the baskets need not be carried far, and pays her a pension of two lacks a day, three in Lack. She has never asked for more. She has asked only, each Unbricking, to be told that none of her children will be returned.

#### Their Place in the Land

In the economy of the Attendance, Dolour is where the Book's mercy goes when it has no other address. A permitted birth must be fed, and a permitted birth that no one will feed is a Second Opinion delayed. Every Opinion in the country knows that if she cannot look at a thing and the Book says it stays, there is a big woman under the Lying-In wall who will take the basket without lifting the cloth. Orla Kest regards her as an institution. The bricklayers of the lower wards bring her their leftover bread. The Lying-In's own book records her nurslings in a separate column, headed *Pym*, and nobody outside the Attendance has read it.

Her power is the power of being needed and having no ambition. She has none of the Attendance's authority and wants none. She depends on the pension, on the bread, on the goodwill of the Paramount, and on the absolute discretion of the women who bring the baskets. If the Lying-In ever decided to recall her nurslings, she could not stop it. She knows that. It is the only thing that has ever made her sharp.

#### What They Carry

- **The willow cradle** — her own first child's, woven by Hugh in 611 and never used. It stands by the hearth with a folded blanket in it. She rocks it with her foot while she works, and talks to it. Visitors are asked not to sit near it.
- **A shawl of nursing-wool** — grey, vast, re-knitted every season around what clings to her. She cannot take it off in one piece any more; it is cut and knitted on.
- **The naming-book** — a cheap Tally-office notebook in which she has written a name for every nursling who lived a year, as the law allows the one who feeds them. There are one hundred and four names. Small. Keep. Tobin. Ninety. Notyet. Mercy. Bit. Hollan, after the first Paramount, which she thought was a joke and the Attendance did not.
- **A horn spoon** — for those who cannot take the breast. Its bowl is worn thin as paper.
- **A lamp of Cradlewrack beeswax** — for the night feeds, and for the walk.
- **The Low Wall pick** — a mason's pick with a short haft, wrapped in rag. It belongs to Absalom Gage. She carries it on certain nights in her shawl, and it is the reason this dossier has a secret.
- **A pot of caul-poultice** — for the skin around the mouths, which cracks and bleeds at every pang. It costs her six lacks a pot, three days' pension, and she buys it every week.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

She wants every one of them fed. That is the whole of her want, and it is enormous. She fears the Lying-In will take her children back; she fears dying before the ones who cannot let go, and leaving them latched to something cold; she fears, very quietly, finding out what was in the first bundle.

**Secret (she knows it):** the Second Opinion does not always finish what it starts. Vey was the god that kept beginning again, and some of what is carried to the Low Wall in the knotted linen is still warm days later, and knocks. Twice a month, on the nights after a pang, Dolour walks to the Stillyard with a lamp and a mason's pick, listens at the unmarked niches of the Low Wall, and takes out the ones that knock. Absalom Gage rebricks the niche in the morning and says nothing, because he has felt them knock too. Seven of her eleven came out of the Low Wall. Two of them were carried there by Wenna Thrale, who named them on the walk.

**Secret (she does not know it):** her milk is not entirely hers. Since the Shortening it has run salt and faintly pink on pang nights, exactly as the springs do, and in the Lying-In's book an Opinion has noted that the creatures who drink it are growing in step with the Dilation, a stride outward and back by less. The Attendance has begun to wonder whether Dolour Pym is nursing the god's children or the god.

#### Ties

- **Orla Kest** — the Paramount sends her baskets and protects her. If Orla learns about the Low Wall, she will have to choose between the knife and the nurse.
- **Absalom Gage** — the mason who rebricks what she opens. They have never discussed it. He leaves the pick on her step; she leaves bread on his.
- **Wenna Thrale** — the apprentice whose list of thirty-one names Dolour has never seen. Two of the names are sleeping in her cottage.
- **Sabeth Thorne** — once brought her a basket and stood in the door for a long time. Dolour thinks Sabeth will come again, with something from Tarnbrow.
- **Sister Maeve Cord** — calls Dolour kin, because what clings to her has grown into her as a cord grows. Dolour finds this kind and slightly frightening.
- **Dov Marrin** — has written to her three times, offering money for any of her nurslings that are Parts. She burned the letters, and then the Assemblers burned her woodshed.

#### In Play

Characters meet Dolour when something they have rescued needs feeding and no one else will touch it, or when a Lying-In basket goes astray, or at night in the Stillyard, where a big woman with a lamp is kneeling at the Low Wall with her ear to the brick. She wants a strong back to carry baskets, a promise that nobody will tell the Attendance, and, if she comes to trust them, someone to look after her children when she dies.

- **The Knock.** The characters see Dolour at the Low Wall, breaking a niche. What she lifts out is warm and moving. A Stillyard watchman has seen too, and runs for the Attendance.
- **The Recall.** The Lying-In sends word that four of her nurslings are to be returned for a late Second Opinion at the Unbricking. Dolour asks the characters to hide them, which means carrying four things nobody can look at across a country where every door opens by itself.
- **What Clings.** One of the mouths that has not let go in eleven years lets go. What it is attached to stands up. Dolour is delighted. Nobody else is.

To threat she responds by holding on, which with her bulk and Endure is a formidable thing. To kindness she responds with food, always too much. To the god she responds as to any newborn: she offers it the breast. If nobody intervenes, the Lying-In will learn of the Low Wall by Tablenight, and the Paramount will go into Dolour's cottage herself, with the knife, and attend.

#### Who They Are

They are small, dark, quick, sharp-chinned, with ink on their fingers and identical crescents of burn-scar puckered white across their left palms. They are not twins. They do not look like twins; Ede is half a hand taller and broader in the face, and Ada has their mother's grey eyes where Ede has their father's brown. But they move like one creature with two heads. When one reaches for a cup, the other's hand twitches. When one laughs, the other has already started. When one is asked a question, the answer comes from both, split down the middle, one voice beginning and the other ending, with no pause at the join. Their voices are different, and that is what makes it unbearable to listen to for long: two people saying one thought, as though someone were playing a single tune on two instruments set a room apart.

They are charming, and they know it, and they use it. They flirt with clerks in tandem and finish each other's jokes and run small cheerful frauds on the naming-exchange under the Tally hall. They smell of oak-gall ink and the lavender water the Tally-house gives its scriveners against the damp. They hold hands almost always, left to right, burned palm to burned palm.

#### Their Story

Ede Crewe was born in 621 to Joan Crewe, a weaver of Kest's Loomside, and was a normal child for four months, until she pulled a lamp off a shelf and burned her left palm to the bone. The scar healed white. Ada was born in Carving 622, more than a year later, with an identical white crescent already healed on her left palm, and the Attendant who delivered her looked at it for a long time and then asked the Two Questions and answered both of them in the affirmative. Ada stays. The Tally records her as *one, living, scarred*.

They spoke the same first word on the same day, though Ada was a year younger. They had one set of nightmares. When Ede broke her collarbone at eight, Ada woke screaming in the next room and had a white line across her collarbone by the morning. The Cutwrights' College examined them in 630 and again in 639, and the second examination ended with the Lecturer refusing to finish his report, because every sentence he began, they finished, and they finished them truthfully. Joan Crewe died in 636 and they mourned her in a single long sentence that went on for three days.

They went to the Tally-house at fourteen, because Rufus Ochre had heard of two girls who could copy one line in half the time with two hands, and he was right. Since Plenty 640 a third voice has begun finishing their sentences. It belongs to a girl of seventeen in Cleave, Nettle Corse, whom they have never met, who says the last words of whatever they are saying at the same moment they say them, forty miles away, in her mother's dairy. The Cleave parish Attendant wrote to the Tally about it. Rufus showed them the letter. They have not spoken aloud in an empty room since.

#### Their Place in the Land

The Tally is the evening news of Cradlewrack, and the Crewe sisters are among the dozen scriveners who make it legible. Every dusk the parish boards are chalked, and every night the chalk is carried by runners to the Tally-house and copied into the great ledger, and the copy is read aloud at the board in the morning. Ede and Ada copy the northern parishes, Ede taking the left-hand columns and Ada the right, a single line growing across the page from two pens. Their copy is the fastest in the house and has never contained an error.

They depend on Rufus, who protects them from the Attendance's interest and the College's, and on their wages, three lacks a day each, which they supplement by knowing the night's births an hour before the naming-exchange does. They have no power in the land, except that they read every birth in the north before anyone else alive, and they finish people's sentences, and in a country where secrets are mostly about what someone bore, that is a great deal of power for two girls who are not quite one.

#### What They Carry

- **The shared pen-case** — one case, two pens, one penknife they pass between them without looking.
- **The Tally copybooks** — the working drafts of the northern columns, which they are not supposed to take home and always do.
- **A pair of left gloves** — two left gloves, one larger than the other, bought from a glover who asked no questions. They wear them to cover the scars when they go out. Neither of them owns a right glove.
- **A letter from Cleave** — from Nettle Corse, in a round dairymaid's hand: *I am sorry. I do not mean to. I hear you all day. Please tell me how the sentence ends.* They have not answered it.
- **Tamsin Gorse's thread** — a twist of catgut and a fine needle, left with them after the last time.
- **A naming-right** — for a Loomside birth due next Grace, bought cheap, which they believe will be a speaking child and a fortune. They read it in the Tally before the mother knew.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

They want to stay two. They want to know which of them is the original, and they want never to know. They fear that one of them will die first and the other will go on, half a sentence, forever. They fear Nettle Corse more than anything in the highlands, because if there can be a third, there was never really a second.

**Secret (they know it):** they wake, more mornings than not, with their burned palms grown together. The skin seals along the scars in the night, warm and seamless, and they cannot pull apart. Once a month Tamsin Gorse comes to their room above the Tally-house with a knife and a needle and cuts them free and stitches each palm closed, and by the next pang the stitches have opened. Each time the seal is a little deeper. In Lack they woke joined to the wrist.

**Secret (they do not know it):** they are not one child born twice. They are one person born in three Parts, as Parts are born, and like Parts they are drawn toward each other. Nettle Corse is the third. She has begun walking toward Kest in her sleep. Una Clayhand of the Assemblers has entered all three names in the Gatherers' book under a single heading.

#### Ties

- **Rufus Ochre** — their employer and protector, who loves them as he loves a well-balanced column. They would do anything for him, and finish anything he says.
- **Tamsin Gorse** — cuts them apart. They think of her as the only person who has ever chosen, deliberately, to treat them as two.
- **Nettle Corse** — the girl in Cleave. Terrifying, apologetic, forty miles away and coming closer.
- **Juniper Dell** — they met her once, in 640, when the Tally sent them to copy the Tarnbrow register. She said her half-sentence and they tried to finish it, as they must, and both their noses bled, and what they wrote on the copy-page in two hands was one word in the old pre-Gorging script, which neither of them can read.
- **Ruth Ninefold** — the name-broker of Kest, who buys their early knowledge and suspects where it comes from.
- **Una Clayhand** — a friendly woman at the naming-exchange who always asks how their hands are.

#### In Play

Characters meet the sisters at the Tally-house, at the naming-exchange, or because they need a birth found, a record read, or a column checked that someone in power wants left unchecked. They want someone to go to Cleave and look at Nettle Corse for them, and then they want that person to tell them it was nothing.

- **The Word.** The characters find the copy-page from Tarnbrow with its one word in the old script. Ambrose can read it. He reads it, and he goes very pale, and he says only that it is a word for someone who sits down at a table without being asked.
- **The Third Voice.** Nettle Corse arrives in Kest, sleepwalking, barefoot, having walked forty miles in four nights. The three girls see each other across a Tally-board at dawn. The characters have a few moments to decide whether to let them touch.
- **Two Columns.** Rufus Ochre is ill and the sisters must copy the unaccounted entries alone. They notice what Rufus has not: who wrote them.

Threatened, they split and run in two directions and meet where neither was going. To kindness they respond with loyalty and gossip. Of the god they speak little; they have felt its want as a want to be together, and they think that is the cruelest thing it could have chosen. If nobody intervenes, by the next Unbricking the three of them will wake in one bed in Kest with their hands joined, and Tamsin will not be able to find the seams.

#### Who They Are

A stooped, soft-bellied man with a halo of white hair, ink to the second knuckle, and spectacles of thick Vigil glass that make his eyes enormous and wet. He speaks quietly and too precisely, correcting himself mid-sentence to get a figure right, and he loves numbers the way other old men love roses: patiently, possessively, with a gardener's grief when one dies. He is kind to clerks and impatient with nobles. He hums when he adds.

#### Their Story

Born in 581 to a Kest chandler, Rufus was sent to the Cutwrights' College at twelve for his arithmetic and left it at twenty-six, a Lecturer, because the College wanted him to trace bloodlines and he wanted to count births. The Crown made him Tallyman in 609. For thirty years he kept the ledger true and dull. In 619 he began to notice that the Tally of births ran ahead of the count of carryings, the record of every pregnancy the Attendance knows of, by a few dozen a year. He reported it. The Attendance thanked him. By 630 the gap was three hundred; by 640, eleven hundred and four.

Last Carving he began to follow the unaccounted. Each is a chalk entry on a parish board at dusk, in proper Tally form, *one, living, born-grown, at the Hollow Stile*, which no Attendant claims to have written. He went to the Hollow Stile with a lantern. He found a naked man of thirty sitting in the wet bracken by a crowning, who looked at him and stood and walked north. Rufus followed him for a night. By dawn two more had joined, in a line, saying nothing. He lost them in the caul-fog below the Sill.

#### Their Place in the Land

The Tally is law, history and news. Descent runs through whoever bore you, and the Tally is the only proof of who that was; benches of the Lying decide inheritances from its pages; the naming-exchange under the Tally hall prices naming-rights by its columns; the Crown taxes households by it. Rufus commands sixty clerks, runners and scriveners, the parish boards, and the great ledger in its iron-bound press. His power is that no one can dispute a figure he has entered. His limit is that he cannot enter what the Attendance will not tell him, and the Lying-In's own book is closed to him.

#### What They Carry

- **The master ledger** — the current year's Tally, an armful of calf-bound folios. He carries the working volume everywhere.
- **Vigil spectacles** — ground in the Vigil in 615 for a fortune. Without them he cannot read a column at arm's length.
- **A counting-frame** — bone beads on brass wire. He counts the pangs on it from habit, and the beads click by themselves when one is coming.
- **Board-chalk** — a stub of the red chalk the parish boards use. He has begun to find it in his pockets when he does not remember putting it there.
- **A dark lantern** — for following the unaccounted at night.
- **The list** — eleven hundred and four entries copied on long strips, with the parish and the hour. Sixty-one are ticked. Those are the ones he has found walking.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

He wants the arithmetic to close. He fears that it will: that when the last figure is entered the sum will equal something, and that he will understand it.

**Secret (he knows it):** since 628 he has let Ruth Ninefold, the name-broker, see the night's copy an hour before it is read at the boards, for forty lacks a month. It paid for his spectacles and his sister's niche in the Stillyard. He tells himself it harms no one.

**Secret (he does not know it):** the unaccounted entries are in his handwriting. He rises in the night in the Taste's restlessness, walks to the boards, and chalks them, and does not remember. - **Orla Kest** — will not open the Lying-In's book to him. He has asked nine times.

He reacts to threat by clutching the ledger and to kindness by explaining the figures at length. Petra does not know whether that counts.

#### Their Place in the Land

To the Attendance, Petra is a criminal; to the turned-back in the Gate's yard, she is the only door. She charges forty lacks a head, or nothing if the woman cannot pay, and the Company's factors overlook her because she keeps her carts off their tolls. The Assemblers have used her without her knowledge: in 639 one of her clients was carrying a Part, not a child, and it went over the border inside her and was sold in Sallowreach.

**Secret (she knows it):** she knows about Bryony Breech. In 638 she walked three carrying women through the Gate in daylight by telling the Marshal, quietly, that she knew. It was blackmail, and it worked, and she has not dared try it twice.

**Secret (she does not know it):** wherever she has delivered a woman abroad, the soil is still warm. There are now forty-one red patches around the Rim, and each has begun, very slowly, to dilate.

She smells of rose-water and poppy and, underneath, of milk gone thin. Her voice is a whisper with perfect court diction. She is gracious, vague, exhausted, and capable of sudden, startling sharpness, and she thanks every servant by name.

#### Their Story

Sibyl Aubrey was born in 551, veiled, a daughter of the Aubrey kings, and married her cousin Aldous the Eighth in 570. Their marriage was long and childless, which in Cradlewrack is a kind of miracle. In Grace 597, at forty-six, she conceived. The Tally entered the heir with rejoicing. He was due in Plenty 598. He did not come.

Aldous the Eighth died in 601 in the deep chambers of the Aubrey palace at Kest, crowning at the breast, and his son was proclaimed King in the womb. The Attendance attended the Queen every month for ten years, and then every year, and then stopped. In 611 the Veiled Council asked the King, through the Lord Chamberlain, whether he wished to be delivered. He did not kick. Since then he has ruled. The questions are read aloud to the Queen's belly in the Throne Room once a week, and the Chamberlain announces the answers, and the Attendance governs accordingly.

In Carving 640, alone in her bed, Sibyl felt him kick three times, then once, then three, then nothing, and then again, over and over until dawn. No one had asked him anything. It has happened every night since.

The Crown is the Attendance's convenience and the Council's instrument. The Lord Chamberlain decides which questions are asked and how. The Queen decides nothing. She is lifted, washed, fed, turned, read to, and carried to the Throne Room on Councildays. Her household of forty women serve her for life. Absalom Gage bricks her chamber each night, at her request, and breaks it open each dawn.

- **A ring of the Eighth Aldous** — her husband's, on a cord, too large for her fingers now.

- **A knitted cap** — for an infant, made in 597. She knits a new one every Lack, a size larger, and never the right size.

**Secret (she knows it):** in Lack 640 she asked him, alone, whether she might be delivered. He did not kick. Then she asked whether he would let her die, and he kicked once. She has told no one.

**Secret (she does not know it):** the three-one-three is a knock. It is the knock the highlands give a new brick in the Stillyard, three times, and then the answer, and then three again. The King is answering someone knocking. Absalom Gage feels the same pattern in the walls he has laid; Old Hinge hears it on the far side of the Sill. A bent, bald, nut-brown old man with knuckles like walnuts, a long humorous face and a dry pleasant voice that never hurries.
