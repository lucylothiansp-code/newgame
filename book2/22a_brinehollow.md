The bell wakes you before your chest does, if Finn Undertow is in a good mood. Lately he isn't. Lately the chest wakes you first, a fist of cold behind the breastbone, and you come up out of sleep already gargling, and the rope round your waist pulls you back down on the pallet like a dog on a lead, and for one heartbeat every single morning you forget why you're tied and you fight it. Then you remember. Then you lie still and taste the salt and wait for the bell to tell you you're allowed to be what you are.
We sleep five on the floor, me and Hanne and her two and my brother's girl, lashed to the iron ring Hanne's grandfather set in the joist. You undo yourself from the outside in. Children last. You check the knots before you untie them, every knot, even though your hands are numb and the cough is coming, because if a knot's been worked loose in the night you want to know whose fingers did it. This morning they were all good. You say a little thing when they're all good. Hold the line. Nobody taught me to say it. Everybody says it.
Then the bucket. Mine's tin, dented, Hanne's mother's before it was mine. You kneel. You put your forehead on the rim. You let it come. It comes up cold and it tastes of every morning of your life, and there's a thread of weed in it today, and a flea that jumps off my lip onto the floorboard and is gone between the planks down sixty feet to the Under. Hanne bails beside me. The children bail across from us. The whole Mile is doing it, the whole nineteen miles of it, a noise like surf if surf was made of people. Then it's done, and you wipe your mouth, and you look in the bucket. You always look. You don't say you're looking. You tip it toward the window, toward the grey, and you look at the color. Clear. Mine's clear. Hanne's is clear. The little one's has a green to it I don't like, and Hanne sees me see it, and she tips it into the gutter before I can look twice, and the gutter carries it west.
Then the count. Mouth to the plank. Dorrit. Wenna. Old Mags. Josiah. And they answer, wet, one after the other, and you feel your shoulders come down out of your ears. Old Mags answers late. Everyone holds their breath. Then she says *I'm here, I was on the pot, mind your business*, and the whole wall laughs. That's a good morning on Mile Thirteen. You get maybe one in three.
Then bread, which is seaweed bread, which is grey, and a cup of sweet water if you've got the bail-token, which I had, because I bailed Mags's husband last week when his arm was broke. Salt with everything. You stop tasting it. My brother says inlanders can taste it in our sweat from across a road.
Then the walk. Thirteen to Nineteen is six miles of plank. You walk west to work, everybody does, the whole Mile flows west in the morning like water down a gutter, and you try not to think about that. Carts. Gulls. The chandlers rolling up their shutters on Mile Fifteen. The smell of the rope-sheds. On Mile Sixteen the Wringer's lot are hanging a new one by the ankles in the fish-cellar window, and the boy's face is purple and he's smiling, and you don't look. On Mile Seventeen the pier starts to go raw, new wood, pale as a peeled finger, and you can smell the Uncovered through the gaps in the boards: low tide, every hour of every day, low tide that never turns.
Nineteen is where the work is. Harbormistress Drowle's barges come in loaded with timber from the Cradlewrack border, and we haul it, and the pile-drivers go, thud, thud, and the new piling goes down into the mud sixty feet and further, and there's always a moment when it drops further than it should, all at once, as if something below took hold of it and tugged, and the whole crew goes quiet. Then the Harbormistress shouts a number and we go on.
You can see the sea from Nineteen. Most days. A grey line. Some days it's closer than the last time you looked and you feel your heart lift like a fool's. It's never closer. It's the light.
At midday somebody stops working and stands at the end-rail facing west and won't answer. Today it was Peder Swell. We took him by the arms and walked him back to the timber stack and sat him down and gave him water and he said *I'm all right, I'm all right, I just wanted to see*, and everyone said *course you did*, and nobody let go of his sleeve for the rest of the shift.
The walk home is east. Uphill, it feels like, though the Mile is flat as a plank. Everyone's slower going home. At dusk Finn rings and the whole Mile bends over its buckets again and you hear it rolling toward you from the Headland end, cough after cough, house after house, like a wave coming in. Like the tide used to.
Then the rope. Then the knots. You tie your own knot. You tie your neighbor's knot, and they tie yours, and you each check the other's, and you each say *hold the line*, and you lie down facing east because nobody faces west, not if they love their family.
And you lie there and listen to the floor. Sixty feet down, under the Mile, there's water moving in the dark where there oughtn't to be any. Twice a day. Every day. You lie there and you listen to it come up and go out again, and you count your people breathing, and some nights, God of the deep forgive me, I lie there and I think: it's warmer down there. It sounds warmer.
That's a day. That's all of it. I don't know why I wanted it written down. Hanne says it's because when it stops being ordinary nobody will believe it ever was.

The core chapter on Brinehollow tells how the coast ate Dromm and what Dromm is doing about it. This chapter walks the ground. It goes plank by plank down the Mile and town by town along the dry shore, and it shows what bread costs and who pays the rope duty. It sets out what happens to a woman who ties her husband's knot too loose, and which hymn the Admiralty's chaplains sing when the dusk bell rings. Then it turns to the people: who holds the lines of this country, and who is quietly cutting them.

Brinehollow is a land of chores. That is the first thing a Carver should understand about it. In Sallowreach the horror is endlessness, and in the Fatlands it is appetite. In Brinehollow it is maintenance. Twice a day, every day, for six centuries, every soul on the coast has knelt and coughed the god up into a bucket, and every night every soul has been tied down by someone who loves them. The horror lives in the routine, and in the morning the routine fails.

## Districts and Settlements

> Long pier, short life.
>> — Mile proverb

### Lowmark, Mile by Mile

Lowmark is a road pretending to be a city. Its old heart stands on the Headland, the original shore, a crown of grey stone and slate roofs above a beach that has not seen a wave in a century. From the Headland the Mile runs west across the dry seabed for nineteen miles on tarred pilings sixty feet high: a single wooden avenue, forty to eighty feet wide, with houses built out over its edges on brackets and stilts, so that from below it looks like the spine of a great fish with its ribs hanging down. Every mile is marked by an iron post with a number and a bell. Every mile is a parish with its own Bailers' Warden, its own tide-post and its own grievances against the miles on either side.

Nobody in Lowmark asks where you live. They ask what mile you are, and they know from the answer how much you earn, how you smell and how likely you are to be Called by Tablenight.

#### The Headland

The old city. Grey stone, steep lanes, slate roofs furred with salt, and the four great buildings of the state facing one another across Tidepost Square: the Admiralty House, the Sounding Hall, the Salt Assay's sealed and half-empty offices, and the vast low bulk of Fathomhouse, which goes further down than it goes up. Officers, clerks, priests and the families of the Lung-line cadets live here in tall narrow houses whose cellars are tiled to the ceiling in white glaze, so the indoor tide leaves no stain. The Headland smells of wet stone, coal smoke and the green, mineral breath that rises from the gratings of Fathomhouse. It sounds of bells: the Admiralty's watch bells, the tide bell in Finn Undertow's tower, and the dull bong of the Sounding Hall's lie-bell, which rings when the pool rises.

The Headland trades in orders, appointments and permits. Every salvage licence, every pier contract and every Rim Road carting bond is stamped here, in wet wax. Its custom is the **Dusk Muster**: at the evening bell, every household with an officer in it stands on its front step in dress uniform and bails in unison, facing east toward the Admiralty flag. Its danger is the gratings. The Fathomhouse drains vent into the square, and on pressure days the air above them thickens until passers-by drop where they stand with blood at the ears. Twice in living memory a child has been found curled asleep on a grating at dawn, perfectly unhurt, speaking in a voice an octave too low.

#### Mile One: the Gilt Mile

The first mile west of the Headland is the oldest pier and the richest street in the country. Its houses are four storeys of painted timber with copper gutters, glass bow-windows (all on the east side) and private bail-rooms tiled in Cradlewrack red. Commodores, successful salvage-brokers, Rim Road factors and the Salt Assay's surviving senior clerks live here. It smells of beeswax, cedar shavings and good tobacco. Gilt Mile households keep **window-wardens**, servants whose only duty is to stand between the family and the west-facing glass at dusk, drawing curtains and gently turning faces away.

The trade is credit. The Gilt Mile lends to every other mile, against pier-rights and against the future value of salvage that has not yet been uncovered. The danger is quieter than elsewhere: the rich are Called less often because they are anchored better, with silk-and-wire ropes and private Netwatch men on retainer, but when they go they go in numbers. Whole households have walked together, hand in hand, servants and all, down the Gilt Mile's ornamental stairs and away across the salt.

#### Miles Two to Four: the Ropewalk and the Chandlers' Reach

Here the Mile is a workshop. The **Ropewalk** of Ottiline Hawse runs a quarter-mile along the pier's north edge in a long, low, tar-black shed where men and women walk backward all day, spinning hemp from bundles at their waists, while wheel-boys turn the hooks. The air is brown with hemp-dust and the reek of hot pine tar from the tarring-house, and the noise is the creak of the wheels and the walkers' chant, which keeps the twist even. Across the street the chandlers sell everything a household needs to stay ashore: rope, buckets, iron anchor-rings, lead bed-weights, oilcloth, lamp oil, salted gull, Saltwick salt, Oathen iron shackles for those who can afford them, and Cradlewrack cauls, stitched into coat linings, which are said to stop the Calling and do not.

The custom here is the **knot-proof**. No rope leaves the Ropewalk without a proving: a length is knotted by the buyer and hung from the shed's beam with a Ropewalk apprentice dangling from it, while the buyer's family watches. The danger is fire. The tar-house has gone up four times, and in a city of tarred wood on tarred stilts, a fire on Mile Three could take nineteen miles of homes. Smoking on the Ropewalk is a flogging offence.

#### Miles Five and Six: the Chapel Reach

The Mile's religious quarter, crowded with chapels of the three doctrines built shoulder to shoulder over the pier's edge, each with a west door bricked up and a bell. Debt chapels of the Theft, where people come to confess what their great-grandparents ate; Lending chapels of the Gift, sweet with candle-smoke, where the Tenanted are blessed at the door; and in the cellars and back rooms, the illegal Deepening congregations, whose lanterns are hung in the windows on procession nights as a signal. The Reach smells of incense, wet wool and candle-fat, and it sounds of hymns sung over the slap of the indoor tide in the chapel crypts.

Its trade is grief: rope funerals, tide-post carving, the hire of mourners and the sale of **name-ropes**, short braided cords with the name of a Called relative woven in, worn at the wrist. Its custom is the **open hour**: from midnight to the first bell, every chapel door on the Reach stands open, and anyone who feels the pull may come in, be tied to a pew by a priest, and sit out the worst of the night in company. Its danger is that the Deepening's people sit in the pews too, and talk softly to the ones who are tied.

#### Mile Seven: Hookside

The Netwatch barracks: a long, grim block of timber built across the whole width of the pier, so that the Mile passes through it by a tunnel, and every traveller walking west at night must pass under the eyes of the watch. Hookside is all bunk-rooms, net-lofts, lantern-stores and the long cold **Return Room**, where the Called who have been dragged home are tied to benches until the dawn bail and collected by their families, or not. It smells of tarred net, lamp oil, sweat and vomited brine. At night it sounds of boots, rattles and, from the Return Room, a low continuous pleading.

Its trade is rescue, paid in Netwatch tallies. Its custom is the **Hook-Toll**: anyone passing west through the Hookside tunnel between dusk and dawn must stop, say their name and mile, and show the Watch their bucket. A traveller with no bucket is assumed to be going somewhere they will not need one, and is detained. Its danger is that the Netwatch is exhausted, short-handed and paid by the head, and the Return Room's tally does not distinguish between the rescued and the recovered.

Nerys Colm lives here, in her sealed room above the net-loft. Everyone on Mile Seven knows which window, and nobody stands under it.

#### Miles Eight to Eleven: the Middle Mile

Ordinary Lowmark. Ropery Row, Bucket Lane, the Lantern tavern on Mile Nine, cooperages, net-menders, bakeries of seaweed bread, schoolrooms where children learn bailing songs before letters. Two-storey houses, families of six or eight, a lodger in the loft, the household's anchoring ring in the floor of the main room. The Middle Mile smells of bread, bail-water, tar and wet dog. It sounds of the morning count most of all, because here the walls are thinnest and the count is a real conversation: names, answers, gossip, complaints, and on bad mornings the silence.

Its trade is everything small. Its custom is the **plank-ear**: in every party wall there is a knot or a worn place, sometimes carved into the shape of an ear, where the count is called. Families are buried, in a sense, by the plank-ear: when a whole household goes, the neighbors cut the ear out of the wall and nail it to the tide-post. Its danger is the knife. The Middle Mile has the most cut ropes in Lowmark, because here families are close enough to love one another past bearing and poor enough to have no servant to sit up with the one who pleads.

Esk Penhallow sits on his own front step on Mile Eight, every night, roped to the rail.

#### Miles Twelve to Sixteen: the Lashings

The slum. Here the houses are shacks hung from the pier on chains and ropes, three or four deep over the edge, so that the poorest live furthest out, swaying sixty feet above the dark. Whole families sleep lashed in a line to a single ring, like a boat's crew in a storm. There is no glass, only oiled paper and canvas, and every window faces whichever way the shack swung when it was hung. The Lashings smell of bail-water, which is poured from the windows rather than the gutters, and of fish-gut, tar smoke and the sweet rot of the shellfish middens. They sound of children, rope creaking and arguments.

The trade is labour: cannery work, pier-hauling, gull-netting, rag-picking, the digging of Uncovered shellfish, and bail-water sold by the bucket to Dry Jonah's buyers at half a crumb a pint. The custom is **the chain**: neighbors in the Lashings link their shacks' anchoring rings with a common chain that runs the whole length of a row, so that if one walks, the whole row wakes, tugged. The dangers are many. Shacks fall. Ropes rot. The Purgation's Wringer runs his mangle in a fish-cellar on Mile Fifteen. And on Calling nights the Lashings empty in dozens, roped families walking west together, still lashed, dragging their own beds behind them across the planks.

#### Miles Seventeen and Eighteen: the Raw Mile

The newest pier, still pale and splintered. Few houses yet: timber yards, pile-driver sheds, workers' bunkhouses, Kessa Drowle's survey office (a single room on stilts above the stilts, reached by ladder) and the barge-landings where timber comes in from the Cradlewrack border by cart along the old shore road. The Raw Mile smells of fresh-sawn pine, hot pitch and the open Uncovered breathing through the gaps in the planks. It sounds of hammers, the thud of the drivers and the foremen calling depths.

The trade is the Chase itself: building the next mile. The custom is the **first nail**: when a new section of pier is begun, a worker who has lost someone to the Calling drives the first nail and speaks the name of the lost, so the pier is built toward them. Its danger is the gap. The Raw Mile has no rails in places, and men and women walking home at dusk at the Appetite stop at the edge, and look down, and step.

#### Mile Nineteen: Harbor End

The end of the world. A ring of piers around a basin of grey water, the only real sea Lowmark can still touch, though its edge creeps a few yards further every month. A licensed fish-market, a customs house where the Rim Road Company still collects port tolls, Dagna Hull's iron slip where the *Follow* lies in its cradle, a Netwatch outpost and a tavern called the Last Rail. The end of the pier is a broad platform with a single iron rail along its western edge, worn bright by hands. Harbor End smells of real sea and of what the licensed boats bring back. It sounds of gulls, surf and, at night, the faint sounds carried in across the water from the far deep, which the boatmen call **the Swallowing**.

The trade is fish, or what passes for it now: the licensed boats come back with eyeless things, with fish whose ribs show through translucent flanks, with great slow eels that have to be killed three times. The custom is **the Rail**: those who have lost someone to the Calling come to Harbor End once a year and stand with both hands on the iron rail at dusk and say the lost one's name to the sea. The danger is the Rail itself. Every year a few of those who come to say the name are still standing at the rail when the Netwatch makes its last round, and do not answer, and must be pried off it finger by finger.

#### The Underpier

Sixty feet below the Mile, on the old seabed, in the permanent twilight under the planks, lives a city nobody counts. The Underpier runs the whole nineteen miles: a lattice of pilings like a drowned forest, hung with the drippings of everything above. Bail-water rains down from the Lashings. Refuse falls. Bodies fall. The mud is black and soft and wet, and twice a day, though no sea reaches it, it floods ankle-deep with cold clear water that rises from beneath and drains away again, leaving kelp and crabs. People live down here: the debt-runaways, the unlicensed, the Purgation's outcasts, the families of the Called who could not bear the count, a few of the Tenanted whose families would not have them back, and the **Pickers**, who live on what the Mile drops.

The Underpier smells of rot, wet timber, excrement and the deep. It sounds of dripping, always, and of the pilings groaning, and at night of a soft, communal humming from the Tenanted camps between Miles Ten and Eleven. Its trade is salvage of the Mile's waste, and a busy black market in cut ropes, stolen buckets and the rings and buttons of the Called, which the indoor tide brings up in the mud. Its custom is **the dropped name**: when someone on the Mile above is Called, an Underpier child goes and stands beneath their house at the next tide and says the name, because the Under believes the lost hear best from below. Its dangers are the falling, the cold and the things that come up through the mud on the flood: crabs too large and too soft, and once, in 638, a hand, very long and grey, that groped along three miles of the Under at dawn and drew back down into the seabed as the water fell.

[box: Running the Mile]
Lowmark is one long street, and that is its gift to the Carver. Everything happens in sequence. Anyone travelling from the Headland to Harbor End passes through every district in order, and the walk takes most of a day: about six hours on foot, two by Mile-cart. Use the walk. Let the party pass a rope funeral on the Chapel Reach, be stopped at the Hookside tunnel, hear the Lashings' chain rattle as somebody tugs, smell the Raw Mile's pitch. Let the westward journey feel like a descent and the eastward one like a climb, though the Mile is flat. Brinehollowers walk west to work and east to sleep, and they feel the difference in their chests.

### Beyond the Mile

The rest of Brinehollow is a ribbon of dry shore three hundred miles long, a strip of salt-grass and grey farmland behind it, and the Uncovered in front, widening every year. The old fishing towns stand in a line along the former waterline like teeth along a jaw, most of them several miles from any sea, most of them half-empty. Each has followed the water in its own way, or refused to.

#### Saltwick

The country's market town, thirty miles inland on the Rim Road spur, where the coast does its buying and selling with the rest of the Table. Low stone houses, a great covered market, a Company waystation and a horse fair. Saltwick's cellars flood twice a day like every cellar in the land, and two centuries ago the townsfolk gave up fighting them and turned them into salt-pans. Every house in Saltwick gathers its own indoor tide in shallow lead trays, dries it on racks by the kitchen fire, and sells the salt. The whole town smells of the sea, though the sea is fifty miles off. Its sound is the scrape of salt-rakes, the market crier and the long horns of the Rim Road carters coming down from the Lowmark Spur.

The trade is Saltwick salt, salvage from the Uncovered, Fatlands meat in brine coming up, and bail-water in green glass going east in Dry Jonah's wagons. The custom is the **salt-gift**: a Saltwick family gives a twist of its own cellar-salt to every guest, and the guest must eat a pinch at once. The danger is the salt itself. Outsiders who eat too much of it begin to dream of drowning, and some begin to bail, and the Company's waystation keeps a quiet list of carters who came down for one season and never left.

#### Keelrow Prop Town

Three miles south of the Headland, beneath the four hundred propped warships of the Dry Fleet, stands a town of tents, sheds and lean-tos built among the props, where the sailors' families have lived since 588. Children grow up in the shadow of hulls, and can name every ship by the shape of its keel above their heads. Keelrow smells of tar, laundry, cooking smoke and barnacle-scrapings, because the High Admiral's weekly inspection orders the hulls scraped of barnacles that are not there, and the scrapers scrape anyway, and the scraped wood is gathered for kindling. It sounds of bosuns' pipes, drills and the wind in four hundred sets of rigging, a vast, aimless creak like a forest of masts complaining.

The trade is the fleet's pay, and the camp-followers who live on it: laundresses, cooks, cobblers, chaplains and gin-sellers. The custom is the **launching**: when a child of Keelrow comes of age, the family hauls them up to the deck of their father's or mother's ship and holds them over the rail, facing the salt flats, and lets go, and the child drops into a net the crew holds below. They call it learning to fall in. The danger is the props. They rot. In 633 the *Constant* came off her cradle in a gale and lay down on forty tents.

#### Slabside

The fishing village above Longslab, the beach of the Beaching, two hours' walk north of the Headland. It is the oldest settlement on the coast and the least changed: grey cottages roofed with turf, all their doors facing inland, all their west walls blind. The ground of Longslab is always wet, and the wet creeps into Slabside's lanes: puddles that do not dry, moss on the doorsteps, salt-rime on the washing. Slabside smells of the deep, the smell the survivors described, a tide pool sealed for a thousand years. It sounds of nothing much. The people speak softly and seldom, and the gulls will not land.

The trade is pilgrimage: Deepening faithful who come to lie face-down on the stones and listen, Gift-doctrine families who come to pray where the god gave itself, and the morbid. Slabside sells them lodging, rope, and pebbles from the beach, which are always damp. The custom is **the listening**: once in their lives, every Slabside child is taken down to Longslab at the turn of the tide and laid face-down on the stones for a full hour by their grandparents, and asked afterward what they heard. The answers are written in the village book. The book is very old. The answers have changed. The danger is that the beach is breathing harder each year, and the middens beneath it are shifting, and last Carving a fisherman's cottage on the edge of Slabside sank two feet in a night, level, as though something had taken a breath underneath it.

[quick] Grandam Weir Sollis, keeper of the Slabside listening book — Threat 2 (Lore, Instinct) · Flesh 9 · Guard 10 · Attack — · Armor 0 · Dread 0. For four hundred years the children said they heard the sea. For the last eleven, every child has said they heard someone counting.

#### Basketfoot

The village at the foot of the Hanging Abbey, forty miles north of Lowmark, among the dry white boulders that were once the sea floor. A few dozen stone huts, a hostel for pilgrims, a winch-house and the great baskets in which food, water and letters are hauled up four hundred feet to the monks, and in which their waste comes down. Basketfoot smells of boiled oats, rust and the cliff's own runoff, which comes down at dawn and dusk in sheets when the thousand Anchorites bail. It sounds of the winches, the creak of the chains above and, at night, the monks' prayers, which drift down like the noise of a distant crowd.

The trade is supplying the Abbey, and taking in those who come to climb it. The custom is **the basket-letter**: anyone may send a letter up to a monk in the food basket, and the monk must read it before he eats. Most letters are from wives and mothers. The danger is falling: things from the cliff, and people. Each year a few chains fail or are worked loose, and what comes down is gathered by the villagers in a long cart and buried, face-down, in the boulder field, with its collar still on.

#### Gullcry

The village at Gullcry Strand, north of Lowmark, where the Deepening's shell road begins. Officially a fishing hamlet. In practice it is the Deepening's country: a straggle of whitewashed cottages with lantern-hooks on every doorpost, shell-paths, and a chapel to nobody with its west door wide open. The faithful who live here sleep unanchored, as an act of devotion, and the village is quieter each Carving. Gullcry smells of lamp oil and crushed shell, which is ground constantly in hand-mills for the road. It sounds of singing, softly, at all hours.

The trade is lanterns, oil, shell and the hospitality of procession nights, when thousands come. The custom is the **open bed**: every bed in Gullcry has its ropes cut and hung over the headboard, as a sign that the sleeper is ready. The danger is the welcome itself. Visitors are fed, sheltered and loved in Gullcry, and the Low Songs are sung to them as they fall asleep, and many visitors who came to fetch a son or daughter home have instead been seen in procession with a lantern, a month later, smiling.

[quick] Wader Linnet Shore, Gullcry's innkeeper and Lantern-Bearer of the Deepening — Threat 3 (Persuade, Rites) · Flesh 10 · Guard 11 · Attack +1 (fist 1) · Armor 0 · Dread 0. She has fed every pursuing parent who came to Gullcry since 619, and she remembers which ones went home.

#### Weirholm

The farm country, forty to sixty miles inland in the low hills toward the Rim, where Brinehollow grows its barley, kale and sheep. Weirholm is a scatter of stone farmsteads and a market cross. The sea has never been in sight of it, and yet every cellar in Weirholm floods twice a day like every cellar on the coast, and the farm children bail like fisher children. Weirholm smells of sheep, peat smoke and, faintly, inexplicably, the sea. It sounds of sheep, dogs and the wind in the hedges.

Weirholm trades food to the coast: barley for seaweed bread, mutton, cheese, and above all **sweet water** from its springs, carted down to the Headland cisterns in sealed casks under Admiralty guard. Without Weirholm the coast would have nothing to drink but rain, and the rain is salt. The custom is **the west room**: every farmhouse has one room with a west window, kept empty, swept and locked, where a family member at the Appetite is shut at dusk with the window shuttered, to stand at it if they must. The danger is distance. When a Weirholm farmer is Called, they walk sixty miles west across country, through fields and hedges and rivers, over two or three days, and the farms they pass leave their gates open, because it is very bad luck to stop the Called on your land.

[quick] Dame Ysolt Haggard, the Admiralty's Water-Warden of Weirholm, who seals the spring casks — Threat 3 (Clause, Intimidate) · Flesh 12 · Guard 11 · Attack +3 (crossbow 4) · Armor 1 · Dread 0. Two of her springs have begun to taste of salt. She has reported neither.

#### Brackmouth

The southern port, on the Fatlands march, at the dry mouth of the River Brack. Once the second harbor of the coast, now a town at the head of a long, empty estuary of cracked mud, its quays standing twenty feet above nothing. Brackmouth lives on the Fatlands trade: barrels of meat in brine come up the march road from the south, and Brinehollow salt and cured gull go down. It is a rough, loud, greasy town, half-Fatlander in its manners, where Brinehollowers eat hot food without shame and Fatlanders learn to bail. It smells of brine, rendering and cheap gin. It sounds of carts, barrel-drays and drunken argument in two accents.

The custom is **the double grace**: at Brackmouth tables the Fatlands thanks to the field and the Brinehollow "keep you dry" are both said, and a guest who forgets either pays for the round. The danger is the meat. Brackmouth's coopers know which barrels came from the Fatlands' far fields after the Reaping, and they mark them with a tiny notch of their own. And in the dry estuary mud, the Brack's bed, salvagers have begun to find bodies laid face-down in rows, weighted with stones, in the old Brinehollow manner, which nobody in Brackmouth admits to having laid.

#### Skerrow

The northernmost town of the coast, near the Cradlewrack border, built on a red-stone headland where the cliffs of Brinehollow begin to turn into the clay highlands. It is a town of quarrymen and wreckers, hard, closed and suspicious, and it buys more Cradlewrack cauls than all the rest of the coast together. Skerrow smells of wet red stone, smoke and fish-oil. It sounds of the quarry hammers and the bell of its single chapel, which rings a different tide from Lowmark's, because Skerrow's bellman refused Finn Undertow's change and still rings the sea's tide, as his fathers did.

The custom is the **caul-coat**: every Skerrow child is sewn into a coat with a Cradlewrack caul in the lining at birth, and the coat is let out every year and never taken off. The danger is that the cauls arriving from the highlands are warm now when they are packed, and warmer when they arrive. Last Grace three Skerrow children woke with their coats grown fast to their backs, the caul knitted into the skin between the shoulders. They are well. They do not bail any more. Nobody in Skerrow is sure what to make of that.

[quick] Bellman Arno Skerrow, who will not ring the body's tide — Threat 2 (Labor, Resolve) · Flesh 12 · Guard 10 · Attack +2 (fist 1) · Armor 0 · Dread 0. His town bails out of step with the rest of the country, and he says that when the moment comes, Skerrow will be the town that is still coughing.

#### The Wreck Orchard Camp

Eight miles out on the Uncovered, in the shallow basin where two hundred wrecks stand upright in rows, salvagers have made a town in the newest hulls: a lawless settlement of perhaps three hundred, with a tavern in the hold of a Company barque, a chapel in a gun-deck and a market on the mud between the keels on the day after Low Water. It smells of rot, tar, the hot oil of lanterns and the deep green stink of leather-weed. It sounds of gulls and wind in shredded rigging, and at night, from the oldest wrecks, of lantern-lit activity in empty holds.

The trade is salvage: brass, iron, coin, ballast, the cargoes of six hundred years of wrecks, and things off the far Uncovered that the Headland's licensed brokers will not handle. The custom is **the hatch-rule**: no salvager enters a wreck whose hatches are closed, and no one closes a hatch. All the wrecks' hatches were open when the water left, and the camp keeps them that way. The danger is the old wrecks at night, and the salvagers who go into them to see who lit the lanterns, and come out in the morning walking west.

## Roads and Ways

> The Mile goes one way. Everything else goes round.
>> — Mile-carter's saying

Brinehollow has two kinds of road: those that run along the coast, north and south, and those that run west, out onto the Uncovered. The first kind is old, slow and safe. The second is new every year and safe never.

**The Mile.** Lowmark's one street is its own road. Foot traffic flows west in the morning and east at dusk; carts pulled by men or by stunted coast ponies carry goods in both directions, keeping to the north side going out and the south side coming in by Admiralty order. A **Mile-cart**, a light two-wheeled barrow with a seat, can be hired at any mile-post for a crumb a mile, with a runner between the shafts. After dark no cart may go west of Mile Seven without a Netwatch pass.

**The Old Shore Road.** The coast road, running along the former waterline from Skerrow in the north to Brackmouth in the south, three hundred miles of rutted stone and shell, connecting the old fishing towns. It is a slow road, winding round the heads of dry inlets, but it is the only road that does not cross the Uncovered. Timber for the Chase comes down it from the north in long wagon-trains.

**The Spur.** The Rim Road Company's branch road from the Lowmark Spur waystation on the Rim, down through Saltwick, to the Headland. Paved, tolled and patrolled, it is the country's lifeline, and its wagons carry sweet water from Weirholm and meat from the south.

**The Shell Road.** The Deepening's nine-mile road of crushed white shell from Gullcry Strand straight out across the Uncovered, marked by lantern-posts every hundred paces. It is well-made, easy to follow and, by day, perfectly safe. By night, on procession nights, it is lit end to end, and walking it is an act of faith whether or not you mean it to be.

**The Salvage Tracks.** Out on the Uncovered there are no roads, only tracks: lines of marker-stakes hammered in by salvage crews, cairns of ballast-stones, rope-lines strung between wrecks across the soft places. Each crew keeps its own tracks secret. The mud changes with every flood of the indoor tide that seeps up from below, and a track that was firm last month may now go into a sinkhole. Travel on the Uncovered without a pilot is legal and is the commonest way to die in Brinehollow that does not involve the Calling.

**The Under.** Beneath the Mile, the Underpier is a road of its own, nineteen miles of mud and pilings that the Pickers walk by lantern. It is the way to move along Lowmark unseen, and the way the Called sometimes go when they drop from the Lashings. It floods ankle-deep twice a day.

## Bread and Coin

> Sweet water, sour neighbors.
>> — Lashings proverb

A country of fishers lost its fish, and a country of sailors lost its sea, and yet Brinehollow eats. It eats badly, and it eats salt, and it eats on credit, but it eats. The economy of the coast rests on four things, and none of them is the ocean any more: **salvage**, the stripping of the Uncovered; **the Chase**, the endless building of the Mile; **salt**, which the indoor tide gives every cellar for nothing; and **water**, sweet water, which nothing gives anyone for nothing.

**Salvage** is the coast's gold rush. Every year the sea gives up another mile of seabed, and on it lie six centuries of wrecks: Company barques with their cargoes still in the holds, warships of the old fleet, fishing smacks, and older things from before the Gorging. The Admiralty licenses ground by the square furlong, Purser-Clerk Penwarden sells the licences, and the salvage-brokers of the Gilt Mile buy them up in advance and hire crews to strip them. Brass, iron, lead, timber, coin, and the bones of the drowned (sold to Cutwrights and surgeons) flow back down the Mile. An unlicensed salvager who strikes a rich wreck can live like a Commodore for a year. Most strike nothing, and some strike things that should have been left in the mud.

**The Chase** is the coast's great public work: a mile of new pier every year, paid for by a tithe on every household and built by thousands of labourers. It is the country's largest employer and its largest debt. Timber comes down from the northern woods toward Cradlewrack by the wagon-train, and every year it comes from further away, and costs more, and is worse. The Chase is how the Admiralty keeps the poor in work and the Mile in reach of water, and it is how the Gilt Mile's moneylenders keep the Admiralty in their ledgers.

**Salt** is free and everywhere, and Brinehollow sells it to the Table by the wagon-load. Saltwick salt is the best on the Rim; cellar-salt from the Mile is coarser and sold to the Fatlands for brining. Salt is the one export the Admiralty's figures admit is growing, because the indoor tide is rising every year.

**Water** is the great scarcity. Rain is salt, wells along the coast run brackish, and every cellar fills twice a day with sea-water that is no use to drink. Sweet water comes from the springs of Weirholm, carted to the Headland cisterns in sealed casks and sold by the cup at Admiralty windows on every mile. A cup costs a bail-token. A family of five needs perhaps ten cups a day for drinking and cooking. Everything else, from washing to laundry to the brewing of the thin bitter Mile ale, is done in salt. The poor are thirsty all their lives, and they live in a land where their own chests fill with water twice a day.

**Bail-water** is a minor trade with a major shadow. Dry Jonah's agents buy it by the bucket in the Lashings at half a crumb a pint, bottle it in green glass, and sell it inland. Families in the Lashings sell their dawn bail to his buyers every morning. Nobody asks what it is for. The price has doubled in two years.

### The Duties

The Admiralty raises money as a navy does, by levy and by impressment, and its taxes are named for ships' business.

- **The Pier Tithe.** One lack in ten of every Lowmark household's earnings, collected by the parish Purser at each mile-post on the first day of each season, toward the Chase. Households that cannot pay can work it off on the Raw Mile.
- **The Rope Duty.** A crumb on every fathom of rope sold, collected at the Ropewalk. It is the most hated tax in the country, because it falls on the one thing nobody can do without, and the Admiralty knows it, and has doubled it twice.
- **The Keel Levy.** Every family in Keelrow pays a lack a month toward the upkeep of its ship's props, in return for living under it.
- **Port Tolls.** The Rim Road Company still pays the Admiralty full harbor dues for the port of Lowmark, on the grounds that it is still a port, and the Admiralty still collects them. Reeve Calder Stokes has noticed that the Company has paid these dues without complaint for a hundred and twenty years on a harbor that has not taken a Company ship since 558, and has begun to wonder what it has been buying.
- **Impressment.** Debtors who cannot pay any of the above are pressed into the Dry Fleet for a term of years, to stand watches on a ship that will not sail.

### Who Is Rich

The House of Vasht is rich beyond counting and cannot spend a crumb of it, because none of its princes can leave their tanks long enough to want anything. The Gilt Mile's salvage-brokers and moneylenders are the true rich of Lowmark, and Madam Orla Cushion and her like own the Mile's future in mortgages on pier that has not yet been built. The Commodores of the Admiralty are rich through their squadrons, which are provinces, and which pay them. The Rim Road factors of Saltwick grow rich on salt going out and water and meat coming in. And Dry Jonah, an outlander who has never bailed in his life, is quietly becoming one of the richest men on the coast by selling the coast's own breath to the east.

The poor own their buckets and their ropes. That is all most of them own, and they own those because the law says they must.

## Law and the Knife

> Tie your own knot.
>> — the first proverb, and the first law

Brinehollow's law is a ship's law written for a country, and its first principle is that nobody leaves their post. The **Articles of the Fleet**, issued in 121 and amended by Standing Order every year since, bind every citizen as a member of the crew. Every household is a watch. Every sleeper is on duty. To fail to anchor, to fail to bail, or to fail one's neighbor at the count is not merely a sin but desertion.

**Who enforces.** The **Netwatch** keeps the night and the shore. By day the parishes keep themselves, each mile through its **Bailers' Warden**, who inspects ropes and buckets, records the count and reports silences. The Admiralty's own **Deck-Wardens**, marines in blue oilskin with cutlasses, guard the Headland, the sweet-water cisterns and the Salt Assay, and carry out the Bench's sentences. On the Uncovered nobody enforces anything except the salvage crews, with their own knives.

**How a trial works.** Every case of consequence goes before the **Drowned Bench** in the Sounding Hall. The accused is brought in at the turn of the tide and stood barefoot in the shallow pool, which is fed by the indoor tide and always cold. Across the pool, on the stone bench beneath the frieze of the Beaching, sit three Tenanted judges, utterly still. A **Sounding Clerk** stands at the side with a brass gauge sunk in the water and a stick of chalk. The charge is read. Witnesses stand in the pool one at a time and speak. The accused may speak, or may hire a **Speaker for the Dry**, an advocate, usually Oathen-trained, who stands beside them in the water. Nobody questions the judges and the judges rarely question anyone. When a statement is made, the Clerk watches the gauge. If the water rises, he marks the line in chalk and rings the lie-bell once.

Nobody can say whether the pool really rises at a lie. The Clerks swear it does. The Bench does not need it to. When the hearing is done, the judges speak the verdict together, in two voices each, six voices in all, and the lower voices sometimes finish first. There is no appeal. No verdict of the Drowned Bench has been successfully overturned in four centuries, and since the Bench grew from nine to ninety, every Tenanted judge in the country has voted the same way in every case this year.

**Sentences.** The Bench's sentences are naval and few. **Flogging with a wet rope** is the common punishment for theft and brawling. **Fines** in lacks are paid to the parish. **Impressment** sends debtors and petty criminals to the Dry Fleet. **Bucketing**, confiscating the offender's bail-bucket for a day and a night so that they must beg every neighbor to bail them, is a shaming punishment for slander and loose knots. **Hanging** from the yardarm of the *Steadfast* is reserved for treason and for officers who say "stranded." And above all there is **loosing**: the convict is sentenced to sleep unanchored for a set number of nights, under guard, with the Netwatch ordered not to intervene. Loosing for a night is a gamble. Loosing for a season is death by inches. Loosing for life is death, and the Bench hands it down with what looks like tenderness.

**The knife in the night.** The law's great failure is that its worst crime is also its commonest act of love. When a rope is cut, the Bench must decide whether it was cut by the sleeper, by a stranger, by no one, or by the husband who could not bear to hear his wife beg any longer. Ottiline Hawse at the Ropewalk is called as a witness in nearly every such case, to read the cut. The Bench listens to her, and to the family, and looks at the accused for a long time with its black eyes. Then it often finds that the rope was cut by the sleeper, and the family goes home, and nobody believes it, and everybody is grateful.

## The Faithful

> Hold us down, O Fathom, as you held the drowned,
> that we may not rise up and frighten those we love.
>> — the oldest line of the Admiralty hymn, older than the Gorging

Before the Meal, Brinehollow prayed to Dromm as a keeper: the god who held the drowned down, held the storms under and kept the cold water beneath the warm. The old prayers ask to be *held*. The coast still uses them, and now they are said by people who are held, every night, by rope.

### The Three Doctrines on the Coast

**The Theft** is called **the Debt** in Brinehollow, and it is the faith of the Admiralty, the Anchorites and most of the old fishing towns. We dragged the god from the sea on ten thousand hooks and ate it while it was still moving. We owe the sea everything we took. The Called are the debt being paid, and the Tenanted are the bailiffs, come to collect what we borrowed and to see that the rest is kept honestly. Debt chapels are plain and bare, with a tide-post in the nave on which the names of the parish's Called are cut, and the sermon is always the same: endure, refuse, and pay what you must without complaint. The Anchorites carry the Debt to its end: if we cannot pay, we can at least refuse to be collected.

**The Gift** is called **the Lending**. Dromm came close to shore in the ninth winter and waited for us, and did not fight, because it loved its people and lent us its body to live on. The Tenanted are its kindness coming home: the god returning its children to their families, gentler than they were. Lending chapels are warm and full of candles, and they bless the Tenanted at the door. The Wreckwives are mostly Lending folk, at first. Many of them leave it.

**The Bait** is called **the Spawning**, and in Brinehollow it is illegal. Dromm let itself be dragged ashore because the god of the deep breeds by being eaten: swallowed by a people, it spreads through ten thousand chests and goes home, a bucket at a time, carrying them with it. The Spawning is the Deepening's doctrine, though the Deepening never uses the word. Mother Limpet calls it homecoming. The Cutwrights' genealogist in Lowmark, Ansby Wrack, has begun to suspect that the Spawning may simply be accurate.

### Shrines and Holy Places

- **Tide-posts.** Every mile, every town and every village has a tide-post, an oak pillar sunk in the ground where the sea once reached, carved from top to bottom with the names of the Called. The oldest, at Lowmark, carries the line "The sea is not going out. It is going in." The posts are the coast's gravestones. People touch them as they pass.
- **West-door niches.** In every house the west door is barred, and in the wall beside it there is a small niche holding a dish of sea-water, a candle and a scrap of the rope of whoever in the family was last Called. It is lit on Tablenight and on the anniversary of the Calling.
- **The Bucket of the Unbailed.** At the crossings of the Middle Mile and the Lashings hang great copper buckets, dented and green, into which passers-by drop a crumb or a bail-token for those with no one to bail them. Parish children like Tolly Brack are paid from them.
- **The *Steadfast*'s chapel.** The Admiralty's chaplains keep a chapel between the flagship's gun-decks, where officers swear on the Articles, and where the hymn is sung every dusk while the propped fleet bails.
- **Longslab.** The holiest place on the coast, and the most feared. The Debt calls it the place of the crime, the Lending the place of the gift, the Spawning the place of the egg.

### Rites

- **The First Bail.** A newborn's first bailing, at its first dusk, is a rite: the mother or midwife holds the child head-down over a new bucket and sings, and the water that comes up is poured into the gutter while the family says, together, "Go west without her." The bucket is the child's for life.
- **The Knotting.** In early Grace every rope in a household is cut down and replaced, and every member of the family ties another's new knot before witnesses. The old ropes are burned on the Chapel Reach in a great tarry bonfire.
- **The Rope Funeral.** For the Called: the cut rope is divided, one piece for each of the family and one for the wall, and the name is cut into the tide-post. The priest asks, "Did the line hold?" and the family answers, "The line held. The sea was stronger." They must say it whether or not it is true.
- **The Face-Down.** For those who die in the ordinary way: the body is laid face-down on the Uncovered, weighted with stones, for a tide that no longer comes. The mourners walk away without looking back. The gulls do the rest.
- **The Wet Welcome.** For the Tenanted who return: the family meets them at the door with a bucket, a towel and a bowl of sea-water. The returned bails, dries and drinks, and is let in. Wreckwives say this rite was made by people who wanted something to do with their hands.

## How Power Moves

> Every rope has two ends, and both of them are being held by someone.
>> — Ottiline Hawse

Power in Brinehollow runs like rope through a block: whoever holds one end feels every tug on the other. The Admiralty rules, but it rules by permission of the tanks above it and the Bench below it, and it pays for everything with money borrowed from the Gilt Mile against a pier that Kessa Drowle knows cannot be finished. The princes in the tanks are the god's own blood and the state's legitimacy, and they are drowning. The Bench is fair, final and not human, and nobody has ever dared ask whose law it is keeping. Underneath them all, the poor tie their knots and call their names, and every year there are fewer of them to call.

#### Who They Are

Corvin Sund is a block of a man, square in the shoulders and square in the jaw, grey-whiskered, with a weather-reddened face that has not seen weather worth the name in fifty years. His dress coat is Admiralty blue gone nearly black, and he has it sponged with brine every morning so that it never quite dries, and so he smells, always, of a harbor at low water: wet wool, weed, the faint ammonia of old rope. His boots are blacked with fish-oil. His voice is a quarterdeck bark meant to carry over a gale, and since there is no gale he uses it on rooms, where it rattles the glass. When he bails, at the dusk bell, on the *Steadfast*'s deck in front of the assembled crew, he does it standing, bent at the waist over the silver bucket, without a sound except the water, and he straightens afterward and wipes his whiskers with a folded square of sailcloth and nods to the bosun as if he had just inspected a gun.

He is not a stupid man, and those who treat him as a blustering relic learn that late. He is a disciplined one. He has decided what is true, and he holds to it as a sailor holds a line in a storm, because he knows what happens if he lets go. Officers fear him. Common sailors, oddly, love him: he knows their names, their ships, their mothers' miles, and he has never once let a Keelrow family go short of rations.

#### Their Story

Corvin Sund was born in 573 on the Headland, the third son of a cadet Lung-line captain, and sent aboard the *Steadfast* as a ship's boy at fifteen. That was 588: the last year the fleet floated. He remembers it all. The pilot that season was a lean young man called Hask Tarn, who let the boy hold the lead-line. He remembers the morning the keel touched mud in Keelrow anchorage and did not lift on the next tide, nor the next, and the old Admiral weeping on the quarterdeck, and the props going in. He remembers that the officers called it a temporary measure. He believed them. He has spent fifty-three years making sure everyone else does.

He rose by competence and by marriage. In 601 he married Elsbet Carrow, a sharp, funny, dry-lunged woman from Weirholm who thought the propped fleet was the most ridiculous thing on the Table and loved him anyway. By 620 he was a Commodore; by 629 High Admiral. In between came the Great Pressure Day of 627, when every window on the Headland burst. The night before, at supper, Elsbet put down her fork and said it plainly: *We're stranded, Corvin. The fleet is stranded and so are we. Admit it, and come inland with me.* He told her she was speaking treason in an officer's house. That night, on a night when eleven hundred and forty people walked, she walked. Her rope was untied, not cut. The knot had been undone with care, by fingers that knew it. He has never let himself think about whose.

The first officer he hanged for saying "stranded" was Lieutenant Amos Prew, in 631. Then Captain Dorran Hale in 634, Sailing-Master Ennis Crabbe in 637 and Lieutenant Jessop Tidewell in 640. He attended each hanging in full dress, and he took each man's signet ring from the body himself. In 639 Brenna Kelp presented her arithmetic to the Standing Orders, and he made it a crime by noon. That winter, alone, he went down into the *Steadfast*'s hold with a two-man saw and a lantern and began to cut through her keel.

#### Their Place in the Land

The High Admiral is the government. He issues the Standing Orders from the *Steadfast*'s deck every first morning of the month, signs every commission, approves every salvage licence over a hundred lacks and commands the Deck-Wardens and, through the Watch-Captain, the Netwatch. His orders need the wet seal of the House of Vasht, which he carries to Fathomhouse in a sealed case each week and receives back through a hatch. He depends on the Gilt Mile's credit to pay for the Chase, on Kessa Drowle to build it, and on the Drowned Bench to try whoever he accuses. He does not command the Bench, and he knows it, and he has never put the matter to the test.

His routine never varies. Dawn bail on the *Steadfast*. Breakfast of salt herring and sweet water. Weekly inspection of the fleet at Keelrow on the first day of every week: four hundred ships, row by row, hulls scraped of barnacles that are not there, rigging tarred, guns blacked. Afternoons at the Admiralty House. Dusk, every dusk, alone at the *Steadfast*'s west rail, both hands on the wood. Then he goes below and sleeps chained by an Oathen iron shackle to the mainmast's foot.

#### What They Carry

- **The Admiral's silver bucket** — made for the first High Admiral in 121, engraved with every holder's name. His is the twenty-sixth. He keeps it polished to a mirror and does not like to look into it after bailing. Worth 400 lacks as plate; never sold.
- **Dress sword** — a straight Admiralty blade (sword, 4) presented at his commission. It has been drawn in anger twice, both times on Calling nights, both times on sailors who were walking.
- **The Articles of the Fleet** — his own copy, oilskin-bound, its margins dense with his annotations in a small, neat hand. Article Nine, the one that forbids "stranded", is underlined so often the page has torn.
- **Spyglass** — brass, Oathen-ground (+2 Search at distance). He uses it at dusk to look at the sea from the stern rail. The sea is nineteen miles off and invisible from Keelrow. He looks anyway.
- **Four signet rings** — Prew, Hale, Crabbe, Tidewell, on a cord inside his coat. He touches them when he says the word in his head.
- **A scrap of tarred rope** — six inches of Elsbet's anchoring line, the untied end, still knotted. He has never sworn on it. He is afraid of what the oath would be.
- **A twist of oak sawdust** — in his waistcoat pocket, from the keel. He carries it because the first night he forgot to brush his cuffs, a bosun saw.
- **Oathen iron shackle and chain** — 60 lacks of the best anchor money can buy; it is locked to the mainmast and to his ankle each night by his steward, who keeps the key.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

He wants the sea back. Failing that, he wants nobody ever to say it is gone, because the day the Admiralty admits the fleet is stranded, the Admiralty has no reason to rule, the Chase has no reason to go on, the Gilt Mile calls in its debts and the poor stop believing that anyone is coming. He is not wrong about that. He fears the numbers. He fears that Elsbet was right. Most of all he fears the dusk, because for a year now, at the west rail, he has felt the pull, and it is gentle, and it is in her voice.

**Secret (the GM may reveal):** the *Steadfast*'s keel is sawn half through. He did it himself, over eleven nights in Lack 639, so that if the sea ever comes back the flagship will break her back rather than float away west without him aboard. A shipwright who examined the hold would know at once.

**Secret he does not know:** his favourite officer, Commodore Lysander Vell, is a member of the Second Table and has been using the Admiral's own seal-case to carry Second Table correspondence to Fathomhouse inside the Standing Orders. And his dawn bail-water has turned blue. Tolly Brack has seen it, in the silver bucket, when the steward tipped it out. The boy has not told anyone, because no one has paid him.

#### Ties

- **Hask Tarn** — the pilot of 588, who let the boy hold the lead. Sund has offered him a commission three times. Tarn has refused, because he would have to swear the sea is coming back.
- **Kessa Drowle** — he cannot rule without her pier, and she has told him to his face, once, the year the timber runs out. He had the conversation struck from the minutes.
- **Commodore Lysander Vell** — protégé, confidant and heir-apparent. Sund trusts him more than any living man.
- **Brenna Kelp** — the traitor he is hunting. He has read her figures three times, alone, and burned them each time.
- **Prince Aurel Vasht** — his liege, whose tank he bows to weekly. The boy asks him questions he will not answer through the glass.
- **Justice Maren Oake** — she tried all four of his hanged officers and confirmed the sentences. He is not sure she agreed.
- **Elsbet Sund** — Called in 627, never returned. He sets her bowl on Tablenight, and the west door of his Headland house is the only one in the street he bars that night.

#### In Play

Guests meet Sund at an inspection, at the Standing Orders, or in the dock when he wants a leaker found. He wants things done quietly by people who will not be missed: Brenna Kelp's copies recovered, an officer's loyalty tested, a rumour about the keel traced to its source. He pays well, in Admiralty commissions and salvage licences, and he keeps his word.

- **The Inspection.** A Keelrow sailor, half-drunk, says "stranded" within the Admiral's hearing while the Guests are present. Sund turns to the Guests and asks, very calmly, whether they heard it. The sailor's life depends on their answer.
- **The Sawdust.** A shipwright from Dagna Hull's yard, surveying the *Steadfast* for a refit, has found the cut. Sund asks the Guests to make sure she does not report it. He does not say how.
- **The Rail.** The Guests are aboard at dusk and see the Admiral at the west rail, hands on the wood, not answering his steward. It is a Calling roll with a man who commands four hundred ships.

Under threat he becomes colder and louder and gives orders, and men obey them. To kindness he is gruffly grateful and does not trust it. Toward the god he is pure refusal, and the refusal is cracking. **If nobody intervenes:** on Tablenight 641 Corvin Sund walks from the *Steadfast*'s deck in full dress, down the gangway and west across the salt flats. The watch sees him go and does not know what to do. Somebody rings the bell for "Hold the Line," and somebody else shouts "Follow the flag," which is an order every sailor of the Dry Fleet has drilled all his life. By dawn, crews of sixty ships are missing from Keelrow, and a column of footprints in Admiralty boots runs straight to Harbor End and over the rail.

#### Who They Are

Kessa Drowle is a short, broad woman built like a bollard, black tar to the elbows and a carpenter's pencil behind each ear. Her grey hair is cropped close because long hair catches in the drivers. She smells of pine pitch, sawdust and rum, and her hands are a map of splinters, burns and two crooked fingers broken by a piling chain. She speaks in measurements, the way other people speak in proverbs: a man is "six foot of trouble", a bad day is "a three-foot sink", her love for her daughter is "deeper than the twentieth mile". Her cough is heavy, and she bails over the side of the work-barges into the sawdust, without stopping her talk.

#### Their Story

Born on Mile Ten in 589, daughter of a pier-wright, she drove her first nail at nine and her first piling at sixteen. She married a driver-man, Tobin Drowle, in 610, and they had one daughter, Wynn. Kessa was foreman of the Raw Mile by 622 and Harbormistress by 630, after her predecessor walked off the end of Mile Fourteen at dusk. In 629 a piling chain parted on the drivers and Tobin was under it. They did not find all of him. She was back on the barges the next week.

In 636 she began to survey the ground beyond the end of the Mile with Hask Tarn, and in 638 she did the sum that has ruined her sleep. The northern woods that supply the Chase will be cut out in nine years. The survey shows that beyond the twentieth mile the seabed falls away down the long slope to the Trench, and pilings will have nothing to stand on. The water, at its present rate of fall, drops off the shelf entirely in the same year: 650. She told the High Admiral. He struck it from the minutes.

#### Their Place in the Land

The Chase is the largest work in Brinehollow and Kessa runs all of it: nine thousand labourers, the timber trains from Skerrow, the barges, the drivers, the tarring sheds and the pay. She answers to the High Admiral and is paid with Gilt Mile money, borrowed against pier she has not yet built. Every family on the Raw Mile depends on her wages. Every moneylender on the Gilt Mile depends on her finishing each mile on time. Her power is real and narrow: she can stop the Chase with a word and bring Lowmark down with it, but she cannot make one more tree grow.

Her routine: dawn bail on the barges, survey at the Raw Mile's end with sounding rod and chain, accounts at noon in the stilted office, the drivers until dusk, rum at the Last Rail. She sleeps in the survey office, roped to a piling.

#### What They Carry

- **Two carpenter's pencils** — one for the true figures, one for the figures she reports. She sharpens both with the same knife.
- **The survey ledger** — every piling driven since 630, its depth and its sink. Admiralty property.
- **The second ledger** — her own: the timber count, the fall of the sea and the year 650. It also holds a list of piling orders she does not remember making.
- **Sounding rod** — iron, twelve feet, marked in feet and fathoms. She pushes it into the mud at the end of every day, and lately she leaves it in a little longer than she needs to.
- **Driving mallet** — (mallet, 3) iron-hooped oak. She has used it on two salvagers who tried to steal timber.
- **Tin bail-bucket** — Tobin's, swapped on their wedding day. She has never swapped it back.
- **Tobin's folding rule** — boxwood and brass, the last foot snapped off where the chain caught it.
- **Oilcloth coat** — tarred until it stands up on its own.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

She wants to finish the Chase with dignity: to drive the last piling, tell the truth to the Mile, and have the Mile still trust her when the pier stops. She fears the twentieth mile, and the day she has to tell nine thousand people their work is done. She fears for Wynn, who works the drivers and stands at the end-rail too long at noon.

**Secret (the GM may reveal):** for a year she has been ordering pilings longer than any sea could need: eighty feet, then a hundred, then a hundred and forty, for ground no deeper than sixty. The orders are in her hand, in the second ledger, and she does not remember writing them. The long timbers lie in a yard on Mile Eighteen. She tells the drivers they are for "the slope."

**Secret she does not know:** someone is driving them. At dawn, three times this Carving, the Raw Mile crews have found new pilings standing in the mud west of the pier's end, in a straight line toward the Trench, driven deep, still wet with tar. Foreman Bastian Gutter has kept it from her. The crews say the Tenanted among them get up in the night, quiet as cats, and come back at dawn with their hands blistered.

She walked back up the steps, wet to the hair, and asked the Netwatch to lock her up. She has heard the invitation in full. She can repeat it. Nobody who has listened to her has stayed ashore.

#### Their Place in the Land

She is a secret the Netwatch keeps and the whole Mile knows. She lives in a room above the net-loft with wax in the keyhole and a speaking-tube she will not use, by her own request. Lieutenant Sable Reef brings her food and books. The Deepening would give anything for her. The Admiralty wants her silent. The Wreckwives want to know what she heard.

#### What They Carry

- **Beeswax** — a pound of it, for the keyhole, the tube and her own ears at Low Water.
- **A slate** — she writes on it when she must talk and does not trust her voice.
- **Her mother's net-needle** — bone; she mends the Netwatch nets with it.
- **A stone from Harbor End** — taken from the bottom step on the night she turned. It is still wet.
- **Sable Reef's bucket** — Reef swapped buckets with her in 637. Neither has explained it.
- **The letter** — she wrote the invitation down once, in a letter she meant to burn. It is missing.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

She wants to know what made her turn. She fears she knows: under the chorus of the invitation there was an eighth voice, lower than the others, and it was hungry.

**Secret (the GM may reveal):** the letter, and what it could do read aloud on a procession night.

**Secret she does not know:** what turned her was her mother's voice in the chorus, saying *don't you follow*: the same words Coralie made Father Ebb swear in 589. And her mother walked in again in 633 not because she was called, but because something else was answering, and she went to stand in front of it.

#### Ties

- **Lieutenant Sable Reef** — keeper, friend, the only person she speaks to through the door.
- **Father Ebb** — her mother's sweetheart. She has never heard his name.
- **Mother Limpet** — wants her at the head of a procession.
- **Prince Aurel Vasht** — has summoned her three times. She has refused, because she likes the sound of him.
- **Coralie Fenn** — her mother. Twice Called. Gone.
- **Ilse Marl** — has asked her to hum the invitation, just the tune, for the chart. Nerys is tempted.

#### In Play

Guests meet her through Lieutenant Reef, behind a door.

- **The Missing Letter.** Roll on the table; follow it.
- **The Eighth Voice.** Nerys asks the Guests whether anyone else has ever heard a hungry voice under the sea. Gannet Shale and Cray Lobbett both have.
- **The Cliff.** Bring her to the foot of the Hanging Abbey at Low Water. Ebb will know her face; it is Coralie's.

**If nobody intervenes:** on the night her letter is read aloud, Nerys takes the wax from her ears, opens the door, and walks down the Mile in silence with hundreds behind her. At Harbor End she turns again, and they do not.
