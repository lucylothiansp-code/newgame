# Sallowreach: The Long Upkeep

*A letter from Teal Mallory, living, aged forty-six, of Weir Row, Lastgate, to her mother Hanne Mallory, Set Aside in the Lofts, Hall Six, Fourth Storey, Shelf Nineteen. To be read aloud by the Reader of Hall Six on the next feast day. Carving, 641 A.G.*
Mam, it is me. Aldo is reading this for you because my hand is no good for the ladders and you know how I am with heights. I am going to tell you a day, the way you used to ask me to when I came home from the weir, so you will have something that is not the shelf to think about.
First bell is still the bell at the Ferrying Post, and it is still flat. I got up in the dark and did the shutters before I did anything else, because the dew-rot came in thick in the night and there were flies on the inside of the glass already, slow ones, the fat green kind. I counted them for the almanac-man like you taught me. Forty-one on the east pane. He says that means a front by Carving's end. I put out the smudge pot and lit it off the smoulder-coal, which Papa lit in the year 590 and which is still going in its tin, and the kitchen filled up with peat and lavender and that was the morning smelling right.
Gran-Gran Ide was sitting up in her chair by the stove where we left her. She does not sleep, as you know, but she likes to be put to bed at night and got up in the morning, so we do it. Her gauze had slipped in the night again, on the jaw side. I wound it back for her. She said, "You have cold hands, girl," and I said, "So do you, Gran," and she laughed the way she does, where the laugh comes out a bit lower down than her mouth. Then I did Grandsire Petter's wraps and Uncle Josse's, and gave Petter's hands their oil, and Josse asked if it was a feast day yet, which he asks every morning, and I said not yet, Uncle. Eleven of them at table now, Mam, counting Papa. Two of us breathing. Brann says that is a good household and it is. It is just a great deal of linen.
Breakfast was lively eel, which you will remember. Brann caught them in the traps last night and they were still going in the bucket when I fried them, and still going on the plate, and Brann says the trick is to bite behind the head and then you are the one in charge. I ate mine standing. The children ate theirs at the table with Gran-Gran watching, and she told them they chewed like heifers, and little Wenn bit his so hard it stopped, for a moment, and everyone clapped. It started again. They always do.
The Tenancy man came at second bell. That is the collector for old Alderman Strake, who owns the house, as he has since 207. Twelve lacks for the quarter, and he looked at the stairs and said he could smell someone up there was going Ripe and we should see to it before the fly-assessor came, because the window-tax goes up if your flies are counted on the outside. It is Uncle Josse. We know. I told him we have him down for Restitching Week. He wrote that in his book. Everyone has a book, Mam. Even the flies have a book now.
I walked to Needle Street to pay the last of the restitching fee for Josse's foot and to buy wire, and on the way I passed the Lofts, so I stopped outside and listened. You can always hear it from the street. Like rain on reeds, the way it always was. But Mam, there is something else under it now. Everyone on Loftside says it. They are all saying the same thing at the same time, very soft. Aldo will not tell me what it is. He said it was not his to read. I asked him if you were saying it too and he said yes. I am not angry, I only want to know what you mean by it. If it is something you need, ask Aldo to write it down plain.
In the afternoon there was a Black Noon. I was on the plank bridge at Cooper's Cut when it came over, and it was like someone drawing a blanket over the whole of Lastgate. I put my face in my shawl and stood still at the rail like you taught me, and I could hear them in my hood and feel them on my hands, ticking, and somebody on the bridge was laughing and could not stop. When it passed the lamps had gone out up and down the canal and the lamplighters had to come with their long poles and light them all again off a coal. My ears are still full of the sound of it. Brann says you never really get the sound out.
After supper I sat with the breath-jar. We have nineteen breaths now, Mam, and four whole minutes, which is the four Papa saved before he went, and one more I put by this Carving from the eel money. That is not even a tenth part of a license yet. But it is a start, and a start is something you can hold in your hand. It is for you. I want you to know it is for you first. Then Gran-Gran, who has been asking. Then Papa, if he ever will. Then whoever is next.
I am writing this by one of Wick Tallow's candles. It is a good steady one. Brann bought it on the Lane for six crumbs. I try not to wonder whose it was. When I turn the page it leans toward me, just a little, the way your head used to when I was talking and you were trying to hear.
I will come on the feast day. I will bring the children if Brann can get off the weir. May you finish, Mam. I mean it the kind way.
Your Teal.

The core book told you what Sallowreach is. This chapter is about what it is like: the smell of a particular street at a particular bell, the price of wire, what a reed-cutter earns, who holds the key to which door, and how a woman of forty-six who is legally a child keeps eleven dead relatives clean on a weir-worker's wage. It is about the long upkeep. The great horror of the north is not that the dead walk. It is that they must be maintained, every day, forever, by people who will one day need maintaining themselves, and that the whole country has been organized for six centuries around the chores.

Read this chapter alongside the land chapter of the core book. It does not repeat the Meal, the history or the factions. It goes down to the boards.

## Districts and Settlements

Sallowreach has roughly a hundred and sixty thousand people, by the Court's own census of 637, and a hundred and forty-four thousand of them are Kept. The census counts the shelved of the Lofts and the barrels of Brinemoss but not the Sump, and it does not count the Smalls, because nobody from the Court will go down to count them. Two in every three Sallowreachers live in or under Lastgate. The rest are scattered across the fen in stilt-hamlets, weir-towns and the walled estates of the Hand-lines, connected by boardwalk, punt-channel and one road.

### Lastgate

From the border road, Lastgate looks like a forest of black posts walking into the fog with houses balanced on their shoulders. It is built entirely on pilings driven into the fen, ten thousand of them, many as old as the Glad Century, all tarred, all slowly going down. Because nothing in Sallowreach finishes, the pilings do not rot through. They soften. They lean. They sink an inch a decade into the peat, and the city sinks with them, and so for six hundred years the people of Lastgate have done what the people of a sinking city must: they build a new storey on top of the old one and move upstairs.

Lastgate is therefore counted in **storeys** rather than streets. The Boards (the present street level, the planks you walk on) are the Ninth Storey. The Eighth is the cellar level, damp, half-lit, where the poor rent rooms by the bed. The Seventh and everything below it are the Drowned Storeys, under the canal-line, inhabited by the Kept, the Smalls and those who have reasons not to be found. Addresses in Lastgate give the storey, the board and the house: *Ninth, Weir Row, the Coombe house* is an ordinary address; *Sixth, Old Weir Row, the Coombe house* is the same house three centuries ago, and there may still be a Coombe living in it.

The city is ruled by bells. The Ferrying Post rings the hours with a flat iron bell that has never sounded properly because it was cast in the year 9 and has never finished cooling. There are nine bells in the day, from first bell before dawn to ninth bell at the Plate's rising, and every Lastgater can tell the hour by the way the bell sounds through the Breath. At sixth bell, by law, every household lights its door-candle, and the whole city glows yellow in the fog like something seen through the skin of a lantern.

#### The Upper Boards

The Court's district, on the highest and driest piling-ground at the heart of the city, around the Hall of Hands and the Hall of Closings. The houses are tall, narrow, black-timbered and double-doored, built in the style the fen calls *shut*: no window on the ground storey, slit windows above, a vestibule between every pair of doors so that nothing opens directly onto anything. The Hand-line houses, Grue, Vane, Sedge, Hethe and the dozen lesser lines, keep their town residences here, and the Unvacated aldermen who own most of the rest of the city keep theirs beside them.

**Who lives here:** the Hand-lines and their cadets, the Unvacated rich (the Kept who have owned things since before 300 A.G.), the Court's clerks and Licensers, Envoy Castellan Dray in his rented rooms, and several thousand living servants, who are not permitted to sleep on the Upper Boards and must walk down to the Eighth Storey each night. **Smell and sound:** cedar, above all. Every great door has a brass brazier beside it, and the braziers are kept burning day and night by hired cedar-wardens, so that the Upper Boards smell like the inside of a new chest. Under the cedar there is myrrh and ambergris and, faintly, the sweetness that all of it is there to hide. The district is quiet. Hand-line houses absorb sound, and the Unvacated do not like to be disturbed. Visitors notice that birds do not sing here, and that the flies are fewer. **Trade:** licenses, minutes and breaths; perfumers' shops (Abelard Myrrh's main shop is on the Lower Pilings, but he keeps a scenting-room here); the best lacquerers; jewellers who specialize in signet rings that can be worn over a glove. **Custom:** *the Threshold Sniff*. A guest at a great house stops between the two doors and is smelled by a footman. Guests who are Ripe are given a cedar-soaked cloth to hold over themselves and shown to the cool room. Guests who smell of nothing are shown in at once and nobody meets their eyes. **Danger:** the Court has hands. Agents of the Right Hand walk here in plain grey coats and take notes on who visits whom. A living person found on the Upper Boards after ninth bell without a pass may be detained as a vagrant, and vagrancy is fined by the hour.

**Ysolde Crane, Cedar-Warden.** A living woman of fifty-two who keeps the braziers burning along the Court side of Hand Street, as her mother did before her. She carries a cedar-box on a strap and a long iron poker and knows every visitor to the Hall of Hands by their smell. She will tell you, for a lack, who came to the Hall of Closings last night, how Ripe they were, and whether they left smelling of nothing. Lately she has been asked to stop keeping the brazier outside the Vane house lit, because Lady Corrow says the smell of burning bothers her. Ysolde has noticed it has been a long while since the flies on that door moved.

#### Needle Street

The Seamers' quarter: a long canal-side street of a hundred narrow shops, each with a work-table in the window and a hook above it, running from the Guildhall at the north end to the wire-drawers' sheds at the south. Needle Street is the only district of Lastgate that is busy at every bell. The Seamers sleep in shifts.

**Who lives here:** Seamers of every rank, from Threaders bunking six to a loft to Masters with houses of their own; wire-drawers, lacquer-boilers, gut-spinners, cork-cutters, glaziers who make false eyes. The Kept come here as clients, carried on boards or walking, holding their parts. **Smell and sound:** hot pitch, cedar lacquer, spirits of wine, the brown smell of gut-thread soaking in vats, and from every open doorway the sweet layered stink of the clients. The sound is the street's famous rhythm: the hiss of the bellows that dry the lacquer, the click of the wire-cutters, and under it a constant murmur of the Kept being reassured, because a Seamer talks to the client the whole time, and the client usually talks back. **Trade:** restitching (three lacks a limb at Guild rates), wire-work, lacquer, false eyes, false teeth, glass noses, gauze, waxed wraps, Seamer's kits. The Guild's stock-room of spare parts, behind the Guildhall, is the most valuable warehouse in the country. **Custom:** *the first seam*. Every Threader's first real stitching is done on a family the Guild chooses, and the family is bound to ask for that Threader every spring for as long as the Threader works. Some Seamers have clients who have asked for them for a hundred and fifty years. **Danger:** the Restitching Week crush. In Grace, when every household in the city brings its grandmothers, Needle Street fills from bank to bank, people fall in the canal, and parts go missing in the press. Marrow Jack's boys work the crowd.

**Mistress Winifred Harl, Seamer.** Aubin Coyle's mistress in the Guild, living, sixty-six, a short broad woman with forearms like a ferryman's and a pipe she never takes out of her mouth except to swear. She has stitched the Coombe family for forty-one years and has seventy-eight families on her list. She knows, because she has been counting, that more parts have come into the Guild stock-room this year than came out of the Sump. She has asked Thessaly Mort about it once. She has not asked again.

#### Tanner's Boards and Wick Lane

The rendering quarter, downwind (when there is wind) on the east edge of the city, where the trades that need fire and stink are kept away from the Upper Boards. Tanner's Boards is a broad plank square ringed by tan-pits sunk in the old Seventh Storey roofs; Wick Lane runs off it to the north, crooked, lined with chandlers, soap-boilers, glue-makers and bone-turners. Wick Tallow's shop is at the far end, with its sign of a candle with a face.

**Who lives here:** tanners, renderers, soap-boilers, chandlers and their journeymen, most of them living (the work is hot, and heat is unkind to the Kept). The Heirs keep several rooms over the tanneries because the stink keeps Court agents away. Jory Welt lives here. **Smell and sound:** oak bark and lime from the tan-pits, hot fat, lye, scorched hair, and, along Wick Lane, the thick yellow smell of rendering that coats the back of the throat and stays there for a day. You hear the scraping of hides, the bubble of the vats, and in the evening the chandlers calling their candles: *Steady light, sixth bell light, light that will not leave you.* **Trade:** leather (Sallowreach leather is very fine and never perishes), soap, glue, Kept-candles, named candles, rendered fat for lamps, bone for lacks (the Rim Road Company's northern curing-shed is on the square). **Custom:** *the candle-blessing*. Families who commission a named candle from a relative carry it home in a procession and light it first at the dinner table, and the eldest Kept present says the name aloud. Then nobody speaks while it burns for the first hour, out of courtesy. **Danger:** the vats. Everything that goes into a Sallowreach vat continues, and a man who falls into one comes out half rendered and entirely aware. The quarter also has the city's only recorded Tallow-Saints.

**Saul Brindle, Tanner.** Jory Welt's landlord, a living man of fifty-eight, bald, brown-stained to the elbows, and a Pamphleteer of the Heirs in his own right. He hides presses in his drying-lofts under racks of hides and has kept the Boy's secrets for twenty years. He has a Kept father, who is Unvacated and owns the tannery, and who sits in the counting-room every day doing the books. Saul has started to watch the old man's hands and wonder how heavy he would be.

#### Weir Row

The eel-fishers' quarter on the north canals, where Lastgate meets the open mere. The houses here are low and wide and built over the water on their own short pilings, each with a trap-door in the kitchen floor through which the family drops its eel-traps straight into the fen. The whole district is a lattice of weirs, nets and wicker traps, and on still nights you can hear the traps working: a soft wet knocking, thousands of times over.

**Who lives here:** eel-catchers, reed-cutters, net-menders, punt-makers, their Kept elders by the dozen per house. This is the most crowded district of the dead in the city, because fishermen drown, and drowned men come home. **Smell and sound:** fen-water, eel-slime, smoke from the eel-smokeries, vinegar, and wet wool. Children's voices (Weir Row has more living children than anywhere in the city), the knock of the traps, the chant of the housekeeping song, and the long wet coughing of the newly drowned coming up the ladders in the morning. **Trade:** eel (lively, smoked, pickled), reeds, wicker, rope, punts, the cheap *sour* beer of the north. **Custom:** *the ladder-light*. Every Weir Row house keeps a lamp burning on its water-ladder all night, because sooner or later everyone in the family goes into the fen, and it is good for them to see the way home when they come up. **Danger:** the fen holds on. Weir Row children are taught to keep their feet on the ladder and never sit with their legs in the water, because things that drowned long ago sometimes take an ankle and will not let go for hours.

**Teal Mallory, Housekeeper.** Living, forty-six, legally a minor, keeper of eleven Kept relatives and two living children on a weir-worker's wage (see the letter that opens this chapter). She is a descendant of Cobb Mallory, one of the parties to the Long Argument, and visits him in the Room every feast day with a pie he cannot eat. She is ordinary, tired and kind, and she is exactly the person the PCs should meet first in Lastgate.

#### The Sinks

The low, sagging southwest of the city, where the pilings have gone down fastest and the Ninth Storey is only a few feet above the canal-line. The Sinks are crooked, over-built and dark: alleys between houses lean so close that their upper storeys touch, and some lanes are entirely roofed by other people's floors. The poor live here, the living poor and the Kept poor together, and the Heirs, and Marrow Jack's markets, and the Room of the Long Argument.

**Who lives here:** day-laborers, dredgers, sackmen, back-alley seamers, part-sellers, fences, Heirs in their safehouses, Finishers between pilgrimages, Pim Sorrel and her mother, and a great many Kept whose families cannot afford to keep them and who have put them out. **Smell and sound:** the Sinks smell of themselves. Peat smoke when there is peat. Underneath, the honest stink of the poor Kept, unperfumed and unlacquered, and the drains. The sound is of too many people in too little space, and of the Kept who have been put out and sit in doorways asking passers-by for a stitch. **Trade:** everything illicit: Kept limbs (Nan Pickering's drying racks are under the Eighth Storey here), stilled meat, back-alley seaming at half Guild rates, Bait pamphlets, fen-gin, minutes of Closing of uncertain provenance. **Custom:** *the doorway coin*. A passer-by who has a crumb to spare drops it into the lap of a put-out Kept, who cannot use it, and who passes it to the first living child who comes by. It is said to be lucky for all three. **Danger:** part-theft. A Kept person who sits still too long in the Sinks wakes without a hand. A living person who looks too long at the wrong door is invited to an Heirs' meeting and does not get to say no.

**Lark Dunnock, Printer.** A living woman of forty-four, an Heir of the Pamphleteer rank, who runs the press for the Heirs' broadsheet, *The Bequest*, from a cellar under the Room of the Long Argument. She is small, ink-black to the wrists, and quick, and she has printed every Hush map Jory Welt has made. Her own father is in the Jar Room, third shelf, sentenced in 612 for striking a Licenser. She prints his name in the corner of every issue.

#### The Landing

The southern waterfront, where the border road comes off its causeway onto the first pilings and the city begins. The Ferrying Post stands here, the oldest piling in Lastgate, black as iron with nine centuries of tar. Around it are the Rim Road Company's warehouses, the customs house, the punt-stands, the inns for foreigners, and Tam Ruddock's ferry.

**Who lives here:** porters, punt-men, Company clerks, customs officers of the Court, innkeepers, foreigners (Fatlands meat-factors, Vigil couriers, Oathen notaries, the occasional Reckoner who has not yet understood), and the smugglers who move the dying north. **Smell and sound:** tar first, from the Post and the hulls; then rum, Fatlands gravy from the foreigners' inns, horse-dung from the Company stables, and the vinegar the customs men splash on everything that comes off the road. You hear foreign accents here and nowhere else in the city, and the Company's post-horn at the arrival of every coach. **Trade:** imports (Fatlands meat, Brinehollow salt, Vigil glass, Oathen paper), exports (Sallowreach leather, smoulder-coal, Kept-candles, undying cheese), money-changing (the only place in the north where a foreigner can buy breaths at a fair rate). **Custom:** *the Turning Back*. Every Kept porter and punt-man on the Landing wears a red cord on the left wrist. It reminds them, and tells others, that they must not take the road south. Living porters tie the cord on for them at hiring, and if a Kept porter is ever seen walking south past the last piling, any living person who sees them is expected to take them by the cord and turn them round. **Danger:** foreigners. People from the other six lands die here. A Brinehollow sailor knifed in a Landing inn dies properly, and then gets up, and cannot go home, and the Landing has a whole street, Stranger's Row, of foreign Kept who made that mistake.

**Gideon Tarr, Tarrer of the Ferrying Post.** A Kept man who has kept the Ferrying Post tarred, at Court wages, since 338. He is tall and dark with old tar, every seam of him sealed with it, so that he looks like a figure carved from the Post itself and smells exactly like it. Twice a year, sometimes more, he finds the eighth figure on the Post freshly gouged, the cut pale and clean, and tars it over again. He has stopped asking who keeps opening it. He has not stopped noticing that the cuts are now coming every month, and that the newest ones are from the inside of the wood outward, as if something were trying to get out rather than someone trying to get in.

#### Loftside

The north edge of the city, around the nine long halls of the Lofts. The streets here are quiet and clean and swept, because the Loft-Wardens insist on it, and because the families who come to visit the shelved want the walk to be decent. Loftside is a district of linen-sellers, camphor-merchants, wrappers, label-writers, readers-for-hire, and small cheap lodgings where Wardens and their families live.

**Who lives here:** the Loft-Wardens and their families; wrappers (the craftsmen who wind the Set-Aside in linen); readers who hire out to families who cannot read; widowers of the shelved who rent rooms near their spouses; and the very poor living, who come because rents are lower near the whispering. **Smell and sound:** camphor, linen, wax, lamp-oil, and old meat. The sound is the whispering, always: a soft continuous rush like rain on reeds, rising at night, coming through the walls of the halls into every house on Loftside. Locals say they stop hearing it in a month. Visitors never stop. **Trade:** linen and waxed wrap, camphor, labels, the Setting Aside (40 lacks to wrap and shelve, 1 lack a season for upkeep), reading-aloud by the hour. **Custom:** *the feast-day queue*. On every feast day the families of the shelved line up outside the halls before first bell with letters, books and small gifts the shelved cannot use, and the line runs the length of Loftside. Those who come to the head of the queue and find they cannot remember which shelf they want are taken in by a Warden and helped to look, without comment. **Danger:** the whisper. Anyone who lodges on Loftside long enough begins to hear the shelved as words rather than sound, and lately there is one word only. Three lodgers on Loft Lane this Carving have stopped speaking anything else.

**Aldo Penny, Reader of Hall Six.** Living, thirty-one, thin, near-sighted, with a soft trained voice and ink on his cuffs. He has read to the four hundred shelved of Hall Six every night for nine years and knows most of them by voice. He refuses to tell families what their shelved are whispering now, on Old Bettany's order, and he has begun sleeping badly, waking at the same hour every night with the word in his mouth.

#### The Drowned Storeys

Beneath all of it. The Drowned Storeys are the old streets of Lastgate that sank below the canal-line, storey by storey, over six centuries: the Seventh at knee-depth, the Sixth at the waist, the Fifth to the neck, and everything below the Fifth fully underwater, green-lit through the gratings of the streets above, inhabited by those who do not need to breathe. Access is by ladder, by stair, by trap-door, by the old chimneys, and by swimming.

**Who lives here:** the Kept who cannot afford the Boards or do not want to be seen; the Smalls, the child-dead, in their own society under Little Nell Ash; old Kept families who never moved up when their storey sank and still sit in their parlors with the canal at their chins; smugglers, part-thieves, and anything the Court has lost interest in. **Smell and sound:** canal-water, green weed, wet plaster, the drains of the city above, and the cold mineral smell of deep stone. The sound is muffled and close: dripping, the slap of water against walls, voices that come from the wrong direction, and, in the Smalls' quarter under the old schoolroom, the sound of children playing a game whose rules no living person has been told. **Trade:** almost none, in money. The Drowned Storeys run on favors, on found objects, and on what falls through the gratings. The Smalls trade in buttons, keys and spoons. **Custom:** *the grating gift*. Living people who live on the Boards above a grating drop something through it on feast days, a toy, a ribbon, a sweet, for the Smalls. Nobody admits doing it. Everybody does. **Danger:** the cold, the dark, the water, the old families who do not like visitors, and the Smalls, who are children, with children's ideas of fairness, and who have had two hundred years to perfect their games.

**Ossie Wade, the Lamp-Wader.** A Kept lamplighter, drowned at the age of nineteen in 498 while lighting the gratings, who has gone on doing it ever since from below. He wades the Sixth and Fifth Storeys with a pole and a tin of smoulder-coal, lighting the fen-glow jars the Court hangs on the old street corners. He is grey-green, soft as a soaked loaf, gentle and very slow, and he is the only adult the Smalls allow into their quarter, because he brings light and never asks them anything.

[box: Getting About Lastgate]
Lastgate has no carts: the boards will not bear them. Goods move by punt, by porter and by hand-barrow. A punt-taxi across the city costs 2 crumbs per board crossed and is faster than walking. Porters (living) charge 1 lack a day; Kept porters charge 6 crumbs, because they do not eat, and are slower. Every district has a ladder-down to the Eighth Storey, and finding a way into the Drowned Storeys is a Hard (14) Eye + Search roll for strangers and Routine for anyone who grew up in the Sinks. A living character in the Fifth Storey or lower needs a breathing-reed or a Lung + Athletics roll each minute below the water.

### Beyond Lastgate

The rest of Sallowreach is fen: miles of reed and black water broken by peat-islands, alder carr, stilt-hamlets and the walled mounds of the Hand-line estates. Every settlement off the border road stands on something: piles, a peat-bank, an old mound, the back of an older village. Most can be reached only by punt or boardwalk.

#### Brinemoss

A hamlet of thirty stilt-huts in the south fen, six hours' punt from Lastgate, built around a broad wooden platform where the pickling is done. Brinemoss is famous for two things: its pickles, which are the best in the north, and Mother Gall's cellar, which holds four hundred customers in barrels. **Who lives here:** picklers, coopers, salt-boilers, and Mother Gall's extended family, living and Kept, all of whom work for her. **Smell and sound:** dill, garlic, vinegar, brine, cooper's shavings, and from under the great hut the creak and knock of four hundred barrels, very soft, steady, like a ship's timbers at anchor. **Trade:** pickles (eel, egg, onion, mushroom, and *Brinemoss pig*, a pickled lively pork that the brine finally stills), barrels, salt, and the cellar, at Mother Gall's prices. **Custom:** *knocking back*. Visitors to the cellar are taught to answer any knock from a barrel with the same number of knocks on the lid. It is the only conversation the customers can have. Some visitors stay for hours. **Danger:** the brine-pits outside the village, where the failed batches are poured. Things that went in them halfway preserved still move there. **Notable:** **Dill Gall**, Mother Gall's living grandson, twenty-six, a cooper with hands like paddles and a slow, sweet manner, who makes and seals every barrel in the cellar and who has begun, without telling his grandmother, to take visitors' money to lift lids at night.

#### The Finishing Post

Nine miles south of Lastgate on the border road, where the tarred stakes mark the edge of the land. On the Sallowreach side of the line stands the **Last Lamp**, the Rim Road Company's border inn, a long low building of stone (the only stone building in the north, carried in by cart) with a palisade and a toll-gate. #### Dunmere Verge

The great encampment around the edge of Dunmere Hush, a ring of tents, lean-tos, punts and pilgrim-shacks strung along the grey line for two miles of its circumference, and moving outward a few yards each month as the silence grows. **Who lives here:** Finishers waiting to walk; Lamps and Singers of the cult who lead them; the families of those who walked, who cannot bring themselves to go home; Court guards (Kept, posted at the main approach, some of whom have walked in themselves); dredgers who wade the edge for dropped valuables; and Dunstan Reave, at his usual spot, with his spyglass. **Smell and sound:** beeswax and fen-mint from the Finishers; wet canvas; cook-smoke; and, at the line itself, nothing, because the Hush has no smell. #### Gruesholt

The Grue estate, a day's punt west of Lastgate beyond the Sump: a long black hall on a peat mound with a reed-cutters' village of sixty houses clustered under it. #### Sedge Staithe

A weir-town on the north mere, a day's punt from Lastgate, under the protection of the Sedge family's estate at Sedgemere Hall. Sedge Staithe is built along a chain of eel-weirs that cross the mere's outflow, and its people have fished those weirs since before the Gorging. #### Smokeholm

A peat-cutters' village on the western rim of the Smoking Ground, two hours by boardwalk east of Lastgate, built on the one hard ridge in that warm, cracked, steaming country. **Who lives here:** peat-cutters, coal-tinners, and the burned: Kept men and women who were caught in fires over the centuries and walk now as figures of black coal, crackling, weeping clear fluid from their cracks. They are the village's best workers, because they do not mind the heat. **Smell and sound:** peat smoke, always, hot and sweet and choking; scorched earth; sulphur from the cracks; and the coal-folk's sound, which is the crackling of embers when they move. **Trade:** peat, and above all *smoulder-coal*: lumps of burning peat sealed in tins, which stay lit for years and are sold in every household in the north to light the door-candle and the smudge pot (1 lack the tin). **Custom:** *lending a light*. No Smokeholm household will refuse to light another's coal, and to refuse a light to anyone in the village is the worst insult there is. **Danger:** the ground. The peat fires under it shift, and a cutter who steps on the wrong turf goes through into a burning hollow, and comes out, eventually, as one of the coal-folk. **Notable:** **Cinder Annet**, Kept since 401, who fell through the turf at eighteen and has been a coal-woman ever since. She is the best tinner in Smokeholm, and the Heirs' contact there, and she guides their meetings onto the Smoking Ground by the paths that will hold. She glows faintly in the dark. She has asked three Hand-line visitors to close her, and been refused three times, and she has stopped asking and started saving minutes.

#### Grinholm

The far north fen, three days by punt from Lastgate, where the Scraplings live: the families who took what nobody else wanted at the Meal, and above all, the face. Grinholm is a scatter of turf huts on a low island ringed by alder, built around a peat pit fenced with stakes. The pit is where the face was boiled, for a week, in the year 0. Nothing grows in it. ## Roads and Ways

Sallowreach has one road and a thousand ways. The road is the border causeway, nine miles of piled peat and old bone from the Finishing Post to the Landing, wide enough for two Company coaches to pass, paved with planks the Court relays every ten years. Everything else is water. A Sallowreacher does not ask how far a place is; they ask how many poles.

**Punts** are the carts of the north: long, flat, black-tarred boats poled from the stern, carrying a family, a ton of peat or a Kept grandmother on her board. Every household that can afford one owns one. Punt-men for hire charge 2 lacks a day and know the channels; strangers who pole themselves get lost within the hour. **Boardwalks**, *the planks*, run between the larger villages on posts sunk into the fen: single planks, often rotten, never finished, swaying in the Breath, with a hand-rope where the parish could afford one. **Wading-ways** are marked routes across shallow fen, staked with alder poles topped with white rags, used by reed-cutters and by those who cannot pay a punt. And under everything, for those who do not breathe, there is the bottom: the Kept walk the bed of the fen as freely as the living walk a road, slowly, in the dark, and the Court's Fen Guard moves its soldiers that way when it wants to arrive unseen.

## Bread and Coin

The economy of Sallowreach is built on one fact and one fear. The fact is that nobody inherits. The fear is the bill for upkeep. Every household in the north is carrying a load of Kept dependents who do not eat but must be stitched, lacquered, perfumed, wrapped, housed and kept from the flies, and who own the house, and whose permission is needed to spend anything. The living work. The dead own. The Guild charges both.

**The great trades** are Keeping (the Seamers' Guild, the wrappers, the perfumers, the lacquer-boilers, the gauze-weavers), eel and reed (the fen's food and thatch), peat and smoulder-coal, leather (which never perishes and is prized everywhere on the Rim), Kept-candles, and the Court's own trade in death. Sallowreach imports meat, grain, salt, glass, paper and cedar, which it cannot grow and burns by the ton. It exports leather, coal, candles, smoked eel, undying cheese, and, increasingly, quietly, bottled Hush-air for buyers in other lands who will not say what they want it for.

**The Kept work for less.** The Kept do not eat and need no bed, and so the law since 214 has permitted them to be paid *dead wages*: half the living rate, often less. This has kept wages in the north the lowest on the Table, and kept the living poor poorer, because no employer hires a living porter at a lack a day when a Kept one will carry the same load for six crumbs. The Heirs' pamphlets call it the second inheritance: the dead took the houses, and then they took the work.

**What things cost locally.** Lively eel is 4 crumbs the pound; stilled meat (legally closed) is 3 lacks the pound, and Fatlands imported beef 2. Reed-bread is a crumb a loaf. A tin of smoulder-coal is 1 lack; a Wick Tallow candle 6 crumbs; a named candle 5 lacks, or 50 lacks to commission the rendering of your own dead. Cedar oil is 3 lacks a vial on the Landing and 5 on the Upper Boards. Restitching is 3 lacks a limb at Guild rates and a lack and a half in the Sinks. A shelf in the Lofts is 40 lacks to wrap and shelve. A barrel at Mother Gall's is 600 lacks paid in advance, and she will not say for how long. A death-license is about 4,000 lacks, a minute 65, a breath 5, and the price of a license rose by three hundred lacks in the last year, because the Hushes have made death seem, for the first time, a thing that might be had for free.

**Taxes.** The Court levies four, and the Unvacated a fifth. The **fly-tax** is assessed per window, by Court fly-assessors who count the flies on the outside of the glass each Plenty; a house with many flies is presumed to be keeping its dead badly and pays more. The **stitching-tithe** is a tenth of every Seamer's fee, paid by the Guild in a lump each Carving after a negotiation that is never friendly. The **door-duty** is a crumb a night for every door-candle lit at sixth bell, collected by the parish. The **license-levy** is a tenth of every Closing fee, which is to say the Court taxes itself, and keeps both halves. And the **Tenancy**, which is not a tax but behaves like one, is the rent that every living person pays to the Unvacated owner of the house they live in, as their parents paid, and their grandparents.

**Who is rich.** The Unvacated, who own nearly every building in Lastgate and draw rents they have no way to spend except on lacquer, perfume, wire and minutes. The Hand-lines, who own death. The Seamers' Guild, which owns the means of keeping. Abelard Myrrh, who owns the smell. And a handful of the living who have found a trade the dead cannot do: Tam Ruddock, Mother Gall, Wick Tallow, and, this year, Bram Hollin, who has more bookings than days.

[box: Minutes in the Floor]
Almost every family in the north has a breath-jar: a stoppered pot under a floorboard, holding the minutes and breaths saved towards a Closing. It is the only form of inheritance Sallowreach permits, because the jar belongs to whoever put the coins in, and the coins pass when they are spent. Breath-jars are a common target of burglars, Heirs and in-laws. A PC who needs money in a hurry in Sallowreach can always find a family willing to break its jar for a favor, and will always find out later what the favor cost: whoever the jar was for will not now be finished for another generation, and they know who to blame.

## Law and the Knife

Law in Sallowreach is very old, very precise and absolutely without end. Every statute ever passed is still in force, because nothing is repealed: a repeal is an ending. Statutes that contradict one another are reconciled by the courts, which have been reconciling them for five centuries. No case is ever closed, because a judgment is a kind of ending too, and so every judgment of a Sallowreach court is, formally, *adjourned with a ruling*, and may be resumed by either party at any time. Most are not. Some are resumed every few decades, by the original parties, out of spite.

**Who enforces.** The **Fen Guard** under Captain Dace Morrow keeps the border, guards the larger Hushes and puts down riots; its soldiers are almost all Kept and cannot be killed, which makes riot work easy and morale terrible. The **Hookmen** are the Court's beadles in Lastgate: living and Kept officers in grey coats carrying Loft-Warden's shelving hooks and sacks, who make arrests by hooking a suspect by the collar or the gauze and bagging their head. The **Right Hand's agents**, plain-coated, unnamed, watch the Upper Boards and the Heirs. The **parish wardens** collect fines for falling apart in public, and carry a bag for what they collect.

**How a trial works.** Small matters, debts, falling-apart fines, nuisance and *grievous unmaking* (what other lands call assault and murder), go before the **Court of Wrongs**, which sits daily in the old Customs Hall on the Landing under three Justices, of whom Justice Ever Lathe is the most senior. The plaintiff speaks, the defendant speaks, and the victim speaks, because the victim is always present, however badly they were unmade. There is no jury. Witnesses swear on the dead: the right hand placed in the mouth of a Kept parent. Witnesses who have no Kept parent swear on the jaw of the court's own oath-keeper, a Kept woman named Mother Clamp who has sat in the witness-box since 402 and is said to have bitten through nine perjurers' fingers. Judgment is given, adjourned, and recorded. Capital matters, unlicensed Closing, stilling, part-theft on the great scale, treason and Hush-dragging, go to the **Hall of Hands**, where the Regent and any three Hands of the Court sit, the accused is not permitted to speak except through an advocate of the Court's choosing, and the sentence is almost always the jar.

The sentenced person is taken to Needle Street, where a Master Seamer of the Guild, under protest and at Court rates, takes them apart: the head is sealed in green glass with lead, the hands in a smaller jar beside it, and the rest is rendered (at Wick Tallow's) or sunk. ## The Faithful

Sallowreach is a land of the Theft. Most people in the north believe in their bones that their ancestors murdered a kind god on a mudbank, and that what they live in now is the bill. The Confessors of the north are the gravest priests on the Table, and their chapels are low, windowless timber houses with a single shut door painted grey, called **Shut Houses**. Inside there is a long bench, a grey cloth on the wall in the shape of two open hands, and nothing else. Worship is sitting in silence on the bench and not leaving. The service ends when the last worshipper decides to go, which in some Shut Houses has not happened in years.

The **Gift** is the faith of the comfortable: Unvacated aldermen, the better Seamers, the families of the Upper Boards who are not Hand-line. Its Thankers in the north preach that Ossel lay down on the shore out of love, to end the famine, and that its refusal to finish now is its love continuing, a kindness gone on too long, as a mother's hand stays on a sleeping child's head after the child is asleep. Gift households keep a little dish of grey salt on the windowsill as a sign. The Gift is popular with the Court, which finds it convenient.

The **Bait** is illegal in Sallowreach and has been since 331, and its pamphlets are the most widely read literature in the Sinks. The northern Baiters teach that Ossel lay down to be eaten as a seed lies down in the ground, and that the Hand-lines are not its rulers but its fruit, and that the Hushes are the harvest. It is the only doctrine whose preachers can stand at the edge of a Hush and point to it as proof. The Court jars their tongues. The tongues go on preaching, in the Jar Room, silently, to each other.

And then there are the **Finishers**, who are a faith in their own right and growing faster than all three, and whose hymns are the only songs in Sallowreach with last verses.

**Shrines.** Every house has a *shut-niche*, a small cupboard with a door, in the wall of the kitchen, holding the household's oath-relics: a lock of hair, a tooth, a ring from each of the Kept. The door is opened only to swear. Every street corner in the old parts of Lastgate has an *eye-post*, a short black post carved at the top with two closed eyes, where people touch their fingers to the lids on the way to a Closing, a sickbed or a quarrel. The Ferrying Post is the greatest shrine of all, and nobody worships at it.

**Holy days.** **Restitching Week** (the first week of Grace), when every household brings its Kept to be mended, the Seamers work without stopping and the churches hold a mending-mass, the only joyful service of the year. **The Shutter Days** (the hottest fortnight of Plenty), when the flies breed and the Kept are shut indoors for their own sakes, and the living keep vigil at their doors with smudge-pots. **Yellowing** (high Carving), when leaves that have hung on the trees for decades yellow a little further and the children gather the ones that finally fall, very rarely, and keep them in boxes as great luck. **Tablenight** (the longest night of Lack), the banquet for the dead. And **the First Morning**, the day after Tablenight, when the hanged thief of the year 0 is remembered by every household hanging a knotted cord on the door and, at first bell, cutting it, and saying *come down, then*.

## How Power Moves

On paper, the Regent rules through the Mortuary Court, and the Court rules everything. In practice power in Sallowreach moves along three cords that are always pulling against one another: **who can end things** (the Hand-lines, and now the Hushes), **who can keep things** (the Seamers, Mother Gall, the Wardens), and **who owns things** (the Unvacated). The living have none of the three, which is why the Heirs exist. The Court's whole art, for three centuries, has been to keep the first cord in its own hand and to tie the other two to it with debt.

That art is failing. The Hushes end things without license. The Guild knows it could stop the country and has begun to wonder whether it should. The Lofts hear everything and have begun to whisper only one word. And the Unvacated, who were supposed to be the Court's oldest allies, have begun to buy minutes in secret against the day the Court will not sell them any more.

#### Who They Are

The Regent of Sallowreach sits in a high-backed chair of black fen-oak at the head of the Hall of Hands, and almost never leaves it. He is small, smaller than his portraits, because the centuries have settled him downward the way they settle every Kept body: the spine has compressed, the shoulders have come forward, and the head, held up by a collar of brass wire hidden under a stock of black silk, sits a little too far in front of the rest of him. His face has been lacquered so often, by so many Masters of the Guild, that it is no longer skin but a deep glossy amber shell, like a violin, through which, in strong lamplight, you can see the dark shapes of what is underneath. The eyes are glass, very good glass, blue, made in the Vigil in 455; his own went in the 380s. The jaw is wired shut. He speaks through a brass tube let into the front of his throat below the larynx, fitted by Thessaly Mort in 470, and the voice that comes out of it is dry, small, metallic and very precise, a clicking whisper that makes clerks lean in, so that everyone who speaks to the Regent ends up bowing.

He smells of cedar first, then varnish, then, faintly, unmistakably, of something sweet: a bowl of fruit forgotten in a warm room. Under the lacquer, his hands are grey to the second knuckle, the nails gone thick and dark as horn. He keeps them in fine black gloves. When he moves them, which is rarely, they move one finger at a time, and there is a small sound inside each joint like a seed rattling in a dry pod.

Those who meet him come away describing not a monster but a schoolmaster: courteous, patient, faintly bored, with an infinite memory for precedent and no interest at all in anything that has not happened before.

#### Their Story

Ansel Grue was born in 229 to the left-hand line of Hessa Grue, in the Gruesholt hall that his family still owns. He was a clever, cold child who learned to read from the Court's ledgers and closed every door in the house by the age of four. At seventy-two, in 301, he was a junior Hand of the Court, and it was Ansel Grue who drafted the fee-schedule of the Licensing: the first price of death, the rule of consent, the threefold question, the license number. Every license since bears the form of words he wrote that winter.

In 340 the Regent of the day, Lorimer Vane, was charged with treason for proposing that the Court give Closings free to the Set-Aside. The charge was drafted by Grue. Lorimer Vane was officially recorded as having "retired to Glovehall, not receiving," and Grue was confirmed as Regent in 341, at the age of a hundred and twelve, living, withered, and already the most feared clerk in the country. Nobody has seen Lorimer Vane since.

In 371, aged a hundred and forty-two, Grue was thrown from a Kept horse on the causeway when it shed a leg under him, and broke his neck and jaw on the planks. He was back in his chair in nine days, stitched, wired, still bleeding a little brown at the collar, and he heard four petitions that afternoon. He has been in the chair ever since. He has outlived (in the only sense that word has here) eleven Masters of the Guild, nine Captains of the Fen Guard and every one of his enemies, all of whom are still around.

In the Carving of 610, alone, at night, he sent for Lady Corrow Vane, then thirty and newly at the Course, and asked her to close him. She took off her glove and laid her hand over his eyes. Nothing happened. He thanked her, and she put her glove back on, and neither of them has spoken of it since.

#### Their Place in the Land

Grue is the keystone. Every death in Sallowreach passes under his hand, because he signs every license personally, in a slow, beautiful, copperplate signature that takes a full minute and cannot be forged, because the ink is mixed with lacquer scraped from his own face. Every minute and breath in circulation bears his name. He presides over the Hall of Hands on every capital case, confirms every Closer's appointment, and receives the Unvacated aldermen, who confirm him in turn every ten years in a ceremony that has never yet been contested.

His day does not vary. At first bell his clerks carry in the night's petitions. At second bell he hears them, in order of filing, which for some petitioners means waiting thirty years. At fifth bell he signs licenses: never more than forty a day, because that is the rule he made in 301. At ninth bell the shutters of the Hall are closed, every one, and he sits on in the dark, alone, with the License Ledger open on his knees, counting.

His power has limits, and he knows each one exactly. He cannot strike the Seamers, who keep his face on. He cannot overrule the Unvacated, who own the buildings the Court meets in. He cannot command the Hushes. And he cannot leave the country, because if he crossed the border his four hundred and twelve years would arrive all at once.

#### What They Carry

- **The brass speaking-tube** — set into his throat in 470, polished daily by his clerk, Anselm Rook. When it is stoppered, he cannot speak. Petitioners who have angered him have watched the clerk reach for the stopper.
- **The Regent's pen** — a black iron nib in a shaft of heron bone, the heron being Ossel's bird. The ink is lamp-black mixed with lacquer from his own face, scraped by the Guild each spring and kept in a sealed pot.
- **The License Ledger** — the current volume (the four hundred and eighteenth) of the record of every license, bound in black calf. He never lets it out of his sight. A license not entered here is void, which makes it the most valuable book in the north.
- **The first minute** — the first minute of Closing ever cut, number 1/1, issued in 301 and never spent. He keeps it on a chain around his neck under the stock. Worth sixty-five lacks; worth more than any jewel in Lastgate as a relic.
- **A jaw-key** — a small brass key that opens the wire of his jaw, held by his clerk. The jaw has been opened four times since 371. Each time he said one thing, aloud, with his own mouth, and nobody who heard any of the four has repeated them.
- **A lead box** — a small lead-lined casket on the arm of his chair, which nobody has seen opened. It holds the note Lady Corrow Vane wrote him the morning after 610: *I did not hold back. C.V.* He does not believe it.
- **Cedar oil** — in a vial, which his clerk uses every hour to wipe the lacquer. It does not hide the sweetness anymore.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

Grue wants order: precedent, process, ledgers that balance and doors that are shut. He fears the Hushes more than anything in the six lands, because they close without license, and an ending without a signature is the one thing his entire life has been built to prevent. He is tempted by Envoy Castellan Dray, not by the Vigil's oil but by the thought of a second country run on his forms.

**Secret the GM can reveal:** he asked Corrow Vane to close him in 610, and her hand did nothing. He does not know whether he is beyond closing or whether she held back, and he has spent thirty-one years watching her for the answer. **A second secret:** Lorimer Vane did not retire. Grue had him sacked and sunk in the Sump in 340, alive and talking, and the dredgers have found Sack Seventeen. If its occupant is Lorimer, every license Grue has ever signed was signed by a Regent who took office by an unlawful sinking, and is void.

**Secret he does not know:** Gruesholt has no Hush because the Hush is in him. Corrow's hand worked. It found the ending in him and closed it, the way a Course-hand closes a candle so it can never be lit again. Grue cannot be finished now, by any hand or any Hush. When the great Hush comes, everything in Sallowreach will end except the Regent, who will sit in his chair in the silence, signing. (The GM may choose instead that Corrow did hold back, or that a Brink-hand like Osric's might still succeed.)

#### Ties

- **Lady Corrow Vane** — his Left Hand and the only person alive who has touched his face. He trusts her completely with every death but his own.
- **Osric Vane** — a weapon he has declined to use and a Brink he has declined to close. He receives a weekly report on the lead mittens.
- **Thessaly Mort** — the only person in the country who can unmake him by withholding a stitch. They have dined together, he not eating, every Restitching Week since 451.
- **Jory Welt** — a petitioner whose case has been adjourned for forty years. Grue knows exactly where the Boy lives and lets him live there.
- **Provost Carrion Sedge** — the man whose ledgers could end the Court. Grue has offered to buy them three times.
- **Envoy Castellan Dray** — a guest he is keeping waiting, as he keeps everyone waiting. He has begun to enjoy the visits.
- **Justice Ever Lathe** — appointed by Grue in 402. Grue reads every one of the Justice's rulings and has never overturned one, and does not know whom he is agreeing with.

#### In Play

PCs meet the Regent when they petition the Court for a license, a pardon or a ruling; when they become a threat to the order of the country; or when they bring him something he cannot ignore, such as Sack Seventeen, Sedge's ledgers or Pim Sorrel. He wants from them what he wants from everyone: that they file properly and wait. He will hire them, through intermediaries, to retrieve documents, silence witnesses or find out where the next Hush will open.

- **The Fortieth Signature.** The Regent has signed thirty-nine licenses today and will not sign the fortieth, because the fortieth is for a Hush-dragged alderman whose family wants it backdated. The family hires the PCs to find out what would make him sign.
- **Sack Seventeen.** The PCs have the sack. Grue's clerk offers them a full license each for it, unopened. The Heirs offer them a printing press and a riot. The sack offers them nothing; it only asks, in a voice like a drowned bell, to see the Regent.
- **The Jaw-Key.** Anselm Rook, the clerk, has been murdered (stitched, but mute) and the jaw-key is missing. Somebody wants to hear what the Regent says with his own mouth.

**Threat:** he files, adjourns, licenses and outlasts; he sends Hookmen, then the Right Hand's agents, then the jar. **Kindness:** he does not understand it, and treats it as a filing error. **The god:** he will not speak of it, and the Want to Close is visible in him only as the way he lowers the lid of the inkwell, very carefully, after every signature. **If nobody intervenes:** he sells Dray the forms of Keeping (not the secret, which is not his to sell), sinks Sack Seventeen deeper, and sits on in the Hall of Hands as the Hushes join around Lastgate, signing licenses for a country that no longer needs them.

#### Who They Are

Corrow Vane is tall, very straight, and very still, and her stillness is the first thing that frightens people, before they notice the smell, or rather its absence. She smells of nothing at all. In a country where everyone carries cedar or vinegar or peat-smoke or rot, she carries nothing, and the air around her is clean as cold water, and people who have stood beside her in a crowded room describe it as standing beside an open grave that has been scrubbed.

She is sixty-one. Her ash-blonde hair, gone silver at the temples, is worn in a single severe coil pinned with black pins. Her face rarely moves; she learned young that expressions frighten clients. Her voice is low and exquisitely courteous, and she uses very few words. Her gloves are black kid, cut long, sewn on at the wrist with nine stitches of black silk each, and under them her hands are slate grey to a hand's breadth above the wrist, cold enough to sting through the leather, the fingers each with two joints too many, so that at rest under the glove they curl slightly the wrong way. She keeps them folded at her waist, left over right, and never, ever gestures.

#### Their Story

Corrow was born at Glovehall in 580, the elder child of Lord Hamnet Vane, a Closer, and was raised as the eldest Vane daughters have been raised since the Meal: to be the hand of the Court. She could not sleep with a door open at three. At fourteen she wore mittens. At twenty-two, in 602, her fingers went grey, and her father took her to the Hall of Closings and stood her in the corner of Chamber One to watch him work.

She performed her first licensed Closing at twenty-three, on a Kept fisherman of the Glad Century who had asked for it for two hundred years. She has performed eleven thousand since: a little over two hundred and sixty a year, four or five each working day. She keeps count. She was named Left Hand in 618, the youngest in three centuries.

She married in 599, by arrangement, Lord Merriman Vane, a gentle Sedge cadet who took her name. She bore two sons, Aldous in 601 and Teodor in 605, and handed each to a nurse at the moment of birth, and has never held either. The boys were taught to keep three paces from her. Aldous, now forty and a Licenser of the Court, drafts the licenses his mother executes, and has never touched her skin. Merriman drowned in the Glovehall moat in 622, under circumstances nobody discusses, and is Kept in the south wing. She has offered, every year, to close him. He has refused every year. He says he would like to stay near the boys.

#### Their Place in the Land

The Left Hand commands the nine Closers of the Court, assigns them their licenses, and performs the most important Closings herself: Unvacated aldermen, Court officers, rewards granted by the Regent. Her waiting list is years long; her fee, on top of the license, is a further three hundred lacks, which goes to the Court. She works in the Hall of Closings from third bell to sixth, five days in seven, and spends the rest at Glovehall, measuring the garden. Her Glovebearer, **Prudence Lisk**, a living clerk of twenty-nine, unpicks the nine stitches before each Closing with a silver needle and sews them back after; the two women have worked side by side for six years and have never exchanged a word not about the work.

She is the Court's sharpest blade and its most reliable one, and she is used, too, for deniable work: a Kept rebel closed in a back room, an inconvenient witness finished in the Jar Room without trial. She does it. She does not ask. Her limits are the license and the consent, which she enforces on every Closer but herself, and Glovehall, which is hers in law because her father is shelved in the Lofts and has signed it over.

#### What They Carry

- **Sewn gloves** — black kid, nine stitches of black silk at each wrist, replaced every month. She owns forty pairs. She has never in her adult life put one on herself.
- **A stiletto** — a slim Oathen blade in her left sleeve, for the living who might need to be persuaded to stand still. She has drawn it twice.
- **The Count-Book** — a small black notebook in which she has written the name of every person she has closed, eleven thousand and some, in a tiny clear hand, with the date. She reads a page every night. She does not know why.
- **A perfect pear** — from the orchard Hush behind Glovehall, picked with tongs by a gardener who would not step over the line. It sits on her desk in the Hall of Closings, a year old, golden, unblemished, and smells of nothing.
- **A knotted cord** — sixty yards of waxed linen knotted every yard, used each night to measure the Glovehall Hush. The newest knots are on the side nearest her own window.
- **A cap under glass** — Aldous's christening cap, in a glass case the size of a book, which she has carried for forty years and never touched bare-handed. She has never opened the case.
- **Sleeping mittens** — lead-lined, padlocked by Prudence each night, because in her sleep her hands go looking for things to close.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

Corrow wants to be told that she is not the cause. She fears only one thing, and it is herself: that the god coming back through her hands is not a duty but a hunger, that she has come to want the moment the room goes cold. She cannot bear laughter. She left Aldous's wedding at the toast.

**Secret the GM can reveal:** the Hush at Glovehall is growing from her rooms, not her brother's. She has measured it every night for a year with the knotted cord. She has hired others to measure it (see In Play) because she wants someone, anyone, to come back with a different answer. **The 610 secret:** she told the Regent she did not hold back. That is true. She felt her hand go into him and close around something, and then he opened his eyes, and she has never told anyone what her hand felt it close.

**Secret she does not know:** her son Aldous has been forging licenses for two years, with his mother's name on them, and selling them to the Heirs, so that grandmothers in the Sinks might be finished at the Hush edge by Heirs instead of being dragged there. He does it because it is the only way he has ever found to touch her work.

#### Ties

- **Osric Vane** — her brother, whom she loves and has locked in the west wing. She holds one of the two mitten-keys. She visits once a month and they speak through the door.
- **Ansel Grue** — her master, whose face she has touched once. She does not know if she failed him or freed him.
- **Lord Merriman Vane** — her Kept husband in the south wing, who refuses her every year. She envies him his refusal.
- **Aldous Vane** — her son, a Licenser, three paces away all his life. He forges her name.
- **Lord Absalom Hethe** — her brother's only other visitor. She finds him charming and does not trust a single word.

- **Pim Sorrel** — a child who can do what Corrow does without the grey, without the cold, and without the Court. Corrow has asked for her to be brought to Glovehall. She will not say why.

#### In Play

She is paying for a different answer, and if they bring her the true one she will thank them, pay them double, and ask them to forget they ever came.
- **The Forged License.** A grandmother in the Sinks has been finished at the Hush edge with a license bearing Corrow's name, and Corrow did not close her. The Court wants the forger. So, quietly, does Corrow. Following the trail leads to her own son.
- **The Laughing House.** A Weir Row family whose old man she is to close on Tablenight are famous for their laughter; the old man wants to go out laughing. Corrow cannot bear it. She asks the PCs to keep the house quiet. The family asks the PCs to make sure it is not.

#### Who They Are

You know you are near the west wing of Glovehall before you reach it, because the sound goes. Your footsteps on the boards grow soft and then grow distant, as though they belonged to someone walking away from you. The flies stop. The lamps lean away from the corridor. And then there is a door, black, double-barred, with a small hatch at head height, and behind it a voice that is warm, educated, gentle and very lonely, saying, *Hello? Is someone there? Please, come and talk. I promise to keep my eyes shut.*

Osric is fifty-four and looks older and younger at once. His face has gone smooth and grey, the features softening as if seen through the Breath, and the lips curve gently upward in an expression everyone at Glovehall has agreed not to call a smile. His hair has gone white and very fine and lifts a little in the still air of the room as if in a draught. He is thin; he eats little, out of a sense that he should not. He wears a scholar's gown, much mended by a Seamer who comes through the hatch. His hands, inside the lead mittens, are grey to the elbow, long, with nine joints to every finger, and when they move inside the lead you can hear them, a dry fan-like clicking like an abacus counted very fast, in a room too quiet to carry the sound.

He reads constantly. He is courteous to everyone, interested in everyone, and asks every guard about their family.

#### Their Story

Osric was born in 587, seven years after his sister, and was the gentler and the cleverer of the two. He came into the Course at nineteen, early even for a Vane, and was a promising Closer of the Court by twenty-five. In 628, on the Landing, he shook hands with a Fatlands meat-factor named Pell Ambry, without thinking, bare, because the man had held his out and Osric had been raised to be polite. Ambry fell to Ruin on the planks and rose Kept an hour later. He can never go home. He lives in Stranger's Row, and writes to his wife in the south, and she does not write back.

Osric asked for the mittens himself, the next morning. In 629, when his hands began to close things he had not touched, he asked to be locked in. He has been in the west wing for twelve years. He reached the Brink in 636. Since then he has not needed to touch anything at all.

#### Their Place in the Land

Officially Osric is an heir of the Vane house, a Hand of the Court in abeyance, and an invalid. In fact he is the most dangerous thing in the Court's keeping and the clearest proof of what every Hand-line is becoming, and the Court does not know what to do with him. The Leaden party would wall him up. The Quiet Market would sell him. The Regent receives a weekly report on his mittens and does nothing. He is guarded by a detail of six Kept soldiers of the Fen Guard, changed every month, and visited by his sister once a month, by a Seamer once a week, and by Lord Absalom Hethe every Thursday.

His routine is the routine of a prisoner who has chosen his cell: reading (a guard turns the pages through the hatch with a long ivory paddle), writing (he dictates), long conversations through the door, and, at night, sitting very still in the middle of the floor with his eyes shut, trying not to want.

#### What They Carry

- **Lead mittens** — two, cast for him by a Lastgate plumber in 629 and re-cast twice as his hands lengthened, padlocked at the wrist with Oathen locks. Each weighs nine pounds. Armor 4 to the hands only; a blow with one is a Stunning strike. The keys are held by Corrow and by the Captain of the detail.
- **A silk blindfold** — grey, which he puts on, awkwardly, with his mittened hands, whenever anyone opens the hatch, so that he will not finish them by looking.
- **The page-turner** — a long ivory paddle, slid through the hatch for the guards to use. He has a list of the books he has read since 629. It is three hundred and nine titles long.
- **A knotted thread** — unravelled from his blanket and knotted with his teeth: twelve knots. One for each guard he has finished through the door. He keeps it under his tongue when he sleeps.
- **A dead fly** — under an upturned glass on his table, the first thing his Closing Gaze ever finished, in 636. Perfect, unrotting, eight years dead. He keeps it to remind himself what looking can do.
- **Hethe's letters** — forty-one letters from Lord Absalom Hethe, read to him by the guards and kept in a box. They speak of usefulness, of service, of the Rim, of a great table where a man of his gifts would be honored.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

Osric wants to be of use. He has spent twelve years doing nothing but not-harming, and it has nearly broken him, and he would accept almost any task framed as a duty: the Reckoners, the Fast, the Second Table, anyone. He fears that he is the cause of the Glovehall Hush, and that his sister has been too kind to tell him.

**Secret the GM can reveal:** he has been finishing his guards through the door, by stare, one a month, for a year. He chooses the ones who have told him they are tired: the ones who say, through the hatch, that they wish it were over. He listens for days to be sure. Then he takes off the blindfold and looks at the place in the door where he knows they are standing, for a full minute. The Court replaces them and does not connect the deaths. He keeps count. **Secret he does not know:** the Glovehall Hush is growing from Corrow's rooms, not his. He has been blaming himself for a silence his sister made. And **Lord Absalom Hethe** does not mean to use him against an enemy. He means to carry him to the Rim in a lead coffin and put him to the work of closing a god when it is ready to be eaten again.

#### Ties

- **Lady Corrow Vane** — his sister, whom he adores and whom he has not seen bare-faced in twelve years. They speak through the door. He does not know what she is hiding.
- **Lord Absalom Hethe** — his only friend, he believes. Thursday is the best day of his week.
- **Pell Ambry** — the Fatlander he made Kept with a handshake. Osric pays his rent in Stranger's Row through a lawyer, and has never been able to write to him.
- **Captain Dace Morrow** — whose men guard him, and keep dying. Morrow has begun to suspect.
- **Ansel Grue** — who reads the weekly reports and has never visited.
- **Lord Casimir Hollowe-Vane** — a young cousin who wrote to him every month, and stopped. Osric believes, rightly, that Casimir ran from Sallowreach to avoid becoming him.
- **Pim Sorrel** — he has heard about her through the hatch. He has asked to meet her, with his eyes covered. He would like to ask her whether it is cold.

#### In Play

PCs meet Osric if they are hired to guard him, visit him on Corrow's or Hethe's errand, try to steal him for the Second Table, the Heirs, the Finishers or Marrow Jack, or come to Glovehall to measure the garden and hear a voice through a door asking for news.

- **The Thirteenth Guard.** A PC is posted to the west-wing detail. Osric is charming. He asks how the PC sleeps. A week later he asks whether the PC is tired. Players who read the knotted thread may work out what is coming.
- **The Lead Coffin.** Lord Hethe has commissioned a lead coffin from a plumber in Tanner's Boards, with air-holes and a lock on the inside. The plumber, frightened, hires the PCs to find out who it is for.
- **The Useful Man.** A Hush is crossing the border road toward the Last Lamp. The Court considers asking Osric to stand at its edge and stare it back. It is not certain he can, or that he would not simply widen it. Osric is thrilled to be asked.

**Threat:** he does not threaten; he apologizes, and the room goes quiet. If attacked, he holds still and asks that nobody hurt themselves on him. **Kindness:** he weeps, without moving his face. **The god:** he feels it as peace, and he is frightened of how much he likes it. **If nobody intervenes:** Lord Hethe takes him out of Glovehall in the lead coffin within the year, by night, across the Sump. He is never seen again in the north. Somewhere on the Rim, a long time later, a regrowing god is closed like a book, and the man who did it is thanked, and then sealed back into his lead.
