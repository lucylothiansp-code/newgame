# The Fast: The Country Kept for a Guest

> Carry your own. Count your chairs. Close the gate, and then go through it again.
>> — what a Wanting mother says at the door each morning, in place of goodbye

[fiction]
A day of Meagre Toft, Measure-Bearer, aged fifteen, of Pantry Close in Wanting. Set down by the Hostwatch clerk who walked with her, for the ledger of ordinary days the Warden of the Map keeps "so that someone will remember what the Fast was like before."

She wakes in the dark because the cold wakes her. There is frost on the inside of the shutter, and she puts her palm flat on it before she does anything else, because frost inside means the god was nowhere near the house in the night. Then she puts her palm flat on the bricked place in the wall where her great-grandmother's grandmother had a hearth. Cold. Good. She says the Five Rules under her breath into the blanket, all five, quickly, the way you check your pockets, and gets up.

Her mother is already standing against the wall, dressed in everything she owns. Her little brother is asleep on the pallet with his head to the door. Nobody says good morning. Her mother says, "Carry your own," and Meagre says, "I carry my own," and takes the ration-cloth off the shelf, and the water flask, and the wax tin, and goes out, and closes the door behind her from the outside, and then opens it and goes back in and closes it again from the inside and goes out again. That is how her family does it. Other families have other ways. All of them are about the door.

Pantry Close is grey at that hour and full of people standing still. The Ration Line has formed in the night, as it does every night, four abreast and back along the Close and round the corner into Hillward, a long dark bundle of wool and breath. Nobody talks in the Line. It costs too much. You can hear the whole town breathing, and the breath smells of pear-drops, which is the smell of bodies eating their own fat because there is nothing else, and you can hear the Pantry clock, which has no face, only a bell, and you can hear coughing. Meagre does not join the Line. She goes round the side to the Bearers' door, which is the only door in Wanting that opens inward, and which is held by a spearman who looks at her face and her hands and the Bearer's cord at her wrist, and says, "Declined," and she says, "Declined," and he lets her in.

Inside the Pantry it is warmer than anywhere else in the town, and it is not warm. It smells of sacking and mouse and dust, and, very faintly, of oats, and to Meagre that is the smell of being alive. Mother Stint Avery is at the great brass scales in the weighing-hall, as she is every morning, with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her forearms white with oat-dust. She does not look up. She is weighing out the Bearers' measures for the outlying hamlets into cloth bundles, one bundle per name, and as she ties each one she says the name aloud, once, flatly, and a clerk writes it, and another clerk checks it against the list, and the bundle goes into a Bearer's basket. Meagre's basket is for Thinwick and Sallow Hatch and the four farms along the Brindle road. Thirty-one names. She counts the bundles. Thirty-one. She counts them again. She is not supposed to look at the list. She has never looked at the list. She knows there were thirty-three names last week.

She walks out of Wanting with the basket on her back as the sky goes pale. The frost is thick on the fields east of the town, which is good. The frost is thin on the fields west, which is not. Out past the Frostline markers, which are white-painted stakes the Hostwatch move every week, the grass goes from white to grey to green, and you can see where the warm ground begins, a soft edge like the line a wet cloth leaves on a table. She keeps east of it. Everyone does. The road is empty and very quiet and the basket is heavy, and the bundles in it smell of oats and lard, and her stomach turns over once, hard, like something in a sack.

At Thinwick a woman is waiting at the hamlet stone with her hands folded. Meagre gives her four bundles. The woman says, "No, thank you," three times, and Meagre offers a fourth time, and the woman takes them, and does not look into Meagre's basket, and does not ask about the two names that are not in it. At the second farm on the Brindle road nobody comes out. Meagre stands at the gate and calls the name, as she is supposed to, three times. Then she puts the bundle on the gatepost, as she is supposed to, and walks on, and does not look back, because a bundle left on a gatepost is not offered, it is only set down, and whoever takes it is carrying their own.

The smell finds her on the way home, at about the time of the afternoon when the light in the Fast stops changing. Bread. It comes from the west, faint, over the warm ground, and her mouth floods so fast she chokes. She turns her face into the wind. There is no wind. She turns her face east, then, toward the Rim, and walks with her mouth open and her tongue flat, breathing as little as she can, and spits every forty steps, and counts them, and does not think about bread, and thinks about bread.

At night Pantry Close is lit by one lamp at the iron door, under which the Keeper reads the next day's list. Meagre goes, as everyone goes. Mother Stint Avery reads the names without any expression at all, for forty minutes, in a voice that does not change on any name. Meagre hears her own. She hears her mother's. She hears her brother's. She does not hear the name of the man who was weeping in the Cold Yards, and when she looks for him in the crowd he is already walking away, slowly, toward the Lean End, with his hands at his sides.

She goes home. She closes the door from the outside, and opens it, and closes it from the inside. She puts her palm on the bricked hearth. Cold. She lies down with her head to the door. Somewhere out on the warm ground, a long way west, there is a single light, gold, the size of a pinprick, where a table has been laid in an empty house for someone, and she watches it through the crack in the shutter until she falls asleep, and does not say thank you to it, and wants to.

## Districts and Settlements

### Wanting: The Town With No Kitchens

Wanting sits on a long, low, limestone ridge on the eastern margin of the Fast, half a day's walk from Brindle Cross and the Rim Road. It was built in 188 A.G., when the last Unfed abandoned the heartland, and it was built to a single idea: that nothing in it should look like an invitation. The streets run straight and the houses are all the same: long, low, grey, slate-roofed, chimneyless, with narrow windows and iron-strapped doors hung to open outward. There are no signs, no painted shutters, no window-boxes, no benches. There is nowhere to sit down in the whole of Wanting except the floor of your own house. Visitors from the Rim find it the most depressing town on the Table, and say so, and are not invited back, which is a joke the Unfed enjoy in silence.

About nine thousand people live in Wanting, and about four thousand more in the hamlets and steadings scattered in a ring around it, none more than a day's walk out, all on the cold side of the Frostline. Everything in the Fast that is not the god's lives within that ring. The town has no walls. It has the Frostline, a ring of white-painted stakes the Hostwatch move every week to mark where the warm ground ends; inside it is the Unfed's country, and outside it is Orrum's.

The town is divided into quarters that the Unfed do not call quarters, since a quarter is a portion. They call them *stands*, after the stretches of the trench at Hallowboard where the families of the hill once stood, and each stand has its own families, its own habits about doors, its own way of saying the Decline. A child of Hillward can be told from a child of the Spit by how they close a gate.

#### Pantry Close

The heart of Wanting is not a square, because a square is a place to gather and gathering is a kind of feast. It is a wide, bare, cobbled ring around the Pantry tower, kept swept and empty, with the town's six main streets running into it like spokes. The Ration Line forms here every night and moves at dawn, four abreast, round the tower and in at the window on the north side, where each household's chit is taken and its measure handed out through an iron hatch. At dusk the Close fills again for the reading of the list. The rest of the day it is the emptiest place in Wanting.

It smells of cold stone, wool, and the pear-drop breath of the Line. It sounds like coughing, and the bell of the Pantry clock, which strikes the hours and, on list nights, a single extra stroke after the last name. Pantry clerks, Measure-Bearers and Listers live in the narrow houses around the Close, and are envied for it, because to live within sight of the iron door is to feel, a little, that you will not be forgotten.

*Custom:* nobody crosses the Close diagonally. You walk around it, as you walk around the bare space in the middle of your own house where a table would be. Children who cut across are made to walk the full ring three times. *Danger:* the Close is where the omitted learn that they are omitted, and every few weeks one of them does not take it quietly. A man left off the list in 639 stood at the iron door and beat on it with both fists until the bones in his hands broke, and the spearmen let him, because to stop him would have been to touch him, and to touch him would have been a kindness.

#### Hillward

The oldest stand, on the crown of the ridge, where the families who claim the clearest descent from Hallowboard live in the longest, coldest, barest houses in town. Hillward houses are older than the rest and plainer, if that is possible, and their doors are the heaviest: some are two inches of oak strapped with four bands of iron, and take two people to open. The Abstinent's elders live here, and Wont Bellamy, and the Wend family. Elder Forbear Wend is carried down Hillward Row to the Pantry every morning in his ash chair, and the street stands back against the walls as he goes by.

Hillward smells of nothing at all, which is its boast. It is the quietest stand in Wanting, and the most watched, because its people are watched by everyone else for any sign of softness: a curtain, a cushion, a cheek that has filled out. The Hillward families trade in nothing. They hold standing, which in the Fast is the only property: the right to stand at a particular place in the trench at Hallowboard on Tablenight, handed down from the ones who stood.

*Custom:* Hillward households keep a *stand-stone*, a chip of chalk from their ancestors' stretch of the trench, set into the floor by the door, and every member of the family puts a bare foot on it before going out. *Danger:* reputation. A Hillward family whose member is seen enjoying a meal can lose its stand, and with it everything, inside a season.

[quick] Old Pinch Corve, Hillward Doorwarden — Threat 3 · Flesh 9 · Guard 10 · Attack none · Armor 0 · Dread 0. Ninety-one, blind, sits at the top of Hillward Row from dawn to dusk and can name every person who passes by the sound of their breathing; he reports any breath that smells of anything but hunger.

#### The Cold Yards

Six walled squares of beaten earth on the south and west edges of town, where all the cooking in Wanting is done. The walls are high, to keep the smell of food from reaching the streets, and the yards are open to the sky. In each yard there are dozens of fire-pits, shallow scrapes in the earth lined with flint, and at the two hours of the day when the yards are open (an hour after dawn and an hour before dusk) each pit has a household crouched at it, warming the day's measure in a clay pot over a fire made of a single handful of Rim kindling. The fire is stamped out the moment the pot steams. No fire in Wanting burns longer than it takes to warm a mouthful.

The Yards are the closest thing in Wanting to a public life, and they are dreadful. Two hundred hungry people crouched in a walled square, each over a little pot, each with their back to the others, each careful not to look, each careful not to smell, and the air thick with the smell of warm oats and lard, which to an Unfed nose is very nearly unbearable. It sounds like spoons on clay and the hiss of stamped-out fires, and every evening, somewhere, someone weeping while they eat.

*Custom:* you eat facing the wall. You eat standing. You do not close your eyes. Children are taught by being struck lightly on the back of the head. *Danger:* the Yards are where hoarding and mouth-theft happen, and where the Abstinent's Yard-wardens watch for them. A Yard-warden's word can cut a family's measure for a month. Worse: on still evenings the smell from the Yards travels west, and twice in living memory a table has been found laid in the West Yard at dawn, inside the walls, with every fire-pit lit.

[quick] Yard-Warden Fallow Steen — Threat 2 · Flesh 11 · Guard 12 · Attack +4 (staff 3) · Armor 1 · Dread 0. A broad, sour woman who walks the West Yard with a staff and a slate, and marks the names of anyone she sees chewing with their eyes shut. She is very good at it because she does it herself, every night, alone.

#### The Spit

A low, muddy, crowded stand on the east side of the ridge, down by the Brindle road, where the Crumb-Runners live. It has the only stables in Wanting, long sheds of rough timber for the oxen and handcarts, and the only buildings with locks on the inside: Runners keep their stock in their houses for a night before the Pantry takes it, and they have learned to bar their doors. The Spit is called the Spit because that is what the rest of Wanting does when a Runner walks by, and it was called that so long ago that the Runners call it that too, without irony, the way a scarred man calls himself Scar.

It smells of ox, wet straw, axle-grease, sacking, and food. That last is the thing. Even after the cargo has gone up to the Pantry, the houses of the Spit smell of oats and cheese and salt fish, faintly, all the time, and to other Unfed the smell is both an outrage and a pull, so that children of other stands dare one another to walk the length of Spit Lane breathing through the nose. The Runners are the least pious, least thin and best informed people in the Fast. They are spat on in the street. They wipe it off and go on.

*Custom:* a Runner who comes home from a run stands in Spit Lane and empties every pocket onto the ground, and turns them out, so that the street can see they have brought back nothing for themselves. *Danger:* poachers. Kale Dragoman's agents drink at the Brimming Cup on the Rim side of Brindle Cross and buy information from Runners in debt, and more than one Unfed taken across the Rim was named to the poachers by someone in the Spit.

[quick] Sparing Doyle, Road-Master of the Spit — Threat 3 · Flesh 15 · Guard 13 · Attack +5 (ox-goad 3) · Armor 1 · Dread 0. Owns eleven carts and twenty-two oxen, the largest fleet in Wanting; eats on the road and lies about it; is the only person in the Spit with whom the Abstinent will speak directly, and despises them for it.

#### The Lean End

The far western tip of the ridge, beyond the last of the Cold Yards, where the houses thin out into sheds and byres and then stop. The Lean House stands here, long and windowless, and around it a scatter of small, half-empty cottages where the families of the omitted live, close enough to visit and far enough not to have to. Abide Kettering's cart-shed is at the bottom of the lane. So is the Bury Gate, through which the dead of Wanting are carried out to the fields.

It is quiet. It smells of clean straw, lye, and something under it that the Unfed have a word for and other peoples do not: the sweetish, appley, nail-varnish smell of a body in the last week of hunger. It is the only part of Wanting with a view west, and on still evenings the people of the Lean End can see the warm ground beginning a quarter-mile away, and lights in it, here and there, where tables are laid.

*Custom:* people walking to the Lean House are not greeted, not looked at, and not offered anything, as a courtesy. People coming back are greeted as though from a long journey. *Danger:* the view. The Lean End is the stand from which most of Wanting's walkers go: people, omitted or not, who get up in the night and walk west toward a light. The Hostwatch keep a rope strung across the end of the lane, belled, and a watchman, and most nights the watchman hears a bell.

[quick] Cease Abbot, Lean End Watchman — Threat 2 · Flesh 12 · Guard 12 · Attack +3 (cudgel 3) · Armor 0 · Dread 0. A one-eyed former Tablebreaker who sits up every night by the bell-rope, and who has stopped a hundred walkers, and lately has begun, on some nights, to look the other way.

#### Bell Ward

The stand of young families, on the gentle southern slope of the ridge, where the houses are smallest and closest together and the doors are hung with hand-bells. Every household with a child under twelve keeps a bell at the door, and at dusk, and whenever the smell on the wind goes sweet, the mother or eldest stands at the door and rings it and counts the children through. On a sweet-smelling evening the whole of Bell Ward rings at once, a thin, frightened, tinny peal, and the counting can be heard up and down the lanes in a hundred voices: *one, two, three, in. One, two, in.*

Bell Ward smells of wet wool and the sour milk-and-ammonia of babies in hard-washed linen. It sounds like bells, and children reciting the Five Rules in chorus at the Rule-house at the bottom of the slope, where every child of the stand is taught. It is the stand the god loves best, and everyone knows it.

*Custom:* when a child goes missing, the family rings the bell at dusk for a year and a day, calling the child's name, and then takes the bell down and gives it to the Rule-house, where it hangs on a long beam among hundreds of others. Nobody rings the beam. *Danger:* Little Fain lives here now, with a foster-family who did not ask for the duty, and the other children of Bell Ward follow Fain about.

[quick] Rule-Mistress Withhold Penn — Threat 2 · Flesh 10 · Guard 10 · Attack none · Armor 0 · Dread 0. Teaches the Five Rules to three hundred children; can recite them so fast they sound like a single word; has found, three mornings this season, a little chair pulled out at the Rule-house door, child-sized, the seat warm.

#### Pinners' Yard

A walled court behind the Pantry, entered from Pantry Close by a narrow gate, where the Hostwatch keep their lodgings, their rope-store, their lamp-room, and the stair down to the Great Map in the cellar below the Pantry. Trackers come and go at all hours, muddy to the thigh, with warm-ground reports scrawled on slates, and the Pin-Setters run them down the stair to be pricked onto the linen. The Yard is the only place in Wanting where people talk about Orrum as a thing that does things rather than a thing to be refused, and the Abstinent watch it closely.

It smells of lamp oil, wet rope and the beeswax the Pin-Setters use to stiffen their linen. It sounds like boots, the clack of slates, and arguments, low and fierce, about gaits and directions and why the god paused for an hour at a particular gate.

*Custom:* a Tracker coming in from the field touches the Yard gate with a bare palm and says, aloud, how far they were from the god at the closest. The number is chalked on the gatepost. It has been getting smaller. *Danger:* the Hostwatch's restricted ledgers, which record the extra chair, are kept here, and two Pin-Setters have been omitted from the list in the last year after copying them.

[quick] Pin-Setter Abate Lowrie — Threat 1 · Flesh 9 · Guard 11 · Attack none · Armor 0 · Dread 0. A fourteen-year-old girl with perfect hands who sets four hundred pins a week, and who has noticed, though nobody has asked her, that the newest pins on the Rim Road make a circle, and that the circle is closing.

#### Knife Row and the West Gate

A single long street of hard-used houses running out to the West Gate, where the Reckoners muster on the first day of Carving and where volunteers come to stand and be looked at. Reckoner families live along it, and Reckoners home between raids, and the widows and widowers of Reckoners who did not come home. The doors on Knife Row are painted black, the only painted doors in Wanting, and each one has notches cut in its lintel: one for each line the household's Knives have ended.

Knife Row smells of the grey, fatless leather Reckoners wear, and of whetstone dust, and, faintly, of Rim weather: wet road, foreign smoke. It sounds like boots and very little talk. Reckoners home from a raid do not speak of it, and the Row has a way of falling silent when they pass that is not quite respect.

*Custom:* a returning band walks the length of the Row from the West Gate to the Pantry carrying nothing, with their empty hands held out at their sides, palms forward, to show they took nothing from the houses they entered. *Danger:* bedside hunger. Reckoners home from the Rim are sometimes not right, and Knife Row has more walkers, more Breaks and more quiet deaths in the night than any stand but the Lean End.

[quick] Forswear Tallow, Reckoner Widow — Threat 2 · Flesh 11 · Guard 12 · Attack +4 (knife 2) · Armor 0 · Dread 0. A descendant of Forswear Kell, keeper of the Row's notched lintels and its memory; she mends Reckoner greys for nothing, and asks every returning Knife the same question, *what were the beds like*, and writes down the answers.

### Beyond the Ridge

Out from Wanting, inside the Frostline, the Unfed live in hamlets and steadings of between a dozen and a few hundred people, each a cold grey cluster of houses around a bare patch of ground where, in any other land, there would be a green. They live on what the Measure-Bearers carry out to them, and on what they can trade at the Fourth-Day market, and on nothing else. Beyond the Frostline, in Orrum's country, there are no Unfed settlements at all, only the stations of those whose work takes them there.

#### Brindle Cross

The crossing point where the Wanting road meets the Rim, already described in the core book: toll-house, stockade, weighing-shed, Marshal Keep Aldane's garrison of sixty, and the Brimming Cup on the Rim side. At ground level it is the noisiest place an Unfed child will ever see: Company wagons, drovers swearing in six accents, oxen bawling, the clatter of scales, the Brimming Cup's kitchen door banging open and shut and letting out gusts of frying. Unfed clerks in grey stand at the weighing-shed with their noses plugged and sign for sacks they do not open.

The garrison's huts stand on the Fast side, behind a ditch, and they are cold, because Aldane will not have a fire in them. The pilgrim camp has grown up along the Rim side of the ditch since 640: tents, lean-tos, carts, and some three hundred Fatlanders sitting in the mud, waiting to be allowed through, and singing.

*Custom:* the eight-year-olds of Wanting are brought here once to watch Rim folk eat through the Brimming Cup's window. Afterward they are made to say the Decline at the window, three times, while the diners watch them back. *Danger:* everything at the Cross is for sale, including people, and the pilgrims are now so many that the garrison cannot watch the ditch at night.

[quick] Tally-Clerk Spare Orme, Pantry Weigher at the Cross — Threat 2 · Flesh 10 · Guard 10 · Attack none · Armor 0 · Dread 0. Has signed for every sack that has come through Brindle Cross in nine years, and knows to the bushel how much lighter the autumn deliveries were; has written it down twice, and both pages have gone missing from his ledger.

#### Avery Steading

A hamlet of a hundred and forty souls, the furthest north-east of any Unfed settlement, at the end of a stony track two days' walk from Wanting, on a bleak shoulder of upland where the Rim's winter bites hard. Its houses are older than Wanting, built by families who would not move all the way in, and the Avery family has held its stand there for four hundred years. It is a village of old people. Most of its young have gone to Wanting or the Reckoners; there are nine children. It is the furthest from the Pantry, the oldest, and the one with the fewest children, which is to say that it is exactly the kind of village Mother Stint Avery has put on her winter list.

It smells of peat they are not allowed to burn, sheep-wool caught on the thorns, and the cold. It sounds like wind, which is rare in the Fast and precious, and the old people of Avery Steading sit in it with their faces turned up, because wind means the god is far away. They trade nothing. They knit. Avery Steading greys are the warmest in the Fast, and the village sends them down to the Fourth-Day market in bales.

*Custom:* the steading keeps a "letter to the Keeper", a long cloth onto which every household stitches one line each Lack, telling the Pantry how they are. It is sent down with the Measure-Bearer. Mother Stint Avery, who was born here, reads it every year, and has never answered it. *Danger:* the Measure-Bearer's road is long, and in Lack the snow cuts the steading off for weeks at a time. This winter, when the snow comes, the Bearer will not set out.

[quick] Grandam Bare Avery, the Steading's Eldest — Threat 2 · Flesh 8 · Guard 9 · Attack none · Armor 0 · Dread 0. Ninety-four, Stint Avery's great-aunt, still knitting; has had a dream three nights running in which Stint, as a girl of nine, stands at the steading stone with a basket and walks past.

#### Thinwick

A hamlet of sixty people two hours' walk south of Wanting, on a chalk stream that the Unfed drink from because it is not offered. It is a poor place even by the Fast's measure: thatched cottages whose thatch nobody can afford to renew, a Rule-stone where the children recite, a single well with a lid chained shut. Abstain Rooke lives here with her mother. So do two families of Reckoners and a retired Tablebreaker who sits by the stream all day mending axe-hafts for the whole district.

Thinwick smells of wet chalk and watercress, which grows in the stream and which nobody may eat, since it grows in the Fast. It sounds like water. The children of Thinwick are the best-behaved in the district, because the hamlet is on the edge of the Frostline, and the warm ground comes up to the bottom of its lane every few weeks and lies there all night like a dog by a door.

*Custom:* every Thinwick window has a sill, and every sill is kept bare. The hamlet's mothers check them at dawn. One has not been checked in a while, because the mother of that house has been working double turns in the Cold Yards since her name was moved to the bottom of the list. *Danger:* the warm ground, and what is on the sill.

Broke seventy tables in his day; now mends hafts and watches the children; has seen what the Rooke girl puts on her windowsill, and has not decided what to do about it, because the cake that came back last week smelled like his wife's.

#### Corve Hatch

The ruined village where, in 44 A.G., the family of Abel Corve sat down at their own table. The village was abandoned within a decade of the First Sitting, and it lies now well beyond the Frostline, in the kept country, a quarter-hour from the Long Table road. Its houses are perfect. The Corve house stands in the middle of them with its window bricked up from outside. The family is still at their table, behind the brick. The Hostwatch, who have put an ear to the wall, say that on still nights you can hear a spoon, very slowly, against a plate, about once an hour.

The Corve family's descendants, by a sister who did not sit, live in Wanting and in Hillward, and they make a pilgrimage here once a year, on the anniversary of the sitting, to stand with their backs to the bricked window through a night. It is the only pilgrimage the Unfed make that is not to Hallowboard, and it is a pilgrimage of shame.

*Custom:* every visitor adds a brick. The bricking over the window is now four feet thick and has begun to lean. *Danger:* the village is laid for guests. Every house in Corve Hatch has its table set every night, and every door is open a hand's width, and the voices that call from them are Corve voices, saying, *Cousin, cousin, there's plenty, come and sit with us*.

#### Barrowgate

A cluster of a dozen hard little stone huts at the foot of Hallowboard hill, beside the Barrows, the only Unfed settlement in the heartland, permitted by the Abstinent because someone must keep the Stand-Roll. The Stand-Roll is a ledger of the trench: an account, stretch by stretch and footprint by footprint, of which family is believed to have stood where on the nine days of the Refusal. Unfed who come to swear an oath on the ones who stood come to Barrowgate first, to be told by the Roll-Keepers where their ancestor's footprint is. The Keepers are a hereditary order of eight families, and they live on a full measure, carried out by Bearers who walk two days each way to bring it.

Barrowgate smells of chalk dust and the long pale grass of the Barrows, which smells, on warm evenings, like hair. It is very quiet. It sounds like the Roll-Keepers reciting the Roll, which takes three days to say aloud and is said once a season. The hill above is always clean.

*Custom:* a swearer walks up the hill barefoot, and down it backward, never turning their back on the stone until they are out of sight of it, and then turns and walks home without looking round. *Danger:* the eighth side. The Roll-Keepers do not keep a record for it, and do not let anyone stand there, and do not say why, and lately they have taken to sleeping in shifts, because on still nights something has been walking around the stone, slowly, and stopping on that side, and standing there, and the chalk in the morning is still untrodden.

[quick] Roll-Keeper Reft Lammas — Threat 2 · Flesh 10 · Guard 11 · Attack none · Armor 0 · Dread 0. Seventy, keeper of the third stretch of the Stand-Roll, who can tell from a footprint in the chalk the weight and age of the person who made it six hundred years ago; she has started finding new footprints, small and bare and fresh, in the old ones.

#### Splinterhall

A long turf-roofed lodge and a scatter of huts in a hollow four miles west of Wanting, just inside the Frostline, where the Tablebreaker bands of the eastern Fast make their base. It is the only place in the Fast where axes are made, and the hollow rings all day with the sound of hafts being shaped and heads being ground. A great heap of splinters stands in the middle of the hollow, a hundred feet across and twenty high, made of the broken pieces of every table the eastern bands have smashed for two hundred years. Nobody burns it. The splinters do not rot. Some days the heap is warm.

Splinterhall smells of fresh-cut ash, oil, and the faint baking smell that rises from the splinter-heap. It sounds like grinding and the slap of axe-heads tested on stumps. The Breakers who live here are proud, deafened, half-blind from years of gauze, and very good company.

*Custom:* every new Axe brings a splinter from their first table and throws it on the heap without looking. An Old Breaker's splinter is kept, and set into the lodge wall, with their name. *Danger:* the heap. Anyone who sleeps beside it wakes with crumbs on their lips.

#### Wax Halt

A stone post-house at the eastern mouth of the Long Table valley, where the old straight road across the heartland enters between the beech hills. It is not a village, only a waystation kept by a single family on a Pantry charter, and its purpose is to wax the ears of travellers before they take the Long Table road. The family sells wax and tallow plugs, linen nose-binds, blinders, and rope, and keeps a register of everyone who goes in and everyone who comes out the far end at the West Mouth, nine miles on. The two lists are compared by runner every week. They do not always match.

Wax Halt smells of melted tallow and the faint baking smell that rolls out of the valley mouth on the still air. It sounds, if you take your plugs out, like a distant murmur of eleven thousand people talking happily over dinner.

*Custom:* travellers going in leave something of theirs on the Halt's long shelf (a glove, a knife, a button) and take it back on the way out. The shelf is full of things nobody came back for. *Danger:* the Halt-keepers. The family has kept Wax Halt for six generations and in that time has never once taken out its own ear-plugs, and it shows.

[quick] Halt-Keeper Sober Pell — Threat 2 · Flesh 11 · Guard 11 · Attack +3 (cudgel 3) · Armor 0 · Dread 1. Has worn wax in his ears since he was six; speaks too loudly; reads lips; has begun to suspect that his wife has been taking her plugs out at night to listen, and that she is smiling.

#### Saints' Acre

A sweep of open fields south of Wanting, outside the last hamlet but inside the Frostline, where the Starved Saints sit. The order has no house. Its Abstainers sit in the open on hurdles and in shallow scrapes, wrapped in sacking, spaced well apart so that no one is near enough to offer anyone anything, and the Watchers walk among them, tending the dying and recording last refusals. The field is dotted with low stones. Each marks where an Abstainer sat and stopped.

It smells of cold earth and, faintly, of the pear-drop sweetness of fasting breath, so strong in places that it catches in the throat. It sounds like the Decline, said at every sound, so that a crow landing or a pilgrim's footstep sets off a soft wave of *no, thank you* across the whole field. Pilgrims from Wanting, and lately from the Rim, come to the field's edge to touch the robes of the Saints who have lasted, and are kept back by the Watchers.

*Custom:* families of novices come to the field's edge to plead, and stand at a distance, because to offer food would be an insult. They are allowed to stand as long as they like. Some stand until the end. *Danger:* the field is changing. Sister Nay Corliss has sat here three years. The frost around her hurdle melts each night in a wide ring, and two other Abstainers have moved their hurdles closer to her.

## Roads and Ways

The Fast is the easiest country on the Table to walk and the hardest to cross. The roads of the heartland are old and good: chalk-and-flint drove roads, sunken lanes between laid hedges, causeways over the water-meadows, all six centuries unused and all perfectly kept, the ruts filled, the drains clear, the milestones scrubbed. There are no bandits. There are no beasts; the wolves and foxes of the old heartland starved out in the first century and never came back. The weather never turns. A traveller can walk thirty miles a day on a level, dry, golden road, through a landscape of combed fields and smoking chimneys, and see nobody, and hear nothing but their own feet, and be offered dinner at every house they pass.

The Unfed travel in four ways, and each has its own discipline.

**On foot, in company, roped.** The commonest. Unfed never travel alone outside the Frostline if they can help it. A party walks in single file with a rope running through every belt, so that no one can step out of line toward a light without pulling the others, and the last walker in the file carries the axe. At night the party sleeps in the open, back to back, the rope knotted round every wrist, with one watcher awake to sniff the air. The Unfed call this *walking the string*, and a person who is good company on the road is "good on the string".

**By ox-cart, by night.** The Crumb-Runners' way. Oxen are the only animal that will work in the Fast, because oxen always know: they stop dead when the warm ground is ahead, and no goad will move them, and a Runner who trusts his oxen lives. Horses and mules go mad in the smell within a day and bolt toward it. Runners travel at night because the lights of the laid tables can be seen at night, and avoided, whereas by day a table in a farmhouse kitchen looks like nothing at all until you are at the door.

**By warm ground.** The Hostwatch's way, and nobody else's. A Tracker walks the warm path the god has left across the fields the night before, a mile behind where it is now, measuring and pinning. It is the fastest way to cross the heartland, because the god walks straight, and the most dangerous, because the god sometimes stops.

**By the Stoopway.** Nobody's way. The old pilgrim road from Hallowboard to the eastern border, along which Orrum walked on the tenth morning, is the most direct road in the Fast and the Unfed do not walk it, ever. Children dare one another to touch the warm patches worn into the undersides of its lintels. Reckoners in a hurry have been known to take it. They have also been known not to come out.

## Bread and Coin

The Fast has no economy in the sense other lands mean it. It produces nothing. It grows nothing. It exports nothing but its own people, sometimes on the end of a knife and sometimes in a poacher's net. Everything that keeps the Unfed alive comes in through Brindle Cross on credit, is pooled in the Pantry, and goes out again by the list. What passes for an economy is a system of measures, chits, favours, and quiet, desperate trading in the margins, and the whole of it rests on one question: whose name is read tonight.

### The Measure

The unit of everything in the Fast is the **Pantry measure**: half an oatcake and a thumb of fat or cheese per adult per day, with pease or salt fish twice a week. It is half a ration in the core rules' sense, and an Unfed who lives on it neither gains nor loses Pangs. A **full measure** is a whole ration, and goes to hard labourers, Reckoners on campaign, Tablebreakers, Hostwatch in the field, and Measure-Bearers on carrying days. Children under twelve get a three-quarter measure. The Abstinent and their clerks get exactly what everyone else gets, and are watched to make sure of it.

The measure is issued against a **ration chit**: a square of unbleached cloth stamped by the Pantry with a single empty bowl. Chits are issued by household, by name, by list. They cannot lawfully be sold, given, or traded, and they are sold, given, and traded constantly. A chit is worth about one lack to a Crumb-Runner in Plenty, and three in Lack. A chit with a struck name on it is worth nothing at the Pantry window and a great deal at Abide Kettering's crossroads, where poachers buy them to find out who has been omitted.

### The Fourth-Day Market

On the fourth day of each week, a few households set out on cloths, around Nay-Saying Hobb's shop at the top of the Brindle road, whatever they can bear to part with: a knitted grey, a length of rope, a carved bone, a spare wax tin. There are no prices. Everything is valued in measures, and every trade is declined three times by both parties and struck in silence on the fourth asking. It is the only market in the Fast, and it sounds like a great many polite refusals.

### Work and Wages

Nobody in the Fast is paid in coin. The Unfed will not touch bone money, because it is cut from Godeaters and cured in Godeater lungs. Work is paid in measure: the right to draw a full measure instead of a half on the days you work. Crumb-Runners, who deal with the Rim, are paid partly in lacks by the Company and partly in food by the Pantry, and are the only Unfed who ever hold coin, which is one more reason they are spat on. ### Taxes and Dues

There are no taxes in the Fast, because there is nothing to tax. There are dues. Every household on the list owes the Pantry one chit a day, which is to say that the Pantry gives you the chit and then takes it back. Every Hillward family owes the Abstinent one member for one year as a Measure-Bearer or a clerk. Every household owes an axe for Tablenight and a body for the Standing, and every household owes the Hostwatch any report of warm ground within a day. Failure to pay any of these dues is punished in the only way the Fast punishes anything: a cut in the measure.

The real tax is paid abroad. The Rim Road Company sells the Fast its grain on credit, under a contract sworn in Oathen before Jessamy Quill, and the debt is now so old and so large that nobody in the Fast but the Chair of the Abstinent knows its sum. It is paid, in part, in silver: the old temple plate of Orrum, the pewter and silver and linen of the heartland's six hundred temples, carried out of the empty country by Pantry-chartered Gleaners and sold to the Company by weight. The temples are almost stripped. The Pantry has begun, quietly, to send Gleaners into the empty farmhouses for their spoons.

### Who Is Rich

Nobody in the Fast is rich, and three people in the Fast are rich. The **Grain-Lords**, the three Crumb-Runner road-masters who control the drove roads from the Rim (Sparing Doyle of the Spit, Old Heft Barrow of the north road, and a woman known only as the Widow Cask who controls the long western route by Ash Mill), each command dozens of carts, hundreds of Backs, and the gratitude of nobody. They eat. They eat on the road, in Rim inns, sitting down, and everyone knows it, and the Abstinent must bargain with them every season, because if a Grain-Lord stops running, a third of the Fast starves.

The other kind of rich in the Fast is the kind that cannot be spent: **standing**. A Hillward family whose ancestor's footprint is clearly marked on the Stand-Roll is richer, in the Fast's own reckoning, than any Grain-Lord, and can marry its children into any house. Standing can be lost (a member seen enjoying food, a member who accepts, a member who emigrates by taking a Rim name) and it can never be bought. Wont Bellamy has a great deal of it.

## Law and the Knife

The Fast has one law, the Five Rules, and one punishment, the measure. It has no judges, no prisons, no gallows, no fines and no executioners, because each of those would require someone to do something to someone, and the Unfed have arranged their whole society so that nobody has to. The Pantry simply does not give. That is the beauty of it, the Abstinent say, and the horror of it, says everyone who has ever stood in Pantry Close and not heard their name.

### Who Enforces

There are no constables in the Fast. Order is kept by four kinds of people, none of whom would call themselves enforcers.

- **The Listers**, Pantry officials of the third rank, who propose names to be added to and struck from the list. They are not police. They are clerks. They listen. A Lister's ear is the closest thing in the Fast to a court.
- **The Yard-Wardens**, who walk the Cold Yards at mealtimes with staves and slates and mark hoarders, mouth-thieves, feeders and anyone seen enjoying a meal. They have no power but the slate.
- **Marshal Keep Aldane's spearmen** at Brindle Cross, the only armed force the Abstinent maintain, who are permitted to kill poachers and are forbidden to touch an Unfed.
- **Families**, who are given the ration of whoever wronged them, and who keep the oaths on the dead, and who decide, every Tablenight, who stands beside whom.

### Crimes and Punishments

| Crime | How it is known | Punishment |
| Hoarding (keeping back part of a ration) | A Yard-warden's slate; a neighbour's word | A cut to half-measure for a month |
| Mouth-theft (stealing a ration) | The victim stands in the Close and names the thief | The thief's measure goes to the victim for a season |

The table has a pattern, and Unfed children learn it without being told. Every punishment is a portion withheld. The worst crimes have no punishment at all, because the criminal has already been taken out of the world of portions: the one who says *Come in* has made themselves a host, and the one who accepts has made themselves a guest, and the Fast does not deal with either.

### The Standing: How a Trial Works

When an accusation is serious enough that a Lister will not decide it alone (mouth-theft between Hillward families, a murder, a charge of selling a neighbour), it is brought to the Abstinent at a **Standing**. A Standing is held on the top floor of the Pantry, in the bare stone room where the council meets around the empty space where a table would be.

The accused stands in the empty space. That is the whole of the ordeal, and it is enough: to stand for hours on the one spot of floor in the Fast where every Unfed has been taught never to stand, while the elders stand around the walls, is a torment, and some of the accused confess within the hour simply to be allowed to step out of it. The accuser stands at the door, with their back to the room. The elders ask questions. The accused answers. The accuser answers. Nobody sits, nobody drinks, and nobody may leave until it is done, which in Wont Bellamy's time on the council has meant Standings of up to two days, ended when an elder fainted.

There is no verdict. At the end, the Chair of the Abstinent says, "We have heard," and the elders file out. That evening, the list is read in Pantry Close. If the accused's name is on it, they were found innocent. If the victim's family's measure has been raised, they were found guilty. Nobody is told. Everyone knows.

[box: Running a Standing]
A Standing is a social endurance contest. Each hour, the accused rolls Gut + Endure against Hard 14 (Grim 18 if Hollow or worse) to remain standing in the empty space; each failure costs 1 Fray and a −1 to their next Tongue roll. The accuser rolls Caul + Resolve each hour at Routine 10 to keep their back to the room. Arguments are made with Tongue + Persuade or Clause against the elders' collective Resolve (Hard 14, Grim 18 for Hillward elders). A character who steps out of the empty space before the Chair speaks has confessed. Forbear Wend has never once been seen to tire at a Standing. He is held up by two boys the whole time.
[/box]

### The List as Law

Every Unfed understands, and none will say aloud, that the list is the law. The Five Rules keep the god out. The list keeps everyone else in line. A name can be struck for a crime, but a name can also be struck for nothing: because the grain is short, because a village is far, because someone on the council has a long memory. There is no appeal but to stand in the Close and ask. The Abstinent say that this is mercy, because it leaves no blood. Withy Gaunt, who keeps the Lean House, keeps a second column in her book beside the names of the omitted, and in it she writes *why*, when she can find out. Most of the entries are blank.

## The Faithful

The Unfed say they have no religion, because they refused their god. This is untrue in every way that matters. The Fast is the most religious country on the Table. Its whole life is a rite, performed every day by every person, of refusing one particular god, and the rite has its doctrines, its holy places, its calendar, its saints, its heretics and its prayers, all of them shaped around an absence the way the floor of an Unfed house is worn smooth around the place where a table is not.

### The Three Doctrines in the Fast

Every land argues about why the gods held still. The Fast argues about it more bitterly than any, because its god is still here to ask, and has never answered.

**The Theft**, in the Fast, is the Keepers' doctrine and the Abstinent's, and it is preached with satisfaction. The others murdered their gods; the regrowth is justice; the Unfed did not murder, and are therefore the only people on the Table who are not being punished. The Lack that never ended in the Fast is not a punishment but a *wage*: the price of cleanness, paid daily. The Theft is taught in every Rule-house, and the version Unfed children learn ends with the line *They laughed at the table; now the table is laughing at them*.

**The Gift** is the Hostwatch's doctrine, though they would not call it one, and Verger Osmond's. Orrum offered itself out of love. It is offering still. The tables are not traps but the same gift, held out for six hundred years by a host whose guests will not come in. The Gift is not illegal in the Fast, because the Abstinent have never needed to make it so; it is simply *soft*, and softness is suspect, and Unfed who preach it find that the Listers have begun to hear their names less clearly.

**The Bait** is Wont Bellamy's doctrine, and the most dangerous in the Fast, because the Unfed have a version of it that no other land has. Elsewhere the Bait says the gods wanted to be eaten so that they could grow back. In the Fast it is whispered that Orrum wanted to be eaten *and still does*, and that the Unfed, by refusing, have kept a whole god whole, hungry and unhoused in the middle of the world, and that this is the worst thing anyone has ever done. Bellamy's circle draws the obvious conclusion. Meek Garrity's papers have drawn a worse one, and are passed house to house at night.

### Shrines of the Fast

The Unfed have no temples (they pulled them down in 97 A.G., save one), and they would be offended by the word shrine. Nonetheless:

- **The bricked hearth.** Every Unfed house has one: the place in the wall where an old fireplace was filled with brick, or, in newer houses, a patch of brick set into the wall for no reason but to be checked. Every morning and every night, every member of the household lays a bare palm flat on it. If it is cold, they say *cold*, under the breath. That is the commonest prayer in the Fast.
- **The stand-stone.** In Hillward and the older stands, a chip of chalk from the family's stretch of the trench at Hallowboard, set into the floor by the door, on which each member places a bare foot before going out.
- **The Barrows.** The only true holy ground of the Fast, where the dead of the Refusal lie nine and ten deep. Oaths are sworn there, and in the trench above, standing in an ancestor's footprint.
### Rites

**The Morning Palm.** A palm on the cold brick, the word *cold*. Every day.

**The Count.** At dusk, and whenever the wind goes sweet, the household stands at the door and counts its members through it. In Bell Ward, with bells.

**The Decline at the Measure.** Before the day's measure is eaten, it is held in the open hand and refused, three times, under the breath, and eaten on the fourth. A child who stumbles over the Five Rules before the Decline does not eat that day.

### The Long Decline

The only hymn the Unfed sing is sung at the Laying-Down and on Tablenight, unaccompanied, very low, almost on one note, by people who do not have the breath to sing. Its words are older than Wanting. It has eight verses. The Unfed sing seven.

[fiction]
We stood on the hill in the snow, and it was laid for us.
No, thank you.
We stood on the hill, and the ones in front lay down, and we stood on them.
No, thank you.
The god lay on the stone with its throat bare, and smiled, and said, you must be starved.
No, thank you.
We were starved. We said it with our mouths full of nothing.
No, thank you.
The god wept, and the snow went soft, and some of us drank it.
No, thank you.
On the tenth morning it got down from the stone and bowed to us.
No, thank you.
It is still laying the table. It is still asking. We are still here.
No, thank you.
[/fiction]

The eighth verse is not sung. Most Unfed do not know it exists. The Roll-Keepers of Barrowgate know it, and will recite it only to one another, on Tablenight, with their backs to the stone; and a Hostwatch Cartographer who overheard it once wrote down only that it is the shortest verse, and that it does not end with the Decline, and that it begins *And then there was one more*.

## How Power Moves

Power in the Fast does not look like power anywhere else. Nobody in the Fast has an army, a fortune, a palace or a throne. Power in the Fast is the ability to decide who eats, and everything else is arranged around that: the grain that comes in, the names that go out, the god that waits outside, and the people beyond the Rim who would buy any Unfed for a noble's ransom. Draw it as a ring with a hole in the middle, like the Table itself: the Pantry at the centre, the Rim around the edge, and the god walking in the empty space between.

### The Lines of Pull

**The Pantry over everyone.** Every Unfed eats by the list. Every Lister, clerk and Bearer can be made or broken by the Keeper's scales and the Chair's pen. This is the first and last fact of Fast politics.

**The Company over the Pantry.** The Pantry eats on credit. The Company can short it at will, as it did in 511 and as it has done this autumn, and the Abstinent cannot so much as complain, because only the Chair has read the contract, and the Chair will not say what is in it.

**The Reckoners beside the Abstinent.** The Abstinent have never approved a raid and never condemned one. Every Knife draws a full measure. Scant Hollis stands at the Abstinent's door, unofficially, and the Abstinent have never asked her to leave, because the Reckoners are the only proof the Fast has that refusing is something you can *do*, and because nobody on the council wants to find out what nine hundred hungry Knives would do if their measure were cut.

**Bellamy against the centre.** Wont Bellamy is the only elder with friends beyond the Rim, and the only one who has ever argued, out loud, in the bare room, that the Unfed should eat. He cannot carry the council. He does not need to. He needs only one more bad winter, and Stint Avery's arithmetic has given him one.

**The god under everything.** None of the Fast's power-holders controls Orrum, and all of them are shaped by it: the Pantry exists so that no one need accept; the list exists because there is not enough; the Reckoners exist because refusing was not enough; the Hostwatch exist because someone must know where it is. If Orrum stopped laying tables tomorrow, every institution in the Fast would lose its reason. The Unfed do not think about this. The Hostwatch think about it all the time.

[box: The Shape of the Winter]
This winter, four forces are moving toward one point. The Pantry has four months of grain for six. The Company shorted the autumn deliveries and holds a clause on the Unfed dead. The pilgrims at Brindle Cross number in the hundreds and are coming in anyway. And Orrum's tables are now laid with one more place than there are guests. When the snow closes the road to Avery Steading, Stint Avery's list will be read, Forbear Wend will have signed it or died, Wont Bellamy will make his proposal to the council, and Scant Hollis will be somewhere beyond the Rim with a knife. GMs: every character in the following dossiers stands somewhere on this map. Ask, for each, what they will do on the night the list is read.
[/box]

#### Who They Are

He weighs, by Mother Stint Avery's scales, sixty-one pounds. He is carried everywhere in a plain ash chair with long poles, by two boys of the Measure-Bearers who are changed every season so that none of them grows attached. His knees are under a grey blanket of Avery Steading wool. His hands rest on the iron key that hangs at his breast, and they are so thin that the key looks heavy on them, like a stone on a leaf. His skull is a lamp-shade of yellowed skin, translucent at the temples, the veins showing blue. His eyes are perfectly clear.

His voice is soft and dry and absolutely distinct. He never raises it; he does not have the breath. He speaks in short sentences and waits between them, and the waiting is part of the sentence. He smells of camphor, old paper, and the pear-drop sourness of a body that has been eating itself for eighty years. When he is set down, the boys must lift him to his feet and hold him there, one under each arm, because the Chair of the Abstinent does not sit in council, and they stand like that for hours, and the boys' arms shake and his never do.

#### Their Story

Forbear Wend was born in 555 in Hillward, to a family that has held the second stretch of the trench since the Stand-Roll began. He was named on the day he refused his mother's breast, at five months, which Hillward still tells as a story about his promise. He was a Measure-Bearer at twelve, a clerk at twenty, a Lister at thirty, and married at thirty-one to a quiet woman named Keep Hollin, who gave him one daughter, Lenten, in 580.

The Fast calls every winter of choosing a *short*, after the Great Short of 511, and in 589 there was a short: a Company dispute, a bad road, three months of half-measures for half the town. Wend was a Lister. It was not his list. The Keeper of that year drew it, and Lenten's name was not on it, because she had been entered in the ledgers under her mother's family after Keep Hollin died, and the Keeper missed the line. Wend saw the omission on the second day. He said nothing, because a Lister who adds his own child's name to a list is a Lister who will never be Chair, and because the list is the list. He carried the measures to the hamlets that week. He carried them past his own door. Lenten was nine. She lasted a month. He counts her among his eleven, though she was not his, because she is the one he could have saved by speaking.

He was elected Chair in 619, at sixty-four. On the night of his election he read the Company contract, alone, in the top room of the Pantry. He has not slept a night through since. In twenty-two years as Chair he has struck ten names with his own pen, all in hard winters, all after praying, and he keeps them in a small ledger of his own with Lenten's name at the head.

#### Their Place in the Land

Wend is the Fast. He holds the last of the Pantry's three keys and signs every list before it is read. He chairs every Standing. He alone has read the contract under which the Company feeds the Fast. The other elders defer to him not because he is powerful but because he is so evidently suffering in the job, and suffering, in the Fast, is authority.

His routine never varies. He is carried down Hillward Row at dawn, while the street stands against the walls. He spends the morning in the top room, standing, reading the ledgers aloud to a clerk who checks them. At noon he eats his measure, standing, facing the wall, and it takes him an hour because he can only swallow a little at a time. In the afternoon he hears Listers and petitioners. At dusk he is carried to the iron door to hear the list read. At night he stands, held up, and prays the eleven names, one by one, and after each he says the Decline, and that takes him until midnight.

His power has one limit, and it is his body. He is dying, slowly, of the same thing everyone in the Fast is dying of, and everyone knows that when he dies the Chair will pass to whoever the council chooses, and that the council is split.

#### What They Carry

- **The last key** — iron, as long as his hand, on a cord of plaited ration-cloth. One of three to the Pantry door, and the only one that opens the inner store and the box where the contract is kept.
- **The Company contract** — twelve leaves of Oathen vellum, sworn before Jessamy Quill, in a locked box in the top room. He has read it once. He could recite it.
- **The Ledger of Eleven** — a palm-sized book of grey paper, in which he has written eleven names, each with the date and a single line about the person. Lenten's line says *she liked to be carried*.
- **A child's knitted mitten** — red, which is to say once dyed, which is to say vanity, and worn to threads. He keeps it inside his shirt against the skin. Nobody has seen it.
- **His stand-stone** — a chip of chalk from the second stretch, which the boys set on the floor for him to touch with a bare foot before he is carried out.
- **A ration-cloth folded in eight** — his burial cloth. He folds and refolds it each night. He has noticed, lately, that he has been folding it into the shape of a napkin, and does not know where he learned it.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

He wants to sign the winter list before he dies, because if he dies first, Stint Avery will sign it, and she will not pray. He wants to be forgiven, and there is no one alive with the standing to do it.

**A secret the GM can reveal:** he knows the Company contract's clause granting it "first refusal" on any Unfed who dies outside the Pantry's care, and he knows what the Company does with bodies. He has said nothing for twenty-two years, because if the Fast knew, it would refuse the grain, and starve to the last child, cleanly.

**A secret he does not know:** Lenten did not die in the Short of 589. She walked west in the last week, toward a light, and sat, and she is at the Long Table, a little over four miles from Wax Halt, fifty-two years at table, and she is nine years old, and she has not grown.

#### Ties

- **Mother Stint Avery** — his Keeper and his opposite; he trusts her arithmetic and dreads her calm. She has not told him what is on the winter list.
- **Wont Bellamy** — the elder who will try to take the Chair; Wend knows Bellamy is too well fed and has never said it aloud.
- **Scant Hollis** — he has never approved a raid and never condemned one, and every night he prays for the lines she ends, which she does not know.
- **Factor Joss Mardle** — the Company's man; they meet four times a year and never shake hands.
- **Withy Gaunt** — she sends him the names from the Lean House each week; he copies them into his prayers.
- **Welcome Ardent** — Wend met him once, at the Empty Chair, sixty years ago, and was offered a fourth time, and refused it, and the Steward thanked him.

#### In Play

PCs meet Wend when they are summoned to the top room of the Pantry to carry a list to an outlying hamlet, unread, sealed. He chooses strangers because strangers will not open it. He wants them to deliver it and come back and tell him the faces of the people who were not on it, because he has started to forget faces, and he needs them for his prayers.

- **The Sealed List.** The list for Avery Steading is sealed with his own signet, and it is blank. He wants the PCs to watch who comes to the steading stone, and choose.
- **The Clause.** A PC who reads the contract over his shoulder learns about the first refusal. Wend asks them, very quietly, whether the Fast should be told.
- **The Mitten.** Hollow Ann Pruett has found a place card at the Long Table with *Lenten Wend* on it in her mother's hand. Someone must decide whether to tell the Chair.

Faced with threat, he does nothing; his bearers are boys. Faced with kindness, he declines three times and, on the fourth, weeps silently and accepts a hand to hold. Faced with the god, he says the Decline, and his voice does not change. If nobody intervenes, he dies on the night of the winter list, standing, after signing it, and the boys hold him up for an hour before anyone notices.

#### Who They Are

She is tall for an Unfed woman, and looks taller because there is nothing on her: a frame of knots and cords, the tendons standing out at her wrists like rope, the hair cropped to grey stubble with her own knife. Forty raids' worth of Rim weather have burned her face to the colour of a saddle. She dresses in undyed, fatless Reckoner greys and soft-soled boots, and she smells of nothing, deliberately: she washes in cold water with no soap and wears no wool that has touched a sheep's grease.

She speaks rarely and flatly, in a low voice that does not carry, and she listens with her head slightly down, like someone listening at a door. She looks at people the way a farmer looks at a field: what grows here, and should it. Reckoners love her. Everyone else in Wanting steps aside when she walks Knife Row, and she has noticed, and it does not trouble her.

#### Their Story

Scant was born in 597 in Thinwick. When she was six, poachers came through the hamlet in the night and took her mother, Tally Hollis, and two others, from their beds. Her father reported it, as the standing order requires, and her mother's name was struck from the list as dead. Scant watched him do it. She has not spoken to him since, and he has been dead for twenty years.

She went to the West Gate on the first day of Carving in 615, at eighteen, and carried on her first raid that winter: a Lung-line house in Lowmark. She did not look away at the bedsides, and was given a knife. She was a Band-Captain by twenty-six. She became Reckoner-Captain in 633, when old Captain Abate Corran did not come home from Oathen. Since then she has set the season's lines and led most of the hardest raids herself. She has crossed the Rim forty times and ended nine High Cut bloodlines, from matriarchs to cradles.

The raid that changed her was the Marrable raid of 637, against a cadet branch of the Marrable Gut-lines at a summer house in Low Tilth. The matriarch could not be killed, being rooted and three-quarters hill; the band ended the cadets instead. In the nursery Scant stood over a sleeping child for a full minute. What she felt was not mercy. It was the bed. It was warm and deep and smelled of milk and good bread digested, and she wanted, more than she had ever wanted anything, to lie down in it. Then she did what she had come to do. She does not talk about the minute.

#### Their Place in the Land

Scant sets the lines each season, with her Kin-Reader, Scarce Odell, and she chooses the Knives. Every Knife on raid draws a full measure, and so the Reckoners are, in effect, the Fast's only standing army, answerable to nobody. She has no official seat with the Abstinent, but she stands outside the door of the top room on council days, and the elders have never asked her to leave. Knife Row is hers. The young of Wanting go to the West Gate because of her.

Her power ends at the Frostline. She has no authority over the Pantry, and if the Abstinent ever cut the Reckoners' measure, she could not feed her Knives for a week. She has never tested it.

#### What They Carry

- **The black-iron knife** — long, guardless, never oiled with fat, the handle wrapped in ration-cloth. It was Captain Corran's. It is the only thing she owns that she did not make.
- **A garrotte of twisted gut** — for the rooms where a knife would wake someone.
- **Scarce Odell's lists** — folded tight, in oilcloth, in her boot: the current season's lines, every member, every cousin, every wet-nurse's address.
- **A ration bag** — her own food for the road, counted, never shared.
- **A servant's apron** — linen, stained, Fatlands cut. She wears it on the Rim to pass through kitchens. She also wears it because her mother, if alive, would be wearing one.
- **Nine pebbles** — one from the garden of each house she has ended. She says they are not trophies. They are kept in a cloth and she has never once taken them out.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

She wants every High Cut line on the Table ended before the Seating, and she has done the arithmetic and knows it cannot be done. She fears bedside hunger, which she has felt once and will feel again, and she fears what she will do the next time she stands still.

**A secret the GM can reveal:** on every raid she has looked, in every High Cut household, for a woman with no godsign working as a servant: her mother. In the Marrable house there was one, an old Unfed woman in the scullery, who looked at Scant across the dark kitchen and did not cry out, and Scant did not stop to ask her name. She has thought about it every night since.

**A secret she does not know:** Scarce Odell has added the name *Hollis* to a list. Scant's maternal grandmother was a High Cut foundling, Gut-line, left at Thinwick as an infant in 540; Scant carries Cut she cannot feel. Odell has not decided what to do.

#### Ties

- **Scarce Odell** — her Kin-Reader, whose lists she trusts absolutely.
- **Wont Bellamy** — she suspects him of being too well fed; she has not yet found out why.
- **Scour, the Returned** — her best Knife, and she has seen his forearms, and she has let him keep raiding, because she understands wanting to die clean.
- **Decline Marsh** — they grew up two doors apart in Thinwick; he is the only person who calls her by her childhood name.
- **Forbear Wend** — she despises the Abstinent's silence and needs it.
- **Kale Dragoman** — she has been hunting him for three years. He took a woman from Thinwick in 603.

#### In Play

- **The Scullery.** She asks the PCs to go back into the Marrable house, without her, and find an old Unfed servant, and ask her name.

#### Who They Are

She is small and colourless as skimmed milk: pale hair, pale lashes, pale eyes almost without a ring to the iris, skin that has never browned. She tilts her head when people talk, as though listening to a conversation in the next room. She is not thin, by Unfed standards, and has never been, which nobody can explain, since she eats only her measure and often less, because it tastes of nothing and she forgets.

She speaks softly and rarely, in complete sentences, with the faintly formal courtesy of someone who learned manners at a very good table. She smells of clean linen and, very faintly, of a candle just blown out. When she touches someone's hand, it is warm, warmer than an Unfed hand should be.

#### Their Story

Lent was born in 618 in Bell Ward, the youngest of four. In the Carving of 627, aged nine, she followed the smell of custard out past the Frostline while her mother was counting her brothers through the door, and found a table laid under an apple tree in a kept orchard, with a little chair pulled out. She sat. For a year she was Seated, in the First Course, flushed and laughing and eating with both hands, and her mother came every day and stood at the orchard gate and rang the bell.

On the first day of Carving 628 she got up. Nobody saw it. Her mother found her at the gate, standing, holding the bell-rope, with her face wet. She was not wasted; she had not lost a pound. She has never been able to say why or how. She remembers only that the food was perfect, and that the host was crying, and that she turned round in her chair to see.

She could taste nothing afterward. That night, a table was laid at the foot of her pallet, and every night since. The Abstinent took her from her family at eleven, for Bell Ward's sake, and she has been moved ever since, a night here, a night there, followed by a Tablebreaker with an axe, to keep the tables from settling in any one house. She has not seen her mother since 631. Children are not allowed near her.

#### Their Place in the Land

Lent belongs to the Abstinent and is studied by the Hostwatch, who send a Tracker to sit up with her once a month to watch the table arrive (no one has ever seen it arrive; one blinks, and it is there). She has no work, because no household will keep her more than a night. She draws a full measure, which she gives back. She is a Wanting legend, a Bell Ward bogey and a living proof of something that nobody can agree on: that the Host can be left, or that it lets go of those it loves.

#### What They Carry

- **The cards** — thirteen years of place cards, one from every table laid for her, in a bundle tied with string: nearly five thousand. Every one says *Lent*. Every one, until this month.
- **A bone spoon** — from the orchard table; the only thing she brought back. She has never used it. It is always warm.
- **A gauze blindfold** — a Breaker's, so that she does not have to see the morning table.
- **Fifty feet of belled Breaker's rope** — tied to her ankle at night by whoever is watching her.
- **A ration-cloth of salt** — she licks it, because salt is the one thing she can almost feel on her tongue, as a sting.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

She wants to be told why she was let go, and she has begun to suspect she was not, and that she is on a long leash. She wants to taste one thing before she dies, and she fears, more than anything, that the one thing will be on a table.

**A secret the GM can reveal:** the card has changed. For thirteen years it said *Lent*. Since the first night of this Carving it has said *Steward*. She has told no one. She has burned each one. The next morning it is on the table again.

**A secret she does not know:** the tables laid for her have always had two places, not one. The second is laid at the far end, pushed in, so that the Breakers who smash them each dawn have never thought to count.

#### Who They Are

Square and hard-handed, with a mason's forearms and a clerk's ink-stained fingers, oat-dust always in the hairs of her arms. She does not smile, and she does not frown.

#### Their Story

Born in 579 in Avery Steading, she walked into Wanting at twelve with a Measure-Bearer and never went home. She was a weighing-clerk at sixteen and Keeper at forty-eight. She has weighed every ration in the Fast herself for fourteen years, on brass scales older than Wanting, and never once given anyone a grain extra. In the short of 589 she was ten, a clerk's runner, and saw the Keeper miss a line.

#### Their Place in the Land

She holds the second key and drafts the list that Forbear Wend signs. She reads it each night in Pantry Close. Every Lister, Bearer and clerk answers to her scales. She has done the arithmetic of this winter and decided: the furthest villages, the oldest, the fewest children. She has not told Wend, because he will pray, and praying is a delay, and delay kills more.

#### Who They Are

Bird-boned and quick, with a gap in her front teeth, straw-coloured hair cut short with a knife, and the downy fur of hunger along her forearms. She asks *why* the way other children breathe. She is solemn, polite, very good, and curious in a country that has made a whole religion of not being curious. She smells of wet chalk from the stream and, at night, faintly, of honey.

#### Their Story

Born in 630 in Thinwick, named on the day she refused the breast. Her father went with the Reckoners in 637 and did not come home. In the spring of 638 she was the girl who found the first place card, *Abstain*, and under it her dead grandmother's pet name for her, which no living person knew. Since the Carving of 640 she has left a crust on her windowsill each night, saved from her own measure. Each morning it has been replaced: a corner of white bread, a ripe plum, a sugared almond, a honey-cake in a leaf. She has eaten none of it. She hides it under a loose floor-stone. It does not rot.

#### Their Place in the Land

She is a child on a three-quarter measure in a hamlet on the edge of the Frostline, and she should have no place at all. But Abby is Thinwick's best reciter, and the Rule-stone's pride, and she walks two hours to Saints' Acre every fourth day to ask Sister Nay questions. Her mother works double turns in the Cold Yards at Wanting since her name was moved to the bottom of the list, and does not check the sill.

#### What They Carry

- **The crust** — tonight's, saved, in her pocket.
- **The hoard** — under the floor-stone: forty-one things, each better than the last. It smells wonderful through the stone at night.
- **A bone toy** — a carved rabbit, her one toy.
- **The bell** — her mother's door-bell, which Abby rings herself at dusk now, counting one.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

She wants to know who her pen-friend is. She wants Mam to stay on the list. She is not afraid. She thinks it is a friendship.

**A secret the GM can reveal:** her mother is on Stint Avery's winter list. Abby has worked out, from what Measure-Bearers do not say, that this means Mam will go to the Lean House, and she has decided that when it happens she will give Mam the hoard, all of it, because it is not accepting if you are only keeping it for someone else.

**A secret she does not know:** the last gift was a little cake with seven points pressed into the icing in a ring, and the eighth place smooth, as though a thumb had been laid there. Haft-Mender Lean Coyle saw it on the sill before she did, and smelled his dead wife's baking, and took it, and has not given it back.

#### Ties

#### Who They Are

He was a stout man. Now he is a draped grey coat with a head on it, the cheeks hollowed to the teeth, the eyes sunk into blue pits that catch the table-lamp only from below, a few white hairs combed carefully across a skull spotted like an egg. His hands are knuckle and tendon, and they lift the silver fork with a delicacy that would grace a Lastgate salon, and put the nothing in his mouth, and he chews, slowly, with every appearance of delight. His voice is dry leaves, warm and full of laughter. His breath smells of acetone and, beneath it, of ripe pears. He is perfectly happy and perfectly sane.

#### Their Story

Hallow Crust was born in 566 in the Spit, with one of the old shameful names, and went to Splinterhall at fifteen to cleanse it. He broke two hundred and forty tables in twenty years and was an Old Breaker at thirty-four, called Brother by his band, as Old Breakers were in his day. He taught a generation the order of breaking: lamp, plates, legs, top, chairs last. He married Meagre Lowe of Hillward in 594.

On the 9th of Carving, 601, on the Long Table road, at the fourth mile, he stepped out of the string, unroped, with his axe, and sat down in an empty chair. He had told nobody. He had decided, after twenty years, to find out if a man could sit and get up again, by will, so the Breakers would know. He has been meaning to get up for forty years. After this course.

#### Their Place in the Land

He is one of eleven thousand on the Long Table, and the most famous, because he talks. Runners trot past with wax in their ears. Measure-Bearers on the crossing road are taught to look straight ahead at the fourth mile. Splinterhall still lists him as an Old Breaker; his name is spoken on Tablenight with the living. He is a warning, a landmark, and, for those who listen too long, a host: people who stop to hear him describe a course pull out a chair.

#### What They Carry

- **A silver fork** — Orrum's, from the table. He holds it like a pen.
- **An axe-wedge** — his own, under the chair, where he dropped it on the first day. He has never looked down at it.
- **His Breaker's tally** — a knotted cord at his belt, two hundred and forty knots. He thinks it is a list of dishes.
- **A napkin** — tucked in his collar, white and pressed, changed every day by nobody.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

He wants to get up, after this course, and go home and tell Splinterhall that it can be done. He wants someone to tell him his wife's name, which he cannot remember, though he remembers every dish he has been served since 601.

**A secret the GM can reveal:** he sat down on purpose, as a test, and has known for forty years, in some back room of himself, that he failed it. When he is very tired, in the third hour after midnight, he weeps, and goes on eating.

**A secret he does not know:** his wife, Meagre Crust, is seventy-one and alive in Hillward, and walks the Long Table road once a year on the 9th of Carving, roped, plugged, blindfolded, to stand opposite him for one minute. He has asked her each year, kindly, if she would like to join him. He has never once recognised her.

#### Ties

- **Decline Marsh** — his pupil; Decline passes him on the road and never stops, and Hallow calls after him, *Lamp, plates, legs, top, chairs, lad!*
- **Lent Ashby** — she sat near him for an hour; he told her what she ate in the orchard, course by course.
- **Meagre Crust** — his wife, whom he does not know.
- **Old Breaker Wane Hollister** — trained by him; she is sleeping on the splinter heap now, and he would understand.
- **Welcome Ardent** — the Steward changes his napkin, and Hallow thanks him every morning.

#### In Play

PCs meet him on the Long Table road. He calls them by name before they reach him.

- **The Test.** He asks a PC to take his hand and pull, gently, just to see. Beyond the cloth he will die.
- **The Wife.** Meagre Crust asks the PCs to tell him her name, once, so he will know.
- **The Order of Breaking.** A Breaker band's new Axe has stopped to listen, and the band needs someone to go and fetch him back.

Threat he does not notice. Kindness he returns with interest and a menu. To the god, he is grateful, and says so, every course. If nobody intervenes, he keeps eating, and in another century he will be a coat and a smile, and when the living pass he will turn with the others, with a sound like reeds, and look.
