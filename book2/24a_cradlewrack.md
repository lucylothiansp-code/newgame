# Cradlewrack: The Country Between Pangs

> Count with me. Not because it helps. Because it is worse alone.
>> — what a highland mother says to a child in false labor

> Nine days is long enough to mend a shirt, wean a lamb, and forget. It is not long enough to stop listening.
>> — Lark Ninedays, to the Tally clerks

[fiction]
A day of Una Knocker, slot-feeder at the Debt Wall in the Stillyard of Kest, as she told it to the Attendance's clerk who came asking what the common people do with their nine days. Taken down in Carving 641, the fourth day after a pang.
I wake in the brick. Everyone in my row does, the ones who can pay the four crumbs. It is black and close and smells of my own breath and the lime in the mortar, and the first thing you do, before you know your own name, is put your hand flat on the wall and feel whether it is warm. Ours was cool this morning. That is a good morning.
Then you wait for the trowel. Old Gage's boy comes down the row at first bell with his pick, and you hear him at the neighbors, tap and crumble, tap and crumble, the bricks coming out one at a time and stacked on the step so they can go back at dusk. When it is your turn he knocks three times first. You knock back three. That is how he knows you are you. If you knock back four, he goes for the Attendance and nobody opens your room till they come. I have never knocked four. My sister's husband did, once, and they say he never knew he'd done it.
The air when the brick comes out is like cold water on the face. You can smell the coppers already, the lye and the boiled aprons, and the mutton fat from the Shambles, and under it the springs, that salt-and-cheek smell that is the smell of home to anybody born here and makes outlanders gag. I knock on my children's walls. Three each. They knock back. I count them as they come out, every morning, three heads, three pairs of hands, and I look at the hands, because that is what you look at.
Then the door. Our door is a curtain of hide on a pole, like everyone's on the Wall Road, because doors on hinges walk open in the night and a curtain at least does not pretend. I knock on it from the inside before I go out, because you never know what has come and stood on the other side of it while you slept.
My work is the slots. The Debt Wall is half a mile of brick on the north slope, and in it are the bricked, the murderers and the forsworn, each in a niche no wider than a coffin with a slot at the height of a mouth. The families pay me a crumb a day each to push bread and a horn of water through, and to listen, and to tell them what I hear. I have forty-one slots. Some of them talk. Some pray. One sings the counting-song all day, from one to sixty and back to one. One has not answered in two years and still takes the bread. I do not ask how. The family pays, and the bread goes, and that is all the Wall wants to know.
At noon I eat clay-bread on the Wall step. Mutton fat, rose salt. The Barren come through about then, grey robes, singing the struck-through names, and we all go quiet while they pass, even the slots.
The pang came at the third bell. You know it before it comes. Every Course-touched soul in the city cries out together a breath before the ground moves, like the city drawing in air, and then it rolls up out of the hill under you, long and low. The Wall groans. Mortar dust sifts out of every seam. Inside the niches the bricked start screaming, all of them at once, because a bricked man feels a pang in the brick as well as in his body, and for sixty counts I sit on the step with my hands over my ears and count, out loud, because they can hear me counting and it helps them. It does. The singing one counts with me. At sixty it stops. It always stops at sixty. The ground goes still and you are always surprised you are still there.
After, the waters. The gutters ran warm and pink down the slope and carried things in them, the way they do. A child's shoe. A great many earthworms. A key nobody owned. I washed my feet in it, which my mother would have slapped me for, but it was warm, and it is Carving.
At dusk the crier at the Tally-board on the Wall Road reads the day. Fourteen births in our quarter. Nine stay. One hand, surrendered. Two struck through. Two "entered without bearer," which is what they say now when there is somebody new in the city and no one carried them. Then the crier says "nine days," and we all say it back, "nine days," and go home.
The trowel comes at second dark. I knock my children in, three each. I count them. I always count them. Then Gage's boy bricks me, and I lie in the dark that smells of lime and breath, and I put my hand on the wall to see if it is warm, and I listen. That is all I do, really. All day. I listen. Write that down for the Attendance. They should know that the whole country is listening.
[/fiction]

Cradlewrack is the land of the Two Questions, the knife, the crowning ground and the god being born out of the earth. Those things are true, and the core of this book has told them. This chapter is about the other things: the price of a night's brick, the way a Rudge clay-digger greets her neighbor, what a shepherd in Tarnbrow pays for the right to name a child who is not yet a child, how the Attendance tries a man for opening a door, and what the slot-feeders hear. A country in labor still has to eat, and wash, and sell its mutton, and brick its children in at night. It does so in nine-day stretches now, the way a woman in a long labor learns to live in the gaps between pains, and it does so with a terrible, admirable ordinariness that is the real horror of the highlands. Nobody in Cradlewrack is surprised any more. They are only counting.

## Districts and Settlements

### Kest, the City of Seven Hills

Kest is built on seven rounded red hills that rise from the valley of the Tull like the knuckles of a buried hand, with the Rim Road running through the valley floor at their feet. No street in Kest is straight and no wall meets another at a corner; the Attendance holds that corners are where things collect, and so the city flows around its hills in curves, ramps and spiralling lanes that strangers lose themselves in for days. Every building is of the red clay, fired or rammed, rounded and windowless on the weather side, so that from the road below the city looks like a vast heap of eggs set out to warm. Smoke rises from every hill. On still mornings the caul-fog lies in the valley to the height of the second storey, and the hills stand up out of it like islands.

There are perhaps sixty thousand people in Kest, though the Tally cannot agree with itself, and the count is complicated by the bricked, the struck-through, the entered-without-bearer and the born-grown who walk in from the hills and ask to be put on the list. Each of the seven hills is a quarter, and two lower districts fill the valley between them.

#### The Round

The central and highest hill, crowned by the Old Lying-In, the birthing-house of the Meal: a squat round building of red brick older than the Gorging, with eight doors in its circumference, none of which has closed since that night. The Round is the Attendance's hill. Here are the Senior houses, the Book-room where the six hundred pages of the Book of Questions are kept under glass with a midwife always reading them, the apprentices' dormitory called the Aprons, and the Hall of the Lying where descent is judged. The streets are paved with fired tiles that the midwives scrub each morning. It smells of lavender, lye, and the cold iron of the knife-cases. It is quiet: nobody on the Round shouts, because Orla Kest does not, and nobody wants to be the one who does. The great custom of the Round is the **Touching of the Arches**: every person who enters the Old Lying-In touches the seven worn figures over its door, in order, left to right, and turns their face from the eighth. The danger of the Round is the Round itself: an outsider who is found to be carrying will be attended, whatever they wish, and the doors of the Old Lying-In have a way of being the only open way out of a crowd.

#### Tally Hill

The commercial heart of Kest and the hill of numbers. The Tally-house sits on its crown, a long curved hall of white-limed clay where Rufus Ochre's scriveners copy the day's births from the parish boards into the great public ledger, in a scratching hush like rain. Lark Ninedays keeps her water clock in the bare room on its roof, and her bell is the loudest thing in the city. Beneath the Tally-house, down a ramp, is the **Naming-Exchange**, a low vaulted cellar where naming-rights are bought and sold by the hour by brokers who shout prices in a cant of their own ("a Rudge boy at forty, a Cleave girl at twelve, a voice-born at ninety, any takers for a Tarnbrow unknown"). Ruth Ninefold has her stall at the foot of the ramp. The hill smells of ink, lamp-oil and wet wool, and in the Exchange of sweat and spilt posset. Its custom is **the Dusk Reading**: at the last bell of every day a crier on the steps of the Tally-house reads the whole city's births aloud, and the crowd on the steps answers each "stays" with a murmur and each "struck through" with silence. Its danger is the Exchange: a family that sells a right to name a child it has not yet borne has sold the child's place in the world, and brokers have been found in the Tull with their ledgers sewn into their mouths.

[quick] Corin Ledge, Exchange crier — Threat 3 · Flesh 10 · Guard 11 · Attack +3 (knife 2) · Armor 0 · Dread 0 · a hoarse man of forty who calls the opening price of every right; he keeps a private book of which children he has sold and visits them on their naming-days with a sugar-stick.

#### The Coppers

The laundry quarter, on the steep south hill, where the aprons of the Attendance and the linen of the whole city are boiled. A hundred great copper vats stand in open yards under lean-to roofs, fires burning beneath them day and night, tended by laundresses with forearms red and shining from the lye. The steam rises in a column visible from the Rim Road. The smell is overwhelming: wet linen, lye, wood-smoke, and the sweet copper of boiled blood, because most of what is washed in the Coppers comes out of birthing-rooms and reopening-wards. The laundresses sing at work in long call-and-answer songs to keep the rhythm of the paddles. Their custom is **the Rinse**: an apron that has gone into a room where the Second Opinion was given is boiled three times, not once, and the third water is poured out on the hill, never down the drain, so that it goes into the ground and not the river. Their danger is the steam: the Coppers' fog is thick enough that the born-grown sometimes walk out of it, naked and new, having been born of a vat in the night. The laundresses have a word for these, "boilings," and a rule: give them an apron and walk them to the Crèche.

[quick] Big Clemency Furrow, Mistress of the Coppers — Threat 3 · Flesh 15 · Guard 12 · Attack +5 (laundry paddle 3, Stunning) · Armor 0 · Dread 0 · a vast, laughing woman whose arms are scarred white from sixty years of lye; she has fished nineteen boilings out of her vats and named every one.

#### Mortar Rise

The masons' hill, on the west, where the brick-kilns stand in rows like beehives and the air shimmers above them. Here live the Trowel, the masons' guild, who brick the dead and the condemned and, nightly, the cautious of the whole city. Mortar Rise is wealthy in the way of a trade everybody needs: masons' houses have real glass, and their wives wear madder-red silk sewn on each morning. The hill smells of hot clay, lime and the sharp tang of the burnt bone the masons mix into their best mortar. The sound is the tap of trowels, which never stops, because somewhere in Kest there is always someone who wants to be shut in. The custom of Mortar Rise is **the Listening Fee**: a mason who lays a course of brick across a living person's room must, by guild rule, put his ear to it for a full count of sixty before he walks away, and if he hears anything but breathing he must break it open again, without charge. The danger is that masons talk, to each other and only to each other, and the Trowel knows exactly who in Kest sleeps where and with whom and behind how many courses; the guild sells that knowledge, very occasionally, very dearly.

[quick] Master Hugh Gage, Warden of the Trowel — Threat 4 · Flesh 13 · Guard 12 · Attack +5 (mason's hammer 3) · Armor 1 · Dread 0 · Absalom Gage's younger brother, fifty-four, white-handed with lime; he runs the guild while his deaf brother runs the Stillyard, and he is very much afraid of the knocking his brother hears.

#### The Stillyard

The north slope of Kest, given over to the dead: the concentric rings of long red walls, each lined with niches where the dead are mortared in behind family-stamped bricks. Absalom Gage is its chief mason. The Stillyard is not a district where people live, except for the slot-feeders and the mourners, who in practice live there, on the steps under the Debt Wall and the family walls, in a shanty-quarter of hide tents called the **Wall Road**. It smells of lime, old wax, cold clay, and, along the Debt Wall, of the living condemned, which is the smell of a sickroom that has been shut for years. The custom of the Stillyard is **the Three Knocks**: mourners knock three times on a new brick and walk away without waiting. The danger is that walls knock back. When one does, the family is fetched, and then the Attendance, and while they wait, the family sits beneath the brick and argues, in low voices, about whether to answer. Some answer. The Attendance has a word for the people who do, and it is not a kind one.

[quick] Una Knocker, slot-feeder — Threat 2 · Flesh 11 · Guard 11 · Attack +2 (knife 2) · Armor 0 · Dread 0 · forty, three children, forty-one slots on the Debt Wall; she knows the voices of all her bricked, and she has noticed that the one who has not answered in two years has begun, at night, to tap back the interval.

#### Seam Street

The stitchers' quarter, a long curved lane down the east shoulder of Tally Hill, lined on both sides with the shops of menders, cord-thread spinners, needle-grinders and catgut-makers, and at its foot the Reopening Ward, where Tamsin Gorse and her stitchers close each morning what the night opened. On the morning after a pang the queue for the Ward reaches the top of the street: old Foundling soldiers bleeding from every wound they ever took, Barren brothers holding their robes closed over their renewed cuttings, women cut for stones, children who fell on a hay-hook five years ago and are bleeding again, all of them standing quietly in line with their hands pressed to their bodies. The street smells of spirit, catgut, wax and blood. Its custom is **the Stitch-Count**: every person in Cradlewrack knows how many stitches they carry, and on Seam Street it is said as a greeting ("Morning, forty-one"), and it is bad manners to ask about a number that has gone up. The danger is the catgut trade itself: the best thread is from sheep, the cheapest is from anything, and a cheap stitch in Cradlewrack sometimes heals into the wound and grows there, and has to be cut out with the flesh around it.

[quick] Old Pell Twine, catgut-spinner — Threat 2 · Flesh 10 · Guard 10 · Attack +2 (spinning-hook 1) · Armor 0 · Dread 0 · a stooped man with fingers like his own thread who sells the finest gut in Kest; he will not say what the very finest is spun from, and his prices for it are oddly low after a crowning.

#### The Shambles

The mutton market and slaughter-yards in the valley under the Coppers, where the highland flocks come down the drove-roads to be killed, jointed and sold. Lamb in Cradlewrack is the cheapest meat on the Table, because the ewes drop endlessly; the Shambles slaughters a thousand head a day in Plenty and still cannot keep up, and the gutters run so red that the Tull below them is called the Pink Water. The district smells of blood, wool-grease, dung and roasting fat, and it is loud: the bleating of the penned, the thud of the poleaxes, the shouts of the drovers, the hiss of fat on the brazier-stalls where a lack buys a whole roast shoulder. The custom of the Shambles is **the Count of the Lambs**: every ewe that comes in is checked for what she carries, and anything she carries that is not a lamb goes to a pen behind the slaughter-yard called the Odd Fold, where the Attendance's agents and the Assemblers' gatherers bid for it in silence. The danger is the Odd Fold itself. Some of what is in it has hands.

[quick] Gerent Fold, master of the Odd Fold — Threat 3 · Flesh 14 · Guard 13 · Attack +5 (poleaxe 5) · Armor 1 · Dread 0 · a broad drover who takes coin from both sides and sleeps very well; he keeps a tally of his own, scratched on the fold-gate, of the things that were sold out of it and later walked back in.

#### The Crèche

The barracks of the Foundling Companies, on the low eastern hill: a great walled compound of long dormitories, drill-yards and nurseries, because the Crèche is both. On one side of the central yard the six Companies drill in formation, spears flashing in perfect time; on the other is the Cradle-House, where the newly born-grown are taught to eat with a spoon, to dress, to speak in their own voices rather than the half-remembered ones they arrived with, and where the soldiers who have **gone home** to infancy in a night are kept in rows of cots by the drill-mothers until they die or grow. The Crèche smells of porridge, leather, sweat and milk. It sounds of drums, and of crying. Its custom is **the Birthday Roll**: on the first day of each season the names of soldiers who have earned a remembered birthday are read aloud and they are given a cake, and grown men weep at it. Its danger is grief. The born-grown have none of the practice the rest of us build up as children, and when a squad-mate goes home, the squad is sometimes found the next morning in the Cradle-House, all of them, curled in the cots, which are much too small.

[quick] Drill-Mother Rowan Seele, First of the Cradle-House — Threat 4 · Flesh 13 · Guard 14 · Attack +6 (cudgel 3, Stunning) · Armor 1 · Dread 0 · a cast-off Caul-line daughter of forty whose caul came off; she has taught four thousand grown men to tie their boots and she sits up with the ones who have gone home until they stop.

#### Cord Court and the Aubrey Gardens

The two lower districts, side by side in the valley of the Tull, could not be less alike. **Cord Court** is the Kest quarter of the Uncut: a maze of long low joined houses near the river, roofed over by the pale living cords that run from window to window and street to street, so that walking through it is like walking under the branches of a strange warm orchard. It smells of wet skin and woodsmoke and, faintly, of milk. Its custom is the **Pulse-Greeting**: the Uncut greet each other by laying two fingers on each other's cords. Its danger is to the careless outsider who touches a cord and feels, too clearly, the heart at the other end of it stop.

The **Aubrey Gardens**, across the river, are the old royal quarter: the Womb Court, a long low palace of rose-colored brick with no stairs anywhere (the Queen Dowager cannot climb them) and a garden of herbs and lavender where she is carried in the evenings. The Gardens smell of lavender and old women. The custom here is the **Reading**: every morning at the third bell the Lord Chamberlain reads the day's questions to the Queen's belly, in the Long Room, before a small audience of officials and petitioners, and they all wait in silence for the kick. Its danger is the slow, polite poison of any court that knows its king is a pretext, and the Chamberlain who reads the questions, and who has lately begun reading them in the night, alone.

### Other Towns and Villages

#### Rudge

The clay-diggers' parish in the western ravines: a long straggle of egg-shaped red houses down both sides of a steep wet valley, where the clay-pits are cut into the hillside in terraces like the steps of a stair for something very large. Some eight hundred people live here, short, broad, red-handed to the elbow and suspicious of everybody, and they dig the clay that Kest's potters and masons need. The parish smells of wet earth, iron and smoke from the kilns, and it sounds of spades. The well that gave birth in 403 A.G. stands in the square under three tons of fired brick and a millstone, and no child in Rudge will walk on that side of the square. The custom of Rudge is **the Spade-Oath**: clay-diggers swear by the spade and the pit, and any promise made with a hand on a spade-blade is said to be "dug in" and binding as a brick. Rudge records more Parts than any parish in the country, and the danger here is the Part-hunters, who come up the ravine at night with their crates and their questions; the diggers meet them with spades, and some of the hunters are in the pits now. Hester Lowe lives in Rudge. So does most of the Dilation's watch, through Madder Rudge's huge and argumentative family.

[quick] Abel Furrow the Younger, Rudge parish Attendant — Threat 3 · Flesh 12 · Guard 13 · Attack +6 (knife 2) · Armor 0 · Dread 1 · great-grandson of the Abel Furrow who discovered that Parts fit, and the only man ever made a parish Attendant in Rudge; he knows exactly which households are hiding Parts, and he has reported none.

#### Tarnbrow

A sheep-town on the northern moors, the last sizeable place before the Sill, built around a cold dark tarn in a bowl of hills. Its people are shepherds and weavers of the coarse red highland wool, and its houses are more scattered than in the south, each with its lambing-shed and its sheepfold. Tarnbrow smells of sheep, peat-smoke and the cold mineral water of the tarn, which is the only water in Cradlewrack that is not warm. It is a town of odd births even by highland measure: the first man to bear, in 188 A.G., was of Tarnbrow; so was the widow of eighty-seven delivered of twins older than she was. Juniper Dell lives here, and so does the shepherd's widow who keeps Sabeth Thorne's spared thing in her loft. The custom of Tarnbrow is **the Fold-Light**: during lambing every house sets a lamp in the window of the fold, so that whatever is born in the night can see its way to the house and need not wander. The danger is the moor: in the fog between Tarnbrow and the Sill, travelers report a shape walking that is too tall for a man, and tracks the size of a cart. Josiah Fenn passes through every few months.

[quick] Annis Dell, shepherd's widow — Threat 2 · Flesh 12 · Guard 11 · Attack +3 (crook 3) · Armor 0 · Dread 0 · thirty-eight, paid a year's wages by Sabeth Thorne to keep a secret in her loft; she has begun to set out a bowl for it at table and to dread the morning she will see it at the foot of her own bed.

#### Cleave

A market-town in the central hills, between Kest and the Dilation, famous for its lambing-sheds, the largest in the highlands, and for the things that are born in them. The first born-grown, a woman of forty, was delivered at Cleave in 101 A.G., mid-scream; General Tibb was born in a Cleave lambing-shed six years ago, mid-stride; the covered dish in which a born heart was kept for nineteen years still sits in Cleave's parish hall. The town is crowded on market days with drovers, wool-buyers and Rimwatch pilgrims on their way to the Dilation, and it smells of sheep and straw and roasting chestnuts. Its custom is **the Shed-Watch**: during lambing, a born-grown soldier of the Companies stands guard at the door of each great shed, because what comes out grown has a right to be met by someone who understands. The danger is a quieter one. Cleave is where the Crewe sisters were born, and lately a third girl in the town has started to finish their sentences, and other children in the town have started to say things in unison.

[quick] Sergeant-Elder Wick, Cleave shed-guard — Threat 3 · Flesh 14 · Guard 15 · Attack +6 (spear 4) · Armor 2 · Dread 0 · born-grown, four years old in fact, fifty in body; he has met eleven born-grown at the shed door and walked them all to Kest, and he weeps easily and is not ashamed of it.

#### Rimwatch

The ring of tents, turf huts and hostels around the rim of the Dilation, a mile across and growing. It is less a town than a crowd that never went home: pilgrims, Barren preachers, Assemblers' agents, Attendance observers, mothers whose children walked into the hole, souvenir-sellers, posset-sellers, and the painted stakes of the watch. Perhaps two thousand people live in Rimwatch at any time; perhaps half of them were there a year ago. It smells of the hole, warm, humid, iron and birth-water, and of peat-smoke and unwashed pilgrims, and the sound under everything is the slow breath of the warm air rising out of the dark. Its custom is **the Morning Stake**: every dawn the watch sets out painted stakes at the rim to mark its advance, and anyone in Rimwatch may pay a crumb to paint their name on one; when the stake is gone by noon, it is lucky. The danger is the rim, which moves: at each pang the bowl widens by a stride and eases back by less, and every pang someone who was standing at the edge is not there any more. The tents nearest the rim are cheapest.

[quick] Hollan Pyke, posset-seller of the rim — Threat 2 · Flesh 11 · Guard 12 · Attack +3 (ladle 1) · Armor 0 · Dread 0 · a cheerful widow of fifty who sells hot posset from a cart and has moved her pitch back nine times; she claims she has never once seen anything come up, and she is lying.

#### Cordway

The village of the Uncut on the moorland west of Morwen Hall, built of long low houses joined end to end so that cords need never pass through a door, and roofed over with living cord in a vast slow net that sways in the wind and is warm to the touch. Some six hundred Uncut live here, in households of thirty and forty joined by cords, with a few Grafted outsiders among them. Cordway smells of warm skin, peat and milk, and it sounds of a low constant hum, which is not a sound at all but the pulse of six hundred hearts felt through the soles of the feet. Its custom is **the Long Table**: the whole village eats together in the great joined hall, because the cords make it painful to be apart at meals. Its danger is the deep cords. Some of the oldest Uncut have let their cords grow down into the clay, and the ground in Cordway has a pulse of its own; at night the cords that go into the earth tighten, all together, as if something below were drawing them gently down. Sister Maeve Cord's mother lies in a long low tomb here, with a slot left for the cord.

[quick] Old Isolde Rootsister, a Root of the Uncut — Threat 4 · Flesh 12 · Guard 10 · Attack +3 (cord-lash 2, Grappling) · Armor 0 · Dread 2 · ninety, blind, her cord in the ground beneath her chair for thirty years; she has not left the chair in a decade and she says the land's child is a girl.

#### Breech's Gate

The great border post on the Rim Road, at the southern descent where the road leaves the highlands toward the Brinehollow coast and the Fatlands beyond: a walled yard, a customs hall, the Examination Rooms, a Rim Road Company inn, and a straggle of lodging houses outside the wall for the turned-back. The yard is always full of travelers waiting their turn to be examined, and of the turned-back, who wait for a change of heart that never comes and who, after a season or two, become a settled village of their own, called **Hold**. Breech's Gate smells of horses, wool, posset and the vinegar the examiners wash their hands in. Its custom is **the Laying-On**: every traveler leaving the highlands stands against a wall while a midwife lays a palm on their belly and an ear to their navel, and men and women alike are asked the date of their last pang-cramp. Its danger is what is found. More travelers are carrying every week, including men who did not know, and the Examination Rooms have their own small lying-in, and their own knife.

[quick] Examiner Bryony Lintel, Attendant of the Gate — Threat 3 · Flesh 12 · Guard 13 · Attack +6 (knife 2) · Armor 0 · Dread 1 · fifty, with cold hands she apologizes for; she has examined forty thousand bellies, and she has started to feel, in a few of them, a second heartbeat that is not a child's but the land's, and she does not know what to write.

#### Tullford

The river town under Morwen Hall, where the Tull is bridged on the road west to Cordway and the sea. It is a Caul-line town: almost everyone here works for the Morwens in some way, as servant, nurse, forester, spring-keeper or bridge-warden, and the great round keep of Morwen Hall looms over it on its wooded ridge, doorless on its lower floors. Tullford smells of river-water, leaf-mold and the orris-root perfume the Morwen servants wear to cover what they carry home on their clothes. It is quiet, prosperous and tight-lipped. Its custom is **the Nurses' Walk**: once a week, at night, a line of Morwen nurses comes down from the Hall carrying covered baskets, crosses the bridge, and walks a mile west into the woods, and comes back with the baskets empty. Nobody in Tullford watches them pass. Its danger is the woods to the west, where something has been growing, out of what the nurses leave, for three hundred years.

[quick] Tess Bridgeward, Keeper of Tull Bridge — Threat 3 · Flesh 13 · Guard 13 · Attack +5 (boathook 4) · Armor 1 · Dread 0 · sixty, the fourth of her name to keep the bridge; she counts the baskets out and the baskets back, and her ledger shows three hundred years of the Nurses' Walk, and the baskets have lately started coming back heavier.

#### Kilnmouth

A brick-burning town in the south, near Nullhouse, where the clay of the highlands meets the first dry ground toward the Fast border and the kilns can be fired without the steam of the springs. It makes nearly all the fired brick in Cradlewrack: the brick that walls the Stillyard, bricks in the Lying-In's lower wards, caps the Rudge well, and is laid each night across ten thousand bedroom doors. Kilnmouth is all smoke and heat, a town of a hundred kilns burning day and night, its people grey with ash and coughing; it smells of hot clay and charcoal and the bone-meal the brickmakers temper their best mortar with. Its custom is **the Stamp**: every brick that leaves Kilnmouth is stamped with its kiln's mark, and a family that bricks its dead buys bricks from one kiln only, generation after generation, so that the walls of the Stillyard can be read like a book of loyalties. Its danger is that the kilns, too, have begun to bear: twice in the last year a kiln opened at dawn held, among the fired bricks, a fired thing, curled and hard, with a pulse. The Barren Order buys its grey stone through Kilnmouth's carters and is not loved here.

[box: The Pang-Houses]
Along every road in Cradlewrack, at roughly every three miles, stands a **pang-house**: a small round red hut, open-doored, with a sunken floor and walls nine bricks thick. They are maintained by the parishes and free to all. When a pang is near, travelers make for the nearest one, lie flat on its sunken floor, and wait out the sixty counts. Each pang-house has the interval scratched on its lintel, every interval since it was built, in a long column of shortening numbers; the oldest have columns going back three hundred years, and reading them is a quick education in why the highlands are frightened. Pang-houses are also where the born-grown most often appear: the Companies send a soldier on a round of the nearer ones the morning after every pang, to see who is lying there, naked and new, asking what year it is.
[/box]

## Roads and Ways

Nobody in Cradlewrack travels far without counting. The first question a highland traveler asks at an inn is not the price of a bed but "how long since?", and the answer decides everything: whether to push on to the next pang-house, whether to wait out the waters, whether the ford at Tull will be passable or running warm and pink to the banks. A journey of four days that crosses a pang is a different journey from one that does not, and travelers plan around the interval the way sailors plan around the tide.

The **Rim Road** runs through the highlands for about a hundred and sixty miles, entering from Sallowreach in the north at the lesser post called **Fogstile**, passing below the seven hills of Kest, and leaving by Breech's Gate in the south toward the Brinehollow coast. It is a Company road, paved with fitted stone (grey stone, carted in at Company expense, which the Barren Order regards as the only holy thing the Company ever did), and its waystations are Company inns with brick-in rooms and Company guards. It is the one road in the country where the pangs do little harm, because Company engineers relaid it on piles after the Shortening began; it shudders, and the inns empty onto the verges, but it does not crack. Inside the highlands, every other road does.

The **clay roads** are the rest: red tracks pounded hard by sheep and carts, winding along the ridges to avoid the ravines, where the waters gather. They are soft after every pang and set like brick in a dry week, and their ruts are a foot deep in Carving. Every crossroads has a **knocking-stone**, a waist-high red pillar on which travelers knock three times before choosing a way; the stones are hollow, and the hollows ring, and in the fog the sound of knocking carries for miles. Milestones in the highlands do not give miles but pang-houses: "Kest, nine houses." The **drove-roads** are wider and older, grassy ways along which the flocks are walked down to the Shambles, and they are safe in all seasons except lambing, when the ewes drop on the road itself and the drovers will not move until each birth has been looked at.


Travel during a pang is forbidden by every parish and practiced by everyone. The rule is simple: at the first cry of the Course-touched, which runs ahead of the quake like a wind, get off the road, lie down flat away from any slope, and count. After the sixty counts, wait for the waters, which come within the hour, and keep to the ridges. Ravines flood warm to the depth of a man in minutes, and drain as fast, and leave the ground rimed with pink salt and littered with what came up. Drovers who have been caught in a ravine flood say the water is the temperature of a body and that it holds you, gently, the way a hand holds an egg.

## Bread and Coin

The highlands are not poor. They are rich in exactly the things that are born, which is to say, everything: lambs, wool, clay, and people. What is scarce in Cradlewrack is anything that stays shut, anything that is finished, and anything that is certain, and those are what cost money.

**Wool and mutton** are the base of the country. The ewes of the highlands drop three and four times a year now, and a flock doubles in a season; the problem of every shepherd is not breeding but culling, and the drove-roads to the Shambles never empty. Highland wool, coarse and red, goes out along the Rim Road by the wagon-load, and highland mutton, salted with the rose-colored spring-salt, is sold as far as the Vigil, where it is said to keep the sleepless awake. Lamb is so cheap at home that the poor eat it daily; the rich, to show they can, eat fish carried from Brinehollow at ruinous expense.

**Clay and brick** are the second trade. Every building in the country is of red clay, and every night ten thousand doors are bricked and every morning broken out, and the dead go into the walls in their thousands each year. Rudge digs, Kilnmouth fires, and the Trowel lays. Masons are among the best-paid commoners in the land.

**Thread and stitching** is the third. Every wound in Cradlewrack reopens, and so the country consumes catgut, linen thread, needles and wax in quantities that astonish visitors. Stitchers are respected and well paid; spinners of fine gut are rich.

**Springs and salt** belong to the Caul-lines. Every warm spring in the highlands is owned by one of the five veiled houses, which charge spring-rents for water and sell the rose spring-salt that is scraped from the rims of the pools. That, more than prophecy, is why the Veiled Council sits beside the Attendance.

**Cauls** are an export. The Rim Road Company buys highland birth-cauls by the crate at Breech's Gate for sale in Brinehollow, where one sewn into a coat is thought to prevent the Calling, and an ordinary family can sell a child's caul for a season's bread. Lately the Caul-lines have begun selling their own, which are larger, and warmer.

**Names** are speculation. The Naming-Exchange under the Tally-house trades naming-rights by the hour, and fortunes are made and lost on rumors that come down from the birthing-rooms. A right bought for two lacks on a Rudge clay-digger's unborn may sell for four hundred if the child is born whole, speaking, and plainly gifted, and a right bought for forty on a noble's bastard is void the moment the knife falls.

And there is the black trade, larger than any of these: **Parts**. The law says every Part belongs to the Lying-In, and the Assemblers pay better than the law. Part-hunters work the western ravines with crates, mothers sell what they bore for the price of a winter, and a healthy hand fetches sixty lacks in the dark of the Odd Fold. The Attendance seizes what it finds and unhinges the families. It finds very little.

### Taxes

The Attendance takes no tax and needs none: the **birth-fee** of twelve lacks for every attended birth is the largest revenue in the country, and it is paid by every household, since every birth must be attended. Paupers pay in kind, a season of laundry at the Coppers or a year of slot-feeding. The Crown takes the **Litter-Levy**, a crumb a pang from every household, nominally to keep the Queen Dowager carried, actually to keep the Chamberlain's office. The **Hinge-Duty** taxes any lock that holds as a luxury, at half its price, on the reasoning that a man who can afford to be shut in can afford to pay for it; Agnes Latch's customers pay a hundred lacks to the Tally on top of her two hundred. The Caul-lines take **spring-rents** for water. The Rim Road Company takes its tolls at Breech's Gate and Fogstile, and buys its cauls duty-free.


## Law and the Knife

The law of Cradlewrack is the law of the Book of Questions, and the Book is mostly cases: six hundred pages of what was born and what was decided, with the last page in Hollan Kest's hand. Crimes are judged by the same habit of mind. Every hearing in the highlands is in form a birth: the accused is "brought before," the parish Attendant asks two questions aloud, and she answers them herself. The questions in court are *what was done*, and *does it stay*, and the second means: does the offender stay in the parish, in the house, in the world.

### Who Enforces

- **Parish Attendants** are magistrates in their parishes. They hear all lesser matters alone, in the parish birthing-room, sitting on the birthing-stool, with the accused standing in the doorway.
- **Hingemen** are the parish constables: two or three to a parish, big men and women with iron bars and axes, whose duty is to fetch the accused and to carry out sentences of unhinging. They knock on the door of the accused before they take it off its hinges, because the law is the law.
- **The Trowel** carries out sentences of bricking, under the eye of an Attendant, and the guild is paid by the Crown per niche.
- **Border Marshals** enforce the law against leaving while carrying, on the Rim Road and the hill-paths.
- **The Foundling Companies** are called out against riots, smugglers and Part-hunting gangs, rarely and reluctantly; the born-grown do not like to arrest mothers.
- **Knockers** are informants, paid by the Attendance a lack a time for word of an unattended birth or a hidden Part. To call someone a knocker in Rudge is to start a fight.

### How a Trial Works

A lesser case is heard the same day the Hingemen bring the accused in. The accused stands in the doorway of the parish birthing-room, half in and half out, which is the position of anyone whose fate is not decided. The Attendant sits on the birthing-stool. Witnesses knock on the jamb before they speak, and the Attendant answers each knock with "come in," and a witness who speaks without knocking is not heard. There are no advocates. But any person may stand behind the accused in the doorway and lay a hand on their shoulder, and this person is called the **doorholder**: they may speak for the accused, and they take a share of the sentence if it is unhinging, since their own door is taken off with the accused's. A man with many doorholders is a man the parish wants to keep, and Attendants listen to that. A man with none is usually lost.

The Attendant asks the First Question, *what was done*, and hears the witnesses, and answers it aloud. Then she asks the Second, *does it stay*, and answers that, and the answer is the sentence. There is no appeal from a parish Attendant except to a Lying, a bench of three Seniors, and a Lying will hear only cases of descent, of bricking, and of the Attendance's own members.

Grave crimes, the ones that carry bricking, are heard in the Hall of the Lying on the Round, before three Seniors, and the sentence is read aloud in the Old Lying-In itself, in the birthing-room of the Meal, with the eight doors open and the city able to listen. The condemned is walked from there to the Stillyard by the Trowel, who carry their tools and the bricks already stamped with the condemned's name.

**Unhinging** deserves a word, because outsiders think it mild. The offender's house has every door taken off by the Hingemen, carried to the parish square, and burned, and the household must live open to the night for the length of the sentence, with no door, no curtain, no brick. In a country where things come through openings, it is a sentence of fear. An unhinged house is watched by its neighbors with pity and horror, and it is not unusual, by the end of a season, for the household to have gained a member nobody can account for, sitting quietly at the table in the morning, and the Tally to enter it as "born to the house, without bearer."

**Bricking** is the great sentence and the only death the Attendance gives except the knife, and it is not quite death. The condemned is mortared alive into a niche in the Debt Wall, with a slot at the height of the mouth through which bread and water are pushed each day by a slot-feeder as long as the family pays. Some families pay for forty years. Some stop after a week. The Wall is the most visited place in Kest, because in the evenings the families sit beneath the slots and talk to their bricked. A few of the oldest niches still take bread, and have not spoken in decades, and the families keep paying, because they are afraid of what will come out of the slot if they stop.

## The Faithful

Highland faith lives in the doorway. Every house in Cradlewrack has a **lintel-niche** over its main door, holding a small faceless clay figure with its arms spread to either side, and the household touches it going out and coming in. Nobody calls it Vey aloud. It is called the Holder, and children are told that it holds the door, and are not told which way.

The three doctrines of the Table all have their highland shapes. **The Shut Door** is the Theft: we shut the door on the god and killed it, and every reopened wound is the debt coming due. It is the faith of the Attendance, which does not preach it but lives it, and of most of the old parishes, and it makes the Second Opinion a penance carried out on the country's behalf. **The Open Arms** is the Gift: Vey spread its arms in the doorway not to bar it but to hold it open for us, and went into the pot so that the Lack would end, and the births are its love, too much of it, given too freely. It is the faith of mothers who keep their Parts and of the Uncut, and of the Late-faction whispering in the Aprons. **The Long Labor** is the Bait: Vey starved the highlands so that the highlands would carry it, and the country is now nine months gone with its god, and always was. The Long Labor is banned in four lands and not in Cradlewrack, because the Book of Questions says a question must be asked aloud; it is preached openly at Rimwatch and in the Barn, and it is the faith of the Assemblers, who believe they are midwives to it.

### Shrines and Holy Places

- **The Seven Arches** over the door of the Old Lying-In, touched by everyone who enters; and the eighth, which is not.
- **Knocking-stones** at every crossroads, hollow pillars knocked three times before a road is chosen.
- **Cord-trees** on the Tull and in Cordway, old alders on which the Uncut hang the dried cords of their dead, so that the wind moves them.
- **The Low Wall** in the Stillyard, where the struck-through are bricked without family marks; mothers come there on the anniversaries of births that were never entered, and lay a hand on the unstamped brick, and do not knock.

### Rites

- **The Knocking-In.** A new house is not lived in until a midwife has knocked on every one of its doors and walls from the outside, and waited for silence.
- **The Naming and the Second Meal.** At a year, a child is named before the household, and the household eats the salted afterbirth, stewed with lamb and onions, starting with the mother. A Caul-line child's caul is eaten raw from a silver dish.
- **The Nine Stitches.** The dead are washed and every opening sewn shut with nine stitches each, by the eldest woman of the family, while the rest sing the counting-song softly; the stitcher ties the knot that cannot come undone.
- **The Count.** On Pangday, families gather in the strongest room, hold hands, and count the sixty aloud together, and the youngest child says the last number.
- **The Reading of the Struck-Through.** A Barren rite now taken up by many who are not Barren: at the rim of the Dilation, or at the Low Wall, the names given in secret to the struck-through are read aloud by whoever knows them.

## How Power Moves

Cradlewrack looks, from outside, like a land ruled absolutely by an order of armed midwives, and in one sense it is: no birth happens without them, and no appeal lies from the knife. But the Attendance rules by being needed, not by owning anything, and everything it needs it must ask for. It needs the Caul-lines' springs and money, the Trowel's brick, the Tally's numbers, the Crown's dignity, the Companies' spears, and above all the willingness of a hundred thousand mothers to send for the midwife when the pains begin. That willingness is what the Assemblers are buying away from it, one Part at a time.

Three currents move under the table. The first is **money from outside**: the Second Table's coin in the Barn, the Purgation's in Nullhouse, the Company's in the caul-crates, all of it buying a stake in what will be born. The second is **the loss of the mothers**: every family that hides a Part instead of calling the midwife is a family that has stopped believing the knife protects it, and the Tally's numbers of concealment have doubled since the Shortening began. The third is **the interval itself**, which rearranges every alliance as it shortens. The Knife-faction and the Barren Order, who would once have despised each other, now meet in the back rooms of the Round to talk about stone. The Crown, which was a pleasant fiction for four centuries, has a king who has started to answer. And Orla Kest, who has the most power of anyone in the country, has quietly decided that when the interval reaches one day, power will be the wrong word for what is needed, and she has begun to set her house in order for someone else.

#### Who They Are

Orla Kest is small, square and grey, and when she comes into a room the room makes itself smaller to fit her. Her hair is cropped short under the white linen coif of the Paramount; her forearms are as thick as a laundress's and as red; her hands are broad, short-nailed, perfectly clean, and steadier than any hands in the highlands. She smells of lye and lavender, and under it, when she has come from a room, of iron. Her voice is low and level and has never in her life been raised. People who have heard her speak to a screaming mother, a weeping father and a dying apprentice in the same quarter-hour say it was the same voice each time, and that this was the most frightening thing about her, and the most comforting.

She moves slowly and does not waste a motion. She sits very still in council and taps one finger, once, when she has decided. She eats little, mostly clay-bread and posset, and she sleeps in the Old Lying-In itself, in a cot in the birthing-room of the Meal, with its eight doors open around her. Apprentices whisper that she does not sleep at all, only rests with her eyes open, counting.

#### Their Story

Orla was born in 573 A.G. in the Kest house of the Paramount line, descended in the female line from Hollan Kest, who crushed the last god between her thumbnails. Her mother was an Attendant of the Round, her grandmother a Paramount. In 582, when Orla was nine, her mother went into labor with a second child, and the Opinion who attended was Orla's aunt, Marrit Kest. There was no apprentice in the house. Marrit put the girl's back against the door and told her to hold it, and Orla held it, and felt it push against her the way doors push, and heard her aunt ask the First Question aloud and then, after the count of three, the Second. She heard her mother not cry out. She felt the bundle, warm, as it was carried past her. The Tally entered a daughter of the house, struck through. Orla has never spoken of it. She has also never, in fifty-nine years, failed to walk past the Low Wall on the anniversary.

She took the apron at sixteen and gave her first Second Opinion at nineteen, in Rudge, during the great flood of 592. She was made an Opinion at twenty-five and a Senior of the Lying at thirty-seven. She was chosen Paramount in 619, at forty-six, by the vote of the Seniors and by the custom that the Paramount is the midwife the others send for when they cannot do it themselves. In 627, at Cleave, she attended the birth of a born-grown captain who came out with a sword-hand already closed, and permitted him; in 629 she attended the birth of the old-born child now called Ambrose in a Kest weaver's house, and permitted him, and has visited him once a year since. In 635 she gave the first Foundlings their first spoons with her own hands. In 638 she sat the inquiry into the burning of the Rudge Part-house, and quietly closed it without naming the Barren Order, because she needed the Order's stone.

She has attended nine thousand births. She permitted six thousand. She remembers the other three thousand.

#### Their Place in the Land

The Midwife-Paramount answers to no one. She has no army but the Companies' goodwill, no money but the birth-fees, no law but the Book. And yet nothing moves in the highlands without her: the Veiled Council's prophecies are read to her before they are read to the Crown; the Chamberlain clears every question for the King Within with her office first; the Tally reports to her each dusk; the Border Marshals hold their law by her leave; the Trowel bricks nobody without an Attendant's word. She sits each morning in the Hall of the Lying to hear what cannot be decided elsewhere, and each afternoon she goes into a room somewhere in Kest, because she must, and because the Book says that a Paramount who stops attending births stops being able to judge them.

Her power has limits she feels every day. The mothers are hiding Parts. The Late-faction is whispering in the Aprons. The Assemblers' figure breathes four hours' walk from her bed and she has not been able to persuade the Crown to send the Companies against the Barn, because General Tibb will not march on a barn full of mothers. She depends on Lark Ninedays for the interval, on Madder Rudge for the rim, on Callum Stillwater for the stone, and on Sabeth Thorne, more than on anyone, for the knife.

#### What They Carry

- **The Second Opinion** — her knife, a short hooked blade in a case of black leather worn on her hip. It was Marrit Kest's. She has never let an apprentice clean it.
- **The Paramount's keys** — a ring of nine iron keys to the doors of the Old Lying-In, none of which locks or has ever locked. She carries them as a reminder that her office is to hold things, not shut them.
- **A ledger of the three thousand** — a thick book in her own small hand, one line per struck-through birth: parish, date, the mother's eye-color, and the answer. She reads in it at night. The count at the end is always one fewer than the entries. (She has entered her sister. She has not counted her.)
- **A stitching kit** on a neck-cord, the same as every highlander's, with gut she spins herself.
- **Lavender** in a linen bag at her belt, for the rooms that need it.
- **A lead-weighted rope** — sixty fathoms of tarred rope with a lead weight at one end, bought from Rimwatch last Plenty and kept rolled under her cot. Nobody has asked her what it is for.
- **A sealed letter from Lark Ninedays** — the date on which, by Lark's arithmetic, the interval will reach one day. Orla has read it once.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

Orla wants the knife to hold until the end, and she wants to be in the room when the end comes. She is not afraid of what she has done. She is afraid that the Late-faction is right: that she has spent fifty years answering small questions while the large one dilated under her feet, and that the knife was six centuries too late. She is afraid of Sabeth Thorne's eleven breaths, because Sabeth is the best of them, and if the best of them has stopped believing, the order is finished.

**Secret (for the GM to reveal):** Orla has asked herself the Second Question about the country, and answered it. When the interval reaches one day, she means to go down into the Dilation on Madder Rudge's rope, with her knife, to attend. She has told no one. She has quietly been setting her house in order: Sabeth is to succeed her.

**Secret (she does not know):** her sister was not struck through. Marrit Kest hesitated, in 582, for the first and only time, and the bundle Orla felt pass her was carried not to the Low Wall but to the Lying-In, where the thing was put into the lower wards and bricked in alive. It is there still, fifty-nine years old, in a cell no bricklayer stays beside for more than a season, and lately it has been knocking, in the interval, every ninth day. The Lying-In's own book records it. Orla has the right to read that book and has never looked at the entry for her own family, because she believed she knew what it said.

#### Ties

- **Sabeth Thorne** — her best knife and chosen successor. Orla knows about the loft in Tarnbrow and is waiting, as her aunt never did, for Sabeth to choose for herself.
- **Brother Callum Stillwater** — an ally of necessity; they meet in the back rooms of the Round to talk about grey stone and do not talk about the Rudge fire.
- **Dov Marrin** — her enemy, and the only surgeon in the highlands whose hands she respects as much as her own. She has tried twice to have him bricked; the Crown would not sign.
- **Ambrose** — she permitted him and visits him each year on his birthday. He once told her the color of her mother's eyes, which she had forgotten.
- **General Tibb** — she gave him his first spoon. He calls her Mother Orla and will not march on the Barn for her.
- **Lark Ninedays** — the only person who tells her the truth every day, in numbers.
- **Madder Rudge** — her eyes on the rim. Orla has asked her how deep a rope can go. Madder has not asked why.
- **Queen Dowager Sibyl Aubrey** — Orla has attended her for forty-four years, every week, and is the only person alive who has felt the King Within from the inside.

#### In Play

The characters meet Orla when one of them is carrying, whatever their sex, since she will attend; when they come close to Sabeth Thorne; or when they bring a Part, a born-grown stranger or a question about the Dilation to the Round. She wants three things from them: to know what Sabeth is hiding without having to ask her; to have word carried to the Lying-In's lower wards that she cannot carry herself; and, near the end, someone to hold the rope.

- **The Door Held Twice.** A bricklayer of the lower wards comes to the characters, frightened, with a story of a cell that knocks in the interval and a name scratched into the mortar from inside: *Kest*. He wants them to tell the Paramount, because he cannot.
- **A Birth in the Ditch.** The characters are with Orla on the road when she hears of a birth a mile off, in a Barren house, where nobody wanted a midwife. She goes. The Barren bar the way. She goes anyway.
- **The Rope.** At the end of a campaign, when the interval is a day, Orla asks the characters to come with her to the rim, and to hold the rope, and not to pull until she knocks.

**Threatened**, she does not raise her voice; she asks the First Question, and things born in Cradlewrack stop to listen. **Shown kindness**, she accepts it gravely and repays it exactly. **Faced with the god**, she asks it what it is. If nobody intervenes, Orla Kest goes down into the Dilation on the day the interval reaches one, and the rope goes slack, and the next Paramount is chosen in an empty Old Lying-In with its eight doors wide.

#### Who They Are

Sabeth Thorne is long-boned and quiet, a tall woman who stoops through doorways that do not need it. Her black hair is cropped close as a soldier's; her face is narrow, sunburned from the roads, with deep lines at the corners of a mouth that does not often move. Her hands are the thing people notice: long-fingered, beautiful, and raw to the wrist, the knuckles split and scabbed from scrubbing, so that she wears linen wraps on them between rooms. She smells of lye and spirit and catgut. She speaks seldom and briefly, in a low flat voice from the Coppers, and she listens with her whole body, head tilted, as if every room were a belly she had her ear to.

She moves very fast when she moves. Apprentices who have held the door for her say they never saw her shoulder move; they only saw that it was done.

#### Their Story

Sabeth was born in 601 A.G. in the Coppers, the fifth child of a laundress, and grew up stirring aprons in the vats. She learned what the Second Opinion was by what came out of the rinse. She asked to apprentice at fifteen, held the door for her first Second Opinion at sixteen, and did not quit, which made her unusual. She was made an Opinion at twenty-two, the youngest in a century, because the Seniors had noticed that she could look at a thing for the count of three and decide, and live with it, and go into the next room. For eighteen years she was the Opinion the other Opinions sent for. She has given the Second Opinion more than eleven hundred times.

Last winter, in Lack 640, she was sent for to a farmhouse in Tarnbrow, where a shepherd named Aled Dell had been in labor for two days. He did not live. What came out of him was the size of a newborn lamb and shaped like a child, with a seam across its throat and eyes that were very dark, and it looked at her, and she counted. One, two, three. It did not look away. She counted to eleven. Then she wrapped it in her own apron, paid Aled's widow, Annis Dell, a year's wages from her own savings to keep it in the loft and say nothing, and entered the Tally as "one, born to the father, died with him." She has walked to Tarnbrow eleven times since with the knife-case. She has come home eleven times with the case unopened.

In Carving 641, at Rudge, with her apprentice Wenna Thrale holding the door, she counted eleven breaths again over a thing born to a man named Corran, and then answered, and used the knife. She has counted eleven ever since. Wenna has noticed.

#### Their Place in the Land

Sabeth is the knife of last resort. Parish Attendants who cannot answer the Second Question send to the Round for an Opinion, and when the Opinion cannot, the Round sends Sabeth. She rides the whole country on a hill-goat with her case and her apprentice, sleeping in pang-houses, attending births in farmhouses, ditches, lambing-sheds and once in a well. She is paid an Opinion's share of the fee, which she gives almost entirely to the Coppers. She has no house of her own; she has a cot in the Aprons and a shelf in the Lying-In. Orla Kest has chosen her as successor and has not yet told her. The Late-faction, which has noticed her eleven breaths, has begun very carefully to court her.

#### What They Carry

- **The knife** — an Opinion's hooked blade, plain, the handle wrapped in fresh linen every morning. She has never named it.
- **The bone hook** — for births that will not come, carved from the shin of a ewe. It has a Hooking quality and she uses it in fights as she uses it in rooms.
- **The cord-thread, the basin, the linen** — laid out in the same order every time, the order the Book gives.
- **A pumice stone** worn to a sliver, for her hands.
- **A wooden horse** — a toy, carved by Annis Dell's dead husband for the second child they never had. Sabeth took it from the farmhouse on her third visit, meaning to give it to the thing in the loft, and has carried it in her apron pocket ever since without giving it.
- **The empty purse** — a year's wages, spent. She is poor now, and nobody at the Round knows why.
- **Wenna Thrale** — not a possession, but Sabeth carries her apprentice the way she carries the knife, as a responsibility she cannot put down.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

Sabeth wants someone else to make her choose. She knows exactly what the Book says and exactly what she must do, and she has walked to Tarnbrow eleven times to do it. She fears the thing in the loft; she fears more that she loves it; and she fears most of all that she will reach the farmhouse one day and find she is too late, because it has learned to walk to her.

**Secret (for the GM to reveal):** the thing in the loft is now the size of a child of six after only ten months. It has Sabeth's eyes, though it cannot have. It calls her Mother. It asks to be taken to see the Sill. It has begun to open doors by looking at them, and Annis Dell has stopped bricking herself in because there is no point. Its naming-year ends at Tablenight, two months from now, and it has asked Sabeth what its name will be.

**Secret (she does not know):** Orla Kest has known about the loft since the first week. A Tarnbrow knocker told the parish Attendant, and the Attendant told the Round. Orla has done nothing, because she once held a door and has spent fifty-nine years wishing someone had let her choose.

#### Ties

- **Orla Kest** — her mistress and the woman she most fears disappointing. Sabeth does not know she is to succeed her.
- **Wenna Thrale** — her apprentice, who wrote down the eleven breaths. Sabeth is teaching her everything and dreads the day Wenna is as good as she is.
- **Annis Dell** — the widow in Tarnbrow; bound to Sabeth by money and fear.
- **Hester Lowe** — Sabeth has never met her, but she would know the callus on that hand at a glance; it is the callus of a knife-grip, like her own.

#### In Play

The characters meet Sabeth in the room: she is attending a birth they are present at, or she rides into a village they are passing through with her case. #### Who They Are

Ambrose looks like a scholar of sixty: tall, straight-backed now, with an iron-grey beard trimmed to a point and a long courteous face. Twelve years ago the beard was white to his chest and his back was bent; every year since, he has stood a little straighter and his voice has lost a little more of its quaver. His eyes are pale blue and very clear, and the left one is noticeably larger than the right. He smells of camphor, which he uses against moths in his books, and of warm milk, which he cannot account for. He speaks in a precise, amused, old-fashioned Table-speech, with words nobody has used in two centuries, and he is unfailingly kind to children, who adore him.

He wears a high silk cravat at all times and is never seen without it.

#### Their Story

Ambrose was born in 629 A.G. in a weaver's house on Tally Hill, to a young woman named Tilda Warp, in a labor Orla Kest herself attended. He came out with a white beard to his chest, arthritic hands, worn yellow teeth and a full vocabulary, and his first words, before the Paramount had asked anything, were "Ah. It's you. You have your grandmother's hands." The Book says old-born children stay. Orla permitted him. His mother, who was seventeen, could not bear to nurse a man of eighty, and he was taken in by the Morwen nursery as a curiosity, and became, within a year, its tutor.

He claims to remember being Vey's left eye. He describes the Meal from inside the copper: the heat, the clay-salt stinging, the ladle, the caul lifting off above him "like a sky going up," the faces of the first highlanders leaning over with their bowls. He names them. The Cutwrights have checked several of the names against the oldest Kest genealogies and found them right. He is getting younger. At twelve, he has the body of a man of sixty. He says that when he reaches the cradle, it will be time.

#### Their Place in the Land

Ambrose teaches letters, history and manners to the children of the Caul-line houses, in the Veiled Galleries of Morwen Hall and in the Aubrey Gardens, for which he is paid in board and books. He also receives visitors, by appointment, in a small round room on Tally Hill: scholars, priests of the three doctrines, Assembler agents, Second Table nobles in silk masks, and the Attendance, who all want to know what the god saw. He answers, courteously, once per visit. The Veiled Council consults him on prophecy and does not like what he says. He is, in a quiet way, the most valuable witness in the country, and he is guarded by nobody.

#### What They Carry

- **A silk cravat** — high, wound three times. Beneath it, at the base of his throat, is his crowning: a round opening with soft lips the size of a coin, through which, at the height of a pang, something presses up toward the light. It is an eye. It is never the same eye twice.
- **A cradle-shaped reliquary** — a small box of carved pear-wood holding his own birth-caul, dried, which he has kept against the Second Meal; he says he will need it.
- **A child's letter-book** — the one he teaches from, its pages soft with handling. In the margin, in his own hand, he has begun copying out simple words as if learning them.
- **Spectacles** — he needed them at birth. He no longer does. He wears them from habit.
- **The Ledger of the Pot** — a calfskin book in which he writes down everything he remembers of the Meal, each night, because he has noticed that he is forgetting it, the way old men forget their youth, from the far end first.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

Ambrose wants to say what he was born to say. He has known since his first breath that there is a sentence he must speak when the time comes, and he is afraid, very simply and without any of his usual humor, that he will forget it before then. He fears cradles, and sits beside them for hours.

**Secret (for the GM to reveal):** he is not growing younger toward birth. He is being drawn back toward the Dilation, and he will be there, the size of a newborn, when it opens. Every year he loses, he walks a mile closer in his sleep; the Morwen servants have found him twice at dawn on the Cleave road, barefoot, facing the rim.

**Secret (he does not know):** the sentence he was born to say is the other half of Juniper Dell's. Her half begins *When it opens, tell them that the...* His half is the end of it. He has already forgotten the first word.

He also remembers a thing he does not write down: that when the caul lifted off above him in the pot, he saw the table where the first highlanders sat, and that there were more bowls than people, and that one chair at the copper was pulled out, and nobody was in it.

#### Ties

- **Dr. Lazar Voss** — they correspond. Voss has drawn him, sleeping, and the drawing shows his crowning open.

#### In Play

#### Who They Are

She is quick to anger and quicker to laugh it off, and she has a Rudge digger's contempt for anyone who has never had clay under their nails, which includes most of Kest. Her house is one of the egg-shaped red houses halfway down the ravine, tidy, warm, with a hide curtain for a door and a hearthstone that has been lifted so often the mortar has given up.

#### Their Story

Hester was born in Rudge in 607 and dug clay from the age of nine. She married Wat Lowe, a digger, at nineteen; they had no children that stayed. Wat died in 637 when a pit wall slid during a pang and buried him with three others. She dug him out herself, with this spade. In Grace 638 she went into labor alone, at night, with no idea she had been carrying, and bore a hand: only a hand, left, healthy, warm, the size of a grown woman's, with a callus on the inner side of its ring finger nobody could explain. She did not call the Attendance. She wrapped it in Wat's shirt and put it in the bread-crock, and in the morning it was holding the edge of the shirt the way a baby holds a finger.

Three years on, she feeds it warm milk through the skin of its palm, which drinks, and talks to it while she works. #### Who They Are

Stumpy, broad, red to the elbows from fifty years of clay, with a face like a dried apple and a pipe she smokes upside down against the steam. She smells of wet earth and black tobacco. She is blunt, profane, and kind to pilgrims, whom she calls "ducks."

#### Their Story

A Rudge digger from childhood, she came to the rim in 621 after her son walked into it in his sleep. She stayed. In 629 she went down ninety feet on a rope, alone, at night, and touched something warm and smooth that moved under her hand. She came up and told no one.

#### Their Place in the Land

She runs the watch: forty stake-setters, mostly her own grandchildren. Her morning stakes are the Attendance's measure of the rim. Orla Kest reads her reports before Lark's.

#### What They Carry

- **The pipe**, upside down; **painted stakes**; **ninety feet of tarred rope**, the same rope; and **one glove**, stained a red that has not washed out in twelve years.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

She wants to know what is down there before it comes up. **Secret:** the thing she touched moved *toward* her. **Secret (she does not know):** it followed her rope partway up, and every night it lies ninety feet below her listening-place, its ear to the clay, listening back. The second pulse is its.

#### Ties

- **Orla Kest** — has asked her how deep a rope can go.
- **Ambrose** — lies beside her twice a year and says the second pulse is his.
- **Josiah Fenn** — she saw the giant tracks go in, and smaller ones come out.
- **Hester Lowe** — cousin by marriage; Madder knows the hand points at the Sill.

#### In Play

- **The Edge Stakes.** A grandchild is gone with the noon stakes. Madder wants a rope party.
- **Ninety Feet.** Orla asks the characters to persuade Madder to lend her rope.
- **Listening Back.** Madder asks a character to put their ear to the clay. It whispers their name.

If nobody intervenes, Madder lowers Orla's rope herself, and holds it, and is still at the rim holding it when the rim moves.

#### Who They Are

Rangy, sun-black, silent, with a wrapped pucker of scar over his navel and widened hips he walks stiffly on. He smells of sheep and peat and old blood.

#### Their Story

In 633 he labored three days in a hill bothy above Tarnbrow, alone, and delivered something that stood up, looked at him with great tenderness, and walked out of the door. He gave his flock to his brother and followed. The tracks have grown to the size of a cart; the Get is twelve feet tall.

#### Their Place in the Land

He has none. Shepherds feed him; the Attendance would like a word; the Get's trail of flattened flocks makes him unwelcome in every fold.

#### What They Carry

- **The measuring-cord** — knotted once for every track measured, eight years long.
- **A shepherd's knife** — he is not sure whether he means it for the Get or himself, and he keeps it sharp so as not to have to decide.
- **A lamb's bell** — found beside his fire one morning, cleaned. The Get leaves him gifts.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

He fears it will not remember him; more, that it will. **Secret:** the Get comes to watch his fire at night, at a distance, and bleats. **Secret (he does not know):** its tracks went into the Dilation, and came out with a second, smaller set beside them. He is a grandfather.

#### Ties

- **Madder Rudge** — saw the tracks go in.
- **Annis Dell** — her husband bore too; she feeds Josiah when he passes.
- **Sabeth Thorne** — would have been sent to the bothy, had he called.
- **General Tibb** — has offered him a Company escort; Josiah refused.

#### In Play

- **Fetch the Footprints.** Josiah begs the characters to walk the last stretch with him.
- **The Gift.** A dead ewe, laid with care at the party's camp.
- **Father's Voice.** The Get is in a village. Only Josiah's call can stop it.

If nobody intervenes, he finds it, and calls, and it comes, and holds him as it holds everything, too tight.

#### Who They Are

Forty-five, broad, deaf in her left ear from the forge, sawdust in her braids and burn-scars on her wrists. Proud, rude, expensive. She smells of bog oak, hot iron and lead.

#### Their Story

A carpenter's daughter, she built her first holding door at twenty, in 616; it lasted a night. She has spent twenty-five years making them hold longer: iron-bound bog oak from the Tullford mosses, locks of nine wards, hinges in poured lead, and a full day of her own knocking on each before sale. Her best held nineteen days. The customer did not die on the twentieth, whatever Kest says; he opened the door and found one more person in the room than he had left there.

#### Their Place in the Land

The rich of Kest wait a year for her locks (200 lacks, and the Hinge-Duty). Tamsin Keyless sells counterfeits that hold only from the outside, and Agnes would gladly break her hands.

#### What They Carry

- **A brass spyglass** — for watching the Sill from a mile off.
- **The Sill's measurements** — every joint, nail and hinge, in her own fine hand.
- **A nine-ward key** — to the one door in her yard she has never sold.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

She wants a door that holds forever. **Secret:** every one of her best doors is built to the Sill's pattern. **Secret (she does not know):** when one of them finally opens, it opens for a breath onto the same place the Sill does: a long warm room, a table laid, and a chair pulled out.

#### Ties

- **Old Hinge** — she pays him to tell her the gap each Grace.
- **Brother Callum Stillwater** — his chapter-room door held eleven days.
- **Absalom Gage** — rival; brick holds and her doors do not.
- **Orla Kest** — has ordered a door for the Lying-In's lowest ward.

#### In Play

- **Nineteen Days.** A customer's door has held eighteen. He hires the characters to be in the room on the twentieth.
- **The Unsold Door.** Something knocks from behind the door in her yard.
- **The Pattern.** Old Hinge says the Sill opened a hand's width this Grace. Agnes asks the characters to measure it, without looking through.

If nobody intervenes, she builds the door Orla ordered, and it holds, and that is the worst thing that could happen.
