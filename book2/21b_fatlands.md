## The People of the Fatlands, Continued

> Everybody in the south is somebody's dinner, sooner or later. The trick is to be somebody's good dinner.
>> — Wakemother Pudding Hesk, to an apprentice who asked why she cried while carving

The first half of the Fatlands' cast sits in the Weighhouse, at the Unending Dinner and in Hogget's Barn: the vast, the hungry and the counting. The second half walks the roads and works the kitchens. These are the drovers and the weighers, the cooks and the knives, the bailiffs and the keepers of mills and groves, the girl who is never hungry and the man who should never have been. They are the people a party actually meets in the south, at an inn table or a wake or a dusk field, and almost every one of them is carrying something they would rather not have to set down.

### Sal Trotter — the Drover

> "Walk on, now. Walk on, my lovelies. No, don't look at me. Look at the road. There's a good lass. There's a good lad. Walk on."
>> — Sal Trotter, to her herd, on the Drove Road at dusk

#### Who They Are

Sal Trotter is lean in a country where lean means guilty. She is a long, tough, wind-dried woman, all tendon and knuckle, burnt brick-red on the face and the backs of the hands and pale as tallow everywhere else, with grey hair braided tight and tucked down the back of a dung-crusted oilskin coat that creaks when she walks. She smells of cattle, rain, wet leather and the sweetish green reek of the Drove Road in Plenty, and under all of it, faintly, of the salt-fat she chews from the tin at her belt to keep the Bailiffs off her: a strip every mile, eaten steadily, without pleasure, the way other drovers spit. Her voice, when she uses it on people, is a dry, flat, uninterested croak. When she uses it on the herd it changes completely: it drops into a low, even, endless croon, half song and half nonsense, that goes on all day and all night and never quite stops, so that people who have walked a drove with her find themselves humming it in their sleep for weeks afterward.

Her eyes are the most noticeable thing about her: pale, red-rimmed, and fixed always ahead, at the road. Sal Trotter does not look back. It is not superstition. It is policy, and she has kept it for eight years.

#### Their Story

She was born in 595 in a drovers' hut on the edge of Low Tilth, the daughter and granddaughter of Union drovers. She married a salter named Abbot Trotter, who drowned in a rendering-vat in 619, and raised one son, Tobias, called Dab, a cheerful, heavy, singing boy who could not keep a lack in his pocket for an afternoon.

In 632 she lost a whole herd: sixty head, gone off the Drove Road in a single night of Belch-wind near Gristmoot, and never found. The Union docked her the value. She could not pay. In Carving 633 Dab, who was seventeen and had signed for his mother's debt without telling her, was read out on a Cullmaster's list in Kettle Furrow square. He ate the dusk-supper with great appetite, she has been told, and joked with the clerks, and walked out to plot LT-114 in a clean white smock with a ribbon on his sickle. She was on the road. She came back to a receipt marked *Paid in Full*.

The next spring the Union gave her a new herd, out of the Board's own stock, without asking for a deposit. The beasts were pinkish-white and broad-faced and very gentle, and on the third night of the drive, when she turned around to count them, the whole herd was standing up. She has not turned around since.

#### Their Place in the Land

Sal is one of perhaps forty drovers still walking stock up the Drove Road, the nine-day track from the Low Tilth stockyards to the Chute; the rest go by barge on the Great Gravy now. The Union keeps a few drovers on because some herds cannot go by water. They panic on the barges, and they open the hatches. Sal's herds are always that kind. Her stock comes from the Board's pens on the edge of the Dusk Acres, where the Cullmasters' calving-men gather young beasts out of the wheat each spring, slick and wet and newborn, standing in the stubble of the plots that were worked at dusk the Carving before.

She is paid twice: once by the Union at the Chute, at the drover's rate of a lack a head, and once, quietly, by a clerk of Varn Sweetbread's office, in meat scrip, for "careful handling." She depends on the inns of the Drove Road to sell her salt-fat and stand her a supper, and the inns depend on her not stopping too long. Children follow her herds for a mile out of every village, singing, until their mothers call them back. The beasts sing too, when the children have gone.

#### What She Carries

- **The drover's goad and bola** — Union issue, the goad's iron tip worn to a nub. She has used the bola twice in eight years, both times on a beast that stood up and ran toward a village, and both times the beast went down, cried like a man, and got up again on four legs.
- **The unlit pipe** — Abbot's. She fills it every morning with good leaf and never lights it. The smell of it is the only thing on the road that is not cattle.
- **The herd-book** — a greasy Union tally-book with a column of names for every drove since 634. Every name has been scratched out with a thumbnail. She named the first herd as she always had (Bluebell, Old Stoop, Butter, Ned). On the fifth night, she called "Ned" and three of them answered, aloud, in a voice like a boy whose voice is breaking.
- **The Cullmaster's receipt** — a slip of grey paper, folded small, sewn into the lining of her coat: *T. Trotter. Plot LT-114. Labour rendered. Paid in Full. V.S.* She has never read it a second time.
- **The bell with no clapper** — the lead steer's bell. She took the clapper out the night the lead steer began to hum her croon back to her, in her own tune, under the bell, so she would not have to hear the two together.
- **A brand-iron, wrapped in sacking** — the Union brand, a circle and a cleaver. She has never used it on the Board's herds. They already have scars where a brand would be, and the scars are letters and numbers.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

Sal wants to finish one drive without looking back: to deliver a herd to the Chute, take her pay, and go home, and never know. She fears the day one of them asks her where they are going, and she knows it is close, because the lead steer, a vast gentle animal with a curl on its brow and a habit of nosing at her coat pocket for the salt-fat the way Dab used to, has started to hum the croon a beat *ahead* of her now, as though it knows the song better than she does.

**A secret she keeps:** Sal knows exactly where her herds come from. She worked it out in the first month and has made her peace with it in the only way she could, by refusing to look. She takes Sweetbread's second wage. She has never told the Union, the Thin, or Pell Hogget, whose wall carries the names of half the people she has walked to market.

**A secret she does not know:** the scar on the lead steer's flank, where a brand would be, reads *LT-114*. The beast is not her son, exactly. It is what the field made of the strip where her son was worked. But it remembers the song.

#### Ties

- **Varn Sweetbread** — pays her second wage. She calls him "sir" and will not take his hand.
- **Mistress Brisket Annalow** — her Union boss, who keeps Sal walking because Sal's herds cannot go by barge and Annalow knows why.
- **Pell Hogget** — she drinks with him at the Kettle Furrow inn twice a year, and never looks at his wall.
- **Tobias Wether** — she found him on the Drove Road in Carving 640, starved half to death at the Rim, and fed him salt-fat from her tin, and gave him her son's name because it was the first that came to her. She has not seen him since.
- **Quartermaster Ivo Stockpot** — stamps her beasts at the Chute and will not meet her eye.
- **Kale Dragoman** — buys her knowledge of the empty drove-tracks along the Fast's border, and feeds his captives on her road. She does not like how gently he does it.

#### In Play

PCs meet Sal on the road: on the Drove Road, at an inn in Low Tilth, or on the Rim Road where it crosses the Fatlands' northern edge. She may hire them as extra hands for a drive (two lacks a day and board), especially in Carving, when drovers die. What she wants from them is simple and impossible: walk behind the herd so she does not have to. Tell her if anything is standing. Never tell her what.

- **The Ninth Night.** On the last night of a drive the party is hired for, the herd stops in a field outside Gristmoot and will not move. Every beast is turned to face the same way, toward a village, and the lead steer is humming the wake-song.
- **A Question on the Road.** A beast asks one of the PCs, quite clearly, where they are going. Sal begs them to lie.
- **Rustlers.** Second Helping pilgrims try to steal the herd for an Offering feast; or Thin activists try to free it. Either way, the beasts must not be allowed to reach a village unwatched.

Under threat, Sal goes for the bola, and then simply walks away, never turning. Kindness confuses her; she takes it and leaves at dawn. Of the god she says nothing at all. If nobody intervenes, the lead steer asks its question on the eighth day of the spring drive of 642; Sal turns around to answer it, sees her herd standing in the road, and walks into the wheat beside the Drove Road and sits down. The herd reaches Gristmoot without her, upright, humming, and opens the gates.

### Wren Hollowell — the Fasting Girl

> "Everyone keeps asking me what it feels like not to be hungry. I don't know. What does it feel like to be? It sounds horrible."
>> — Wren Hollowell, to Corder Lank, the first night in the Orchards

[stat: Wren Hollowell — the Fasting Girl]

#### Who They Are

Wren is small and narrow and pale, with a long plain face, straight mouse-brown hair cut raggedly at one side, and grey eyes that rest on things without wanting them. In the Fatlands, where every face has been built by a lifetime of eating, she looks like a drawing of a girl rather than a girl: thin lines, empty spaces, no weight anywhere.

She smells of nothing. That is the first thing Fatlanders notice and the thing they cannot stop noticing. Every Fatlander smells faintly of the fields, of bread and grass and dung; Wren smells like rain on a stone. Her voice is quiet and a little hoarse, because she has spent two years whispering in barns, and she is dry and blunt and occasionally very funny in the way of someone who has been looked at too much. She eats when someone reminds her, slowly, without interest, and leaves the crust. In the south, leaving the crust is a fine. She has been fined, by her own count, four hundred and twelve times.

#### Their Story

She was born in Bramble Furrow, a Low Tilth hamlet of nine houses, in 626, to Hennet Hollowell, a rush-weaver, and Ruddle Hollowell, a hedger. She did not cry for the breast. The midwife, who had seen everything, put her to the nipple anyway, and she fed politely and stopped and went to sleep, and the midwife looked at Hennet over the baby's head and said nothing, and Hennet understood that she must never let anyone see the child refuse food.

For twelve years Hennet fed Wren loudly. She fed her in the doorway, in the lane, at every one of the five meals, singing, so the neighbours would hear the spoon. She sewed weights into the hem of the child's smock before the weekly weighing and bribed the parish weigher with eggs. It worked until 638, when the parish weigher died and a Cutwrights' College surveyor came through Low Tilth taking examinations for the southern registry, and Wren, at twelve, put out her tongue and let him look at her teeth and her nails and the backs of her eyes, and he went very still and wrote in his ledger for a long time.

The Bailiffs came within the month. Ruddle hid her in a hay-rick and walked out to meet them with a hedging-bill and was taken to the Gavage, where he is, by all report, now very large. Hennet carried Wren by night across Low Tilth to the edge of the Bleeding Orchards, put her into the hands of a Thin woman she had never met, and walked back home to be arrested. Wren has not seen either of them since. The Board's bounty went up in Carving 639: her weight in bone coin, to whoever brings her in, and the proclamation did not say alive.

#### Their Place in the Land


The Thin kneel to her, which she hates. To Corder Lank's people she is proof that the hunger is not natural, that a Fatland body *can* be empty, and they move her from barn to barn through the Orchards and the hedges of Low Tilth like a relic carried in procession, a step ahead of the Bailiffs. The Board wants her because it wants nobody to have proof of anything. The Second Table wants her organs: the rumour in the high houses is that a Blank's heart, eaten, fills you forever, and while that is false, a Blank's blood and marrow are believed to slow the Regrowth, and Master Quillon Barr has a surgery in Sated with a table already scrubbed. She wants to see the sea.

#### What She Carries

- **A gleaner's apron** — twelve pockets, stained to the elbow, her mother's work. She gleaned behind the harvesters as a child like every child in Low Tilth, and the habit of picking up what others leave has stayed with her.
- **A small knife** — a hedger's knife of her father's, honed thin. She has not used it on a person. She has thought about it.
- **One shell** — a ribbed white cockle, given her by a Rim Road carter who said it came from Brinehollow. She holds it to her ear at night. The carter told her you could hear the sea in it. She cannot. She has decided that is because the sea is leaving, and the shell is only being honest.
- **Half her hair** — the other half was cut off in her sleep in Lack 640 by Ottilie Crane, a Lean of nineteen she had begun to love, who sold it in Sated for nine hundred lacks to buy her brother off a dusk-shift list. It hangs at the throats of rich women now, who boast that they no longer feel their first Partake each day. Wren wants it back. She wants Ottilie back more, and knows she cannot have that.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

She wants the sea: not a cure, not a cause, not to be a saint, but a horizon with no field in it. She wants her mother. She wants people to stop looking at her the way hungry people look at a window. She fears the Gavage, not for herself but for what she will see on her father's face. She fears, more quietly, that she is lying to herself about not being hungry, and that one day it will arrive all at once.

**A secret she keeps:** Corder Lank has tried to pass his hunger to her. He fasted beside her for nine days in 640, hoping that if his gnawing could go anywhere it could go into a vessel with nothing in it, and so free his people. It would not go into her. It went *around* her, like water round a stone, and fell back into the Thin of the barn, doubled. Three of them broke that night. Wren knows. Lank knows she knows.

**A secret she does not know:** the fields do not go quiet because they cannot smell her. They go quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone who is not expected comes in and stands in the doorway. Toothed wheat, for twenty paces around her, holds perfectly still: not empty, not indifferent. *Holding still*. The Orchard-keepers who have seen it say the trees do the same, and the oldest of them, Marigold Chine, says she has heard only one other thing that made her grove fall that silent, and that was the song about the Meal. In the carvings, the gods are said not to have moved either.

#### Ties

- **Corder Lank** — her protector and the person she trusts least: he needs her too much.
- **Marigold Chine** — brings her orchard-fruit in the Old Grove and does not kneel. Wren loves her for it.
- **Lord Chastain Loin** — hunting her for the Second Table's cellar, through poachers and pleasant letters left in Thin barns.
- **Kale Dragoman** — the poacher who has come closest; he once got near enough to offer her stew, and was startled when she simply said no thank you once and meant it.
- **Tobias Wether** — the only other person in the south who knows what the absence of the god is like; he came out of it and she was born in it. She would like to meet him. He would like very much to eat with her.
- **Bailiff Dunmow Fatt** — took her father to the Gavage. Weeps about it. She has watched him from a hedge.

#### In Play

PCs meet Wren through the Thin, through the bounty, or by accident: a girl in a hedge who does not flinch when the field beside the road leans in at dusk. She wants from them what no one else has offered her: to be taken somewhere and left there. The hook *Wren for Weight* in the core book is her story in brief; she is the centre of the Blank Trade thread.

- **To the Sea.** Wren asks the party to escort her out of the Fatlands, along the Rim to Brinehollow. Every bounty-hunter in the south follows, and in Brinehollow the sea is a mile further out than the map says.
- **The Silent Field.** Low Tilth farmers discover that a Reaping does not happen where Wren walks. Within a week, every village wants to borrow her for Carving dusk. The Cullmasters want her for the opposite reason.
- **Her Father.** Wren will give herself up to the Board if someone brings her father out of the Gavage alive and thin. She means it.

Under threat, Wren hides, quietly and very well; she does not panic, because panic is a kind of appetite. Kindness she accepts carefully, testing it the way you test ice. Of the god she has nothing to say, except that she does not believe it can see her. If nobody intervenes, she is taken in Carving 641 by Dragoman's men in a barn the Thin thought safe, and stage two of the Blank Trade thread follows: a Gut-line matriarch at the next Weighing, ten stone lighter, not eating, smiling.

"She's the only proof. One girl who isn't hungry, and the whole Board of Plenty sweats in its benches." — Corder Lank
"I put a bowl down in front of her and she said thank you and didn't touch it, and I swear the soup went cold faster than it should." — a farm-wife of Low Tilth who sheltered her for a night

### Garrick Tripe — the Weigher

> "Every man's weight is a matter of record, sir, and every record is a matter of who keeps it. Ten lacks the pound. Lead's extra if you want it warm."
>> — Garrick Tripe, in the robing-room behind the Great Scales, the morning of a Weighing

#### Who They Are

Garrick Tripe is the most cheerful man in the Weighhouse and the only one who is not afraid of the Scales. He is a broad, glossy, well-upholstered man of four hundred and twenty pounds, which in Sated is middling-respectable, poured into a bottle-green coat with two rows of brass buttons that strain like the lid of a pot on the boil, with a waxed ginger-grey moustache and a ring on every finger. He smells of pomade, cloves, warm brass and the faint metallic tang of lead, which gets into the skin of anyone who handles it for forty years. He talks constantly: a rolling, oily, confiding patter full of *sir* and *madam* and *between ourselves*, the voice of a man selling you a horse he knows is lame and wants you to know he knows.


#### Their Story

He was born in 583 in the Scale Rings of Sated, the son of a Weighhouse sweeper, and grew up under the Great Scales, sweeping the crumbs of the great from around the pans. At sixteen he was taken on as apprentice to old Weigher Dimmock Hale, who taught him the three arts of the office: how to read the beam, how to read the face of the person in the pan, and how to read the purse behind the face.

He elected his first government at twenty-five. At the Weighing of 608 the chair of the Board hung between Obb Tullow, then a mere fourteen hundred pounds and still walking, and Dame Hessaly Gorringe of the Gorringe Hill-line, who outweighed him by fifty-one pounds. Old Hale was ill. Young Garrick ran the Scales. Sixty pounds of lead shot, sewn into the hem of the Tullow bed-linen in sealed pockets the night before, won the Chairmanship by nine pounds, and Garrick Tripe was Weigher by Plenty.

His second government was the Export Committee of 622, which kept the Renderers' Union in its monopoly against a Board tax by a margin of fourteen pounds. His third was the Steadiness Appropriation of 637, which doubled the Cullmasters' budget and lengthened the dusk-shift lists across Low Tilth. It passed by eleven pounds, all of them lead, sewn into the ample smock of Deacon Rolle Dimsworth, who had been paid to vote yes and was too light to carry it alone. Then the Long Count began to circulate in Sated, copied on greasy paper and pinned in the Scale Rings, and he read the figures for Low Tilth for Carving 637 and 638 and 639, and stopped telling it.

#### Their Place in the Land

The Weighing is the Fatlands' election, coronation and holy day in one, and Garrick Tripe is its priest. The Weigher reads the beam and cries the figure, and the clerks write it into the Book of Weight, and what he cries is law. Nobody has ever appealed a Weighing and won.

He depends on the Board's appetite for advantage, and on the Carriers who load the pans and are paid to look away. He rents his skill to anybody: Gut-line houses who want a committee seat, deacons who want to cross the threshold to Member, merchants who want a Board ally heavier than his rivals'. His fee is ten lacks a pound, cash or scrip, more for discretion, double for adding weight to an enemy's pockets in the form of a sudden, unaccountable *lightness*, which is more delicate work, since nobody wants to be seen to have lost weight. He is, in the narrowest sense, the most powerful man on the Board who has no vote.

#### What He Carries

- **The Weigher's brass key** — a foot long, worn on a chain under his coat, which unlocks the beam-housing of the Great Scales. There is one other copy. The Chairman keeps it.
- **The lead-ledger** — a small calf-bound book in cipher: every pound of lead he has ever sewn, poured or slipped into a pan, with names, dates and fees. It is the most dangerous document in the Fatlands after the Weighhouse cellar records, and it is his insurance.
- **A pouch of lead shot** — fine, soft, grey as a dead eye, dusted with flour so it will not click.
- **Twelve rings** — including a seal-ring from the Tullow house (608), a Renderers' Union ring of salt-cured bone (622), and a plain iron band he bought himself in 637 and does not take off, for reasons he will not explain.
- **A hollow counterweight** — a brass weight of the Great Scales, bored out and plugged with tallow, which can be swapped for its twin in the time it takes to sneeze. He keeps it in a velvet bag like a pet.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

Garrick wants to retire rich, to a house on the Little Gravy with a south-facing kitchen and a cook who sings, and to die fat and be eaten well.

He fears the Chairman. Tullow knows exactly how much of his vote is lead, because Tullow paid for the first sixty pounds of it, and Tullow has the other brass key. For thirty-three years the two of them have kept each other's secret: the Chairman's seat was bought, and the Weigher bought it. Now Tullow is nineteen hundred pounds, needs no lead, and is rooted through his dray, and Garrick has begun to understand that he is no longer a partner but a loose end.

**A secret he keeps:** at the Weighing of 641 Garrick took no fees. None. He weighed the Board honestly for the first time in his career, out of something he has not named, and the result moved the inner committee by two seats against the Cullmasters. Nobody noticed except Varn Sweetbread, who checked the arithmetic and sent Garrick a polite note of congratulation on his accuracy.

**A secret he does not know:** Tullow intends to expose him at the Weighing of 642, publicly, with the second key, and to void every vote cast since 608 by any member whose weight Garrick touched. That would unseat a third of the Board and leave Tullow, the honest giant, the only clean scale in the house. The Chairman only needs Garrick alive long enough to stand trial. Jem Crackle has been asked to be ready afterward.

#### Ties

- **Chairman Obb Tullow** — partner in the original sin; holder of the other key; the man who will end him.
- **Varn Sweetbread** — owes him eleven pounds of appropriation and knows Garrick has stopped selling them.
- **Mistress Brisket Annalow** — an old client; they still drink together, and she would sell him in an afternoon.
- **Jem Crackle** — has cooked for Garrick's suppers twice, without killing anyone. Garrick tips well and talks too much.
- **Nell Haslet, the Chairman's Washer** — he pays her for gossip from the dray. She tells him less than she tells the Count.
- **Lord Chastain Loin** — a current client: Loin wants a committee seat in 642 and has ordered a hundred and ten pounds.

#### In Play

PCs meet Garrick at a supper in Sated, at the Weighing festival, or when a patron hires them to find out why his rival is suddenly heavier. He wants from them either a delicate job (getting the lead-ledger to a safe place outside the Fatlands, or getting a pound of lead into a Gut-line's linen) or, as his fear grows, protection, which he will never ask for directly.

- **The Light Member.** A Board member has lost forty pounds overnight and is screaming fraud. Garrick did not do it. Somebody else has learned his trade, or the member is being eaten from within.

#### Who They Are

She is tall for a Gut-line, perhaps seven hundred pounds carried high and smooth, like a pear in silk: sleek, plump, upright, with a long neck she is vain about and hair the colour of clarified butter lacquered into a high glossy coil. Her skin is very pale and very fine and slightly translucent at the temples, where a blue vein shows like a thread through a consommé. She smells of bay, gelatine, white pepper and cold rooms. Her laugh is cold and musical, a little run of notes like a spoon drawn along a row of glasses, and she uses it in place of disagreement. Her kitchens are spotless and silent. Her staff wear felt slippers.

#### Their Story

Aspic was the second daughter of House Dellamore, a middling Gut-line with an estate of cold-cellars and ice-houses on the north bank of the Great Gravy, and its heir was her elder sister, Lady Galantine. Aspic was sent to learn cookery from Master Anselm Lardner, the finest preserving-cook in Sated, in the expectation that she would marry a Renderer and be useful. She learned from him how a Gut-line body, which refuses to finish hungering, can be kept by cold and gelatine far longer than meat ought to keep. In 627, at twenty-nine, she set her sister.

She did it in the ice-house over three days, with Galantine drugged on poppy and marrow-brandy, and when it was finished the heir of House Dellamore stood in a tall glass case in the family hall, perfectly preserved, garnished with chervil and quail's eggs, and *blinking*. Aspic set Master Lardner in 629. Then the restaurateur Lorrimer Bastable, who beat her in the pie-contest at the Weighing of 630; then Dame Orla Crisp, a dining critic who had called her sauces "frightened"; then five others. Her Gallery has nine pieces now.

#### Their Place in the Land

Lady Aspic is the south's arbiter of taste. A Gut-line house that wants to make an impression has her cater it; a merchant who wants to be noticed sends her a gift of something rare to cook. Her jelly, sold by the jar through a discreet shop on the Ladle Steps, is the most expensive food in the Fatlands: it is clarified from the trimmings and seepage of the Gallery, and it carries a little of the Gut-line hunger of whoever it came off, and it blinks. Board members eat it at the Weighing for luck. The Rim Market lists it at thirty lacks.


#### What She Carries

- **The silver ladle** — her teacher's, taken from his hand before she set him. Lardner watches her use it from his case.
- **A measuring tape** — of oiled silk, marked in inches, pounds and setting-hours. She has been measuring Dame Suet Marrable at the Unending Dinner, discreetly, at the wrist, the throat and the circumference of the chair.
- **A little book of setting-times** — every piece, with dates, temperatures, and remarks on its expression ("Bastable sulking; Galantine reproachful as ever; Crisp improving").
- **A jar of her own jelly** — on a chain at her waist, tawny-gold, with a faint pulse in it. She eats a spoonful before difficult conversations. It is cut from Galantine.
- **A poppy-and-marrow cordial** — for guests who are to be set. Taint 1 Hunger; Gut + Endure vs Grim (18) or sleep for a night.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

She wants to be the greatest cook on the Table: not the best-liked, the *greatest*, the one whose dishes are remembered when the gods have stood up. She fears blandness above death, and has begun to fear it in herself, because she can no longer taste salt and every dish she makes this year has been sent back once.

**A secret she keeps:** Dame Suet Marrable has commissioned her. Marrable's feet have begun to grow through the floor of her hall, and she knows the Unending Dinner will not outlast a hostess who has become a hill. She has asked Aspic, privately, to set her at the head of the table when the time comes, so that she may preside forever, conscious and hungry, watching her guests eat. Aspic accepted. She is planning instead to set Marrable for the Gallery, as its centrepiece, and has already ordered the glass.

**A secret she does not know:** the pieces are rooting. Hair-fine white threads have crept from their feet through the jelly, through the cases' brass plinths, through the Gallery floor and into the cellar, where they have twined into one mat. When the nine of them Seat, they will Seat as one hill, and Dellamore House will be inside it.

#### Ties

- **Hob Gristle** — once tasted her jelly and named all nine pieces, aloud, at a supper. She has not forgiven him.

#### In Play

- **The Centrepiece.** Aspic needs an eight-foot case carried across Sated at night without anyone asking what it is for. The PCs carry it. They may notice, later, that it is exactly Dame Marrable's size.

If nobody intervenes, she sets Dame Marrable in Lack 642, and when the Dinner's guests find their hostess gone, the Unending Dinner ends; and some weeks later the Gallery floor of Dellamore House bulges, cracks and rises, warm and grassy, with ten faces in it.

#### Who They Are

He looks like the boy who turns your spit. That is the whole of his art. Jem Crackle is wiry, quick and unremarkable, about five and a half feet and two hundred pounds, which in Sated reads as underfed, with a cheerful, sweaty, snub-nosed face, red knuckles, cropped sandy hair, and burn scars to the elbow on both arms, laid over one another in shiny pink lacework from fifteen years of reaching into ovens. He smells of onions, hot iron, singed hair and bay leaf. He whistles while he works, badly. He moves around a kitchen the way water moves around a pot, never in anyone's way, always where the knife is.


#### Their Story

Jem was born in 614 in the Spit Rows of Sated, the kitchen-quarter behind the Weighhouse where the great houses' cooks live, and was sold at eight to the kitchens of Marrable Hall as a spit-boy. He spent nine years turning joints in front of the great ranges that feed the Unending Dinner, and so saw, at eleven, his first guest carried down to the kitchen from the hall, dead of a burst stomach between the fish and the fowl, and jointed by the under-cook while the Dinner went on above, and sent back up as the fowl. Nobody screamed. The under-cook winked at him and gave him the oysters from the back.

In 631, at seventeen, he killed a man for the first time: a Board clerk who had come down to Marrable's kitchens to collect a debt from the under-cook, and who had pushed Jem into the range. Jem cut him at the chopping-block, quickly and quietly, and then, because there was nothing else to do with him and the Dinner was waiting, cooked him. The clerk went up that evening as a fricassee. Dame Marrable sent down her compliments. A week later a Gut-line footman came down with a purse and a name, and Jem's career began.

He left Marrable Hall at twenty. Since then he has worked for the Board, the Union, the Second Table, rival Gut-line houses and once, memorably, for a widow who wanted her husband served to his mistress. He owes nothing to anyone.

#### Their Place in the Land

In the Fatlands murder is a crime, and the punishment is to be rendered in the Renderers' yards with the victim's family taking the first cut. But a death in a kitchen, followed by a beautiful meal, sits in a gap in the law no court has closed. The body has been eaten, which is proper. The family has been fed. The Feasting Laws are honoured. Jem's victims' families often do not complain, because the dish was so good, and because complaining would mean admitting they ate it.

So Jem is an institution: the polite, deniable way Sated removes people. His fee is two hundred lacks for a commoner, a thousand for a member of the Board, payable half before and half after tasting. He needs a kitchen, which means the target must be lured into one, and so he works with housekeepers, cooks and stewards all over the Crown Ring, who know that a fat purse and a quiet word mean they will be given a holiday. He works nowhere else. He is the reason that in great houses in Sated nobody of importance ever goes into the kitchen.

#### What He Carries

- **A spit-iron** — a yard of black iron with a wooden grip, heated in any range: his favourite tool and his oldest. It closes what it opens.

He fears boredom, and has begun to fear his own appetite, which has been growing since Carving 640, when he tasted the Chute-meat a client had served and could not stop eating it.

**A secret he keeps:** the Bill of Fare. Each page is a lever on a family: proof that they ate their own, or ate an enemy, or ate a Board member's son. **A secret he does not know:** he has been asked, through a Board steward, to be ready to kill the Weigher Garrick Tripe after the Weighing of 642. #### Who They Are

He wears the blue sash of the Bailiffs over a vast white smock, and carries the funnel of office (polished horn, bound in brass) in a baldric like a sword, and the ladle of office (a deep pewter ladle on an iron handle) at his belt like a sceptre. Everybody in Sated agrees that Dunmow Fatt is gentle. He kneels to feed the stubborn. He married Clemency Haws, a seamstress, who died of a burst gut at the Harvest Home of 628, eating, laughing; and he served her at her wake with his own hands, and could not stop crying, and ate the most of anyone.

The Feasting Laws are the Fatlands' moral order, and Bailiffs are its parish constables, tax-men and priests. Dunmow's duties are many: to weigh children weekly and fine the parents of those who fail to gain; to inspect plates at inns and fine the waste; to attend wakes and see the dead eaten; to arrest those who refuse a meal; and to deliver the Thin to the Gavage. He answers to the Board's Ladle-Captain of Sated and through her to the Board. He depends on the goodwill of every household in his ring, and has it, because he is kind; and they depend on him not to look too hard. Marigold is slender for a Fatlander, perhaps two hundred and fifty pounds, freckled across every inch of her that the sun has touched, with a wide humorous mouth, faded red hair under a broad frayed straw hat, and the forearms of a woman who has climbed trees for forty years. Her voice is a strong, warm, slightly rough alto, the voice of a woman who has sung outdoors her whole life, and she uses it constantly: humming, singing under her breath, calling to the undergardeners in their hand-language and then singing the words anyway, because she can. She is the only Orchard-keeper in a generation who can hear, and she talks like it, too much, with the joy of a person in a country of the deaf.

#### Their Story

She was born in 591 in the keepers' cottages at the foot of the Old Grove, the most ancient rows of the Bleeding Orchards, whose trees are thick as barrels and bear fruit with a pit like a knuckle. Her mother was a keeper, deaf; her grandmother was a keeper, deaf; every keeper is deafened at fourteen with heated needles, the night before the first pruning they will work, so as not to go mad. At fourteen Marigold ran away the night before the needles, walked to Sated, and sang in taverns for two years. At sixteen she came home and climbed into the Old Grove alone at the first pruning of 607, with a hook and a pail, and pruned all day while ten thousand trees screamed around her, and sang over them, loudly, every song she knew. At dusk she climbed down bleeding from the ears, but not deaf. The Lodge let her stay.

She has pruned the Old Grove every Grace since, singing. In 634 the trees began to sing back: first a hum under the screaming, then a melody, then, by 637, the words, in her own voice, a thousand Marigolds at once, slightly out of time with one another, like a round. The other keepers cannot hear it but feel it in their teeth and their breastbones and the soles of their feet. They have begun to stay away from the Old Grove at pruning time. She does not mind.

#### Their Place in the Land


What the Lodge does not say is that the deafened keepers are the Thin's oldest allies. A keeper cannot overhear, and so cannot testify, and the Orchards' long rows and sap-houses are where the Thin have met since the Rising. Marigold, who *can* hear, has kept the secret for thirty years. She leaves her sap-house unlocked, her loft swept, and her windfalls by the gate. She has never fasted. She simply thinks the Thin are owed a dry place to sit.

#### What She Carries

- **The pruning hook** — her mother's, black with old sap. The trees know it, the keepers say. When she raises it, the nearest rows sway away.
- **A sap-pail** — copper, with a lid. The sap goes to the blood-pudding makers of Sated. She keeps a little back to lick, to know which tree has whom in its roots.
- **A bag of bone-pits** — from windfalls. She sucks the marrow while she works, and has begun to crunch the pits, which no one should be able to do.
- **The forbidden verses** — not written; held. She knows the whole of the song that the Board has twice tried to forbid, the song of the Meal whose last four words are *Is there any more?* She learned it from the grove.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

She wants to learn what the trees are trying to say. She wants a keeper after her who can hear. She fears she already knows the answer: the grove's song has begun to carry a last verse that she never taught it, and it is the wake-song, and the name in it is hers.

**A secret she keeps:** Wren Hollowell sleeps in her loft, on and off, and when the Fasting Girl walks through the Old Grove the trees stop singing altogether, and hold so still that not a leaf moves, as they did, the song says, on the night of the Meal. Marigold has seen it three times. It is the only silence she has ever been afraid of.

**A secret she does not know:** the Old Grove grows from a hill, and the hill was a woman: Wenna Strake, the girl who walked across the Trencher with a flint sickle and made the first cut. She Laded and Seated unrecorded in the first century, among her own trees. The College lost her line. Marigold's mother's mother was a Strake. The grove sings in Marigold's voice because it is the same voice; and it has been waiting six hundred years for one of its own to come and sing it the rest of the song.

#### Ties

- **Wren Hollowell** — her guest, whom she does not kneel to.
- **Corder Lank** — an old friend who uses her sap-house and has never once asked her to fast.
- **Old Mother Rind** — an orchard-hill herself; Marigold walks out to Kettle Furrow once a year to sing to her pear trees, which do not sing back.
- **Mercy Sowerby** — the Hill-Speaker came to the Orchards to ask whether the trees speak the older language the Dowagers have begun to use. Marigold said no. That was a lie.
- **Bailiff Dunmow Fatt** — buys her fruit for the Gavage; she charges him double and gives the difference to the Thin.
- **The Keepers' Lodge** — her employers, who are, slowly, pressing her to accept the needles.

#### In Play

PCs meet Marigold at pruning time, when the screaming of the Orchards is audible for miles and only one human voice can be heard above it, or when the Thin hide them in her sap-house. She wants from them a pair of ears: a witness, someone else who can hear what her grove is saying and tell her she is not mad.

- **The Last Verse.** The PCs hear the grove sing the wake-song with Marigold's name in it. She asks them to help her find out what it means. The answer is in the Trencher, and the Board forbids entry.
- **Raid on the Sap-House.** Bailiffs come for the Thin in Marigold's grove at pruning time. The only people who can hear them coming are Marigold and the PCs.
- **The Needles.** The Lodge has voted. Marigold will be deafened on the first day of Grace. She asks the PCs to stop it, or to sit with her while it is done.

Threatened, Marigold climbs: into the grove, where the branches lean to hide her and the keepers will not follow. Kindness she repays in fruit and song. Of the god she says, *it's only listening, dear, the same as you*. If nobody intervenes, she stands still at the end of the pruning of 642 in the heart of the Old Grove, singing the last verse with the trees, and does not climb down, and the roots go down from her bare feet, and by Carving there is one more tree in the oldest row, freckled in the bark, with a voice.

#### Who They Are

He came over the Rim thin as a rake, all wrists and collarbones and blue fingertips, with the reddish straw hair and the downy forearms of the Unfed. A year later he is filling out, and it is horrible to watch, because he does not look like a Fatlander growing heavy. He looks like a starved man being *stuffed*: the new flesh sits on him unevenly, soft at the belly and the jowl while his hands stay bony, and his skin has taken on a warm brown loam-tint that grows darker at the elbows and the backs of the knees, where, last month, the first pale green shoot appeared. He smells of bread and turned earth and, underneath, of pear-drops, the starved Unfed smell that has not quite gone. He is ashamed, delighted and terrified, often in one mouthful.

#### Their Story

He was born in 617 in the hearthless town of Wanting and named *Spare Moss* on the day he refused the breast. In Carving 640, after his sister was omitted from the Pantry list and died in the Lean House, he walked south down the Hungry Lane to the Rim with nothing but his flask. On the Fatlands verge of the Road, on the third night, a drover with a herd she would not look back at sat him down by her fire, broke a strip of salt-fat in half, and offered it. *No, thank you,* he said. *No, thank you. No, thank you.* She offered it a fourth time. He took it.

He asked what it was. "Wether," said Sal Trotter, meaning mutton, which it was not. He asked her name for him, because the Unfed are named on the day of their first refusal and he felt he had been born again on the day of his first acceptance. She gave him her son's, Tobias, because it was the first that came. He has been Tobias Wether since. The Seasoning took him in three meals: the first at her fire, the second at an inn in Gristmoot, the third at a wake in the Little Gravy parishes where he ate the dead with both hands and could not stop. The Cutwrights' College measured him in Lack 640: Cut 1, of Ummer's line, which by the ledgers is impossible. Odo Venn of the College tasted his blood, went pale, and did not report the flavour.

#### Their Place in the Land

He is a celebrity and a specimen. The Second Helping took him in at Gladbelly as a living sermon: a man of the refusing people who came to the table and was filled, proof that the god wants everyone. Brother Glut embraces him daily. The Reckoners want him dead, as an abomination and a precedent. He lives at Gladbelly because it is the only place where everyone wanting him at once keeps any one of them from taking him.

#### What He Carries

- **A ration-cloth** — the square of linen the Unfed carry their one meal in. He keeps it folded in his breast. He cannot bring himself to use it, or throw it away.
- **Sal Trotter's tin** — she left it with him when she walked on. It still smells of salt-fat. He licks the lid at night.
- **A Hostwatch frost-marker** — a chalk stick that changes colour on ground where the Host has passed, brought from the Fast. On Fatland soil, around his bed, it has twice gone the colour of a bruise.
- **A place card** — stiff and cream, his name spelled correctly, found under his plate at Gladbelly. Nobody at Gladbelly wrote it. Wherever he sleeps, by morning, a table is laid.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

He wants to be forgiven, by the Fast, by his dead sister, by himself, and he wants another plate. He fears going home, and fears more that he will never be let go home. He fears the dream: a long, dim room with a table running down it into the dark, and a chair at the far end, pulled out, and himself walking toward it, closing every door behind him.

**A secret he keeps:** he eats the soil. Not from the Appetite (he is not that far along) but because Fatland food has stopped satisfying him at all. Only earth from the edge of the Rim, where Fatland soil meets the Fast's, quiets him, and he walks out at night with the frost-marker to find it.

**A secret he does not know:** what is growing in him is not only Ummer. The ledgers are right; an Unfed stomach should not hold a god. It holds something that was *waiting* for a place to be laid. When he sets one more place at a table than there are diners, he is not being polite. He is being a host. The Reckoner called Scour, a hundred miles north, has started to do the same thing, at the same hour.

#### Ties

- **Sal Trotter** — fed him and named him for her culled son; she is the only person whose approval he wants.
- **Brother Glut** — loves him, adores him, would like to share a meal *of* him one day.
- **Corder Lank** — wants to fast with him, to see if the god will leave a man who has never had one before.
- **Dame Suet Marrable** — has his card on her cellar table.
- **Wren Hollowell** — he has heard of the girl who is never hungry and thinks of her the way a drowning man thinks of dry land.
- **Scour, the Returned** — a stranger he has never met who dreams the same room.

#### In Play

PCs meet Tobias at Gladbelly, at a wake, or on the Rim Road fleeing one party or another. He wants from them protection, food, and above all an answer: what is happening to him. He may be the focus of the thread *The First to Eat*.

- **Bring Him Home.** The Fast's Abstinent, through an intermediary, offer the PCs a fee to bring him back across the Hem. On the walk north, the dead grey soil sprouts in his footprints.
- **The Card.** Marrable's cellar steward invites the PCs to the Unending Dinner, and asks them to bring Tobias. His card is already on the table.
- **The Extra Chair.** At an inn, Tobias lays an extra place. In the morning the place has been used.

Threatened, Tobias bolts, and is very good at being quiet. Kindness he takes, once he has refused it three times, and is overwhelmed. Of the god he asks questions endlessly and believes none of the answers. If nobody intervenes, he and Scour vanish from their guarded rooms on the same night in Lack 642, and two tables are found on the Rim at dawn, laid, with their names on the cards and the chairs pushed in.

#### Who They Are

Seen from the village, it is a scarecrow: a tall thin shape of sacking and straw on a pole in the far field, arms spread on a crossbar, in a smock and a wide-brimmed hat, its back to Kettle Furrow, facing the open plain. It is seven feet high. Closer, it is wrong in ways that accumulate. The stake is not driven into the ground; the ground has grown up around it, in a little mound, like a lip. The straw is toothed wheat, and every ear is closed. The sacking is warm, and gives under the hand like a full belly, and is damp. The smock is a white field-smock of the dusk-shift, gone grey with weather, with a plot-number stitched at the breast. Under the hat, the face is a painted sack, the paint old and very good, smiling. Under the smile, if you lift the hat, is a mouth.

It has never been seen to move. It is never where it was.

#### Their Story

The first Kettle Furrow knew of it was Carving 629, the autumn after Pell Hogget scratched his first tally on his barn wall. The far field had been worked at dusk that season, by the Cullmasters' line, and in the morning it was standing there, a quarter-mile out, its back to the houses. Nobody had put it up. The farmers assumed a Cullmaster's clerk had, as a marker, and left it alone, because you leave alone what the Board does. Each Carving since it has been a few furrows closer: eleven furrows in 630, fourteen in 633, thirty in 638. In Carving 640, at dusk, a farm-girl walking home along the headland looked back and saw it facing her. It had turned around. In the morning it had its back to the village again, but it was facing, now, very slightly to the left: toward Hogget's Barn.


#### Their Place in the Land

The village has built its life around it the way a village builds its life around a river that floods. Nobody works the far field alone. Nobody looks away from it at dusk; in Carving, a child is set to watch it from the barn roof in shifts, with a bell, and if the bell rings everyone in the village comes out and stares at it until full dark. The Cullmasters have stopped sending dusk-shifts to that plot. Their ledgers record its yield as the highest in Low Tilth. The Board's Wolf-Warden reports a den of wolves in the far field each autumn. One handful of its straw, taken by such a dare, will sell at the Rim Market for thirty lacks, because scattered in a field it shows which person will be Reaped there next dusk.


#### What It Carries

- **A dusk-shift smock** — white linen, a plot-number at the breast: *KF-31*. The Cullmasters' register for Carving 637 lists plot KF-31 against the name of Nell Hogget, Pell's wife.
- **A wide hat** — a farmer's straw hat, the band stuck with wheat-teeth, adult teeth, each one a different colour.
- **A ribbon** — red, tied round its right wrist: the strip of cloth a Cullmaster unrolls at the end of a row, to show a worker which side of it to stay on.
- **Its stuffing** — straw, packed meat, hair, fingernails, small bones, a wedding ring, a silver-filled molar, a boot-lace still tied in a double knot. Pulled out, the stuffing is warm and sorts itself, slowly, into piles.
- **A mouth** — under the hat. It is full of names. It says them at dusk, in the voices that owned them, to anyone close enough.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

It wants to reach the village, and every year it is closer. It fears nothing anyone has found. It does not move while watched, and it has, perhaps, learned that a village counting its dead is a village watching.

**A secret the GM may reveal:** the Scarecrow is walking toward the Weighhouse, by way of every village that has stopped counting. It is slowed only where the Long Count is kept. Where a village gives up its tally, the next Carving the Scarecrow is a mile further on, in that village's far field, and nobody put it up. There are already three in Low Tilth.

**A secret the Scarecrow's maker does not know:** nobody put it up, but somebody *dressed* it. In the smock of her own culling, Nell Hogget is standing in the far field, more or less, and walking home. When it reaches the barn, it will put its mark on the wall.

#### Ties

- **Pell Hogget** — the Count holds it back; the Count is its path; the barn is its destination.
- **Varn Sweetbread** — has walked its field and bowed to it, and recorded its yield.
- **Wolf-Warden Crust Leggat** — reports it every year as a wolf-den, and has begun to find tracks leading from it.
- **Granny Cracknel** — its hat-teeth match sets on her wires. She has asked for the hat.
- **Tansy Lard** — her root at home, which says her name at dusk, has started to turn its leaves toward the far field.
- **Wren Hollowell** — the only living person who has walked up to it at dusk and touched it, on a dare from the Thin. It did not move. It held perfectly, perfectly still.

#### Who They Are

Ivo Stockpot is a portly, fussy, deeply honest man of six hundred pounds, with a bald freckled head that he polishes with a handkerchief when nervous, which is always, spectacles on a ribbon, a quartermaster's buff coat with too many pockets, and a tremor in his left hand that began in 636 and has not stopped. The Board's brass stamp hangs on a cord round his neck and bumps on his belly when he walks. He smells of brine, ink, cooperage-pitch and the peppermint he chews for his stomach. He says *son* and *dear* and *now then*, apologises before contradicting anyone, and is never wrong about a number.

#### Their Story

He was born in 580 in Gristmoot, a cooper's son, and joined the Board's commissariat at seventeen as a ration-clerk for the Bailiffs' levy and the Rim garrisons, the closest thing the Fatlands has to an army. He rose by being reliable. In 619 he was made Quartermaster of the Board's Provision: he feeds every Bailiff, Carrier and garrison soldier in the Fatlands, and stamps every export barrel that leaves by the Chute for the Board's tax, as Barrel-Warden, beside the Union's own stamp.

In Carving 636 a barrel split on the Chute ramp in front of him, and he saw what was in it, and recognised the face. It was a boy from Gristmoot he had taught his letters to. He stamped the next barrel, as he had to. Then he took out his penknife and cut a small notch under the stamp, where only a man looking would see it. *I would not, myself.* He has notched every barrel since that he would not eat from. His notch-ledger stands at three thousand, one hundred and nine.

#### Their Place in the Land

Without his stamp no barrel leaves the Chute legally; with it, any barrel can. He depends on the Union, who pack the barrels, and on the Board, who pay him, and both depend on him not to ask what the Dusk Acres' consignments are. Mistress Annalow knows about the notches and dislikes them, because a notched barrel is evidence and her second ledger is evidence enough. The Rim Road Company's tally-clerks have begun to notice them. A notched barrel was in the Vigil convoy that came to grief at Thimble Cross; thirteen of its forty-four, under Cullmaster Hedley Brawn's seal.

#### What He Carries

- **The Board's stamp** — brass, on a cord: a wheatsheaf in a circle and the word PLENTY.
- **A penknife** — bone-handled, very sharp, for notching.
- **The notch-ledger** — every notched barrel, by date, consignment and destination: the Vigil, Oathen, the Rim garrisons, Sallowreach's tallow-buyers. He has written beside some of them what he could tell through the bung.
- **A manifest-book** — the official one, in which every notched barrel is entered as *pork, salted, first quality*.
- **Army biscuit** — hard, dull, made with Rim-bought flour, the only food in the Fatlands he trusts. Every bite of it is a little fine for leaving Fatland food uneaten, if the Bailiffs knew.
- **The sealed list** — a copy of the ledger, sealed, addressed to nobody. He has been waiting for someone from outside to give it to.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

He wants someone outside the Fatlands to know. He fears that if they knew, they would keep buying. He fears that most of all, because it would mean the notch never meant anything.

**A secret he keeps:** the Board's own garrisons eat from the notched barrels. He has been feeding the soldiers on the Rim the Dusk Acres for five years, because the Board told him to keep the best for export. He has not eaten a garrison meal since.

**A secret he does not know:** the Vigil buyer who has fallen ill with something very like the Appetite is not the first. In Oathen, three buyers of Fatland flour have begun, in their sleep, to chew. Something in the notched barrels is not just meat. It is crop, and it is looking for soil.

#### Who They Are

Granny Cracknel is tiny, perhaps four feet and ninety pounds, which in the south is the size of a ghost, dusted white with flour from her cap to her slippers, so that she looks like something stored in a larder. Her face is a walnut. She has no teeth at all and her mouth folds in over the gums when she chuckles, which is constantly: a dry, high, delighted *hee hee* that runs under everything she says. She smells of flour, millstone, river-water and the faint copper of the old bloodstains on the mill floor. She moves around her mill with a staff, very fast, and the teeth on her wires clatter whenever she passes, like wind-chimes, like a crowd tutting.

#### Their Story

She was born Nell Cracknel in 548 at the mill, which her family has worked since the Tooth Harvest. At twenty, in 568, she went Thin, with a Low Tilth boy she loved, and was taken to the Gavage. The boxwood wedge broke every tooth in her head on the first day; she would not stop biting the funnel. She came out a season later, fat, silent, and toothless, and the boy had been fed until he died. She came home and started to keep the teeth from the sieve, which her father had always thrown in the river. She has kept them for seventy-three years.

Her mill has eleven hundred sets now, strung on wire in rows from the rafters, sorted by village and then by family, each set matched by her, tooth to tooth, until it makes a mouth. She knows that some of them match people she knew, because she knew their smiles. In 630 she found, for the first time, a set in the first milling of Carving that matched a boy who was still alive. He was Reaped eleven days later.

#### Their Place in the Land

Cracknel's Mill grinds the toothed wheat of four parishes along the Little Gravy, and by an old Board charter the miller keeps the teeth: a trifling right, the Board thought. She sells matched sets at eighty lacks to the bereaved, who hold them in the mouth for a night and dream the dead one's last meal and last sight; the last sight is always the same field at dusk. She bakes biting loaves for widows to set on their sills. Pell Hogget has bought eleven sets from her and checks her wires against his wall. The Cullmasters buy her predictions quietly. The Thin buy them louder. She sells to everyone, and chuckles.

#### What She Carries

- **A tooth-sieve** — brass mesh in an ash hoop, worn bright, the mesh exactly the size of a molar.
- **A pocket of teeth** — the morning's sieving, unsorted. She rattles them while she talks.
- **A basket of biting loaves** — for widows, five crumbs each, given free to the poor. After a week they grow a second row.
- **Her ledger** — names, villages, dates of milling and dates of Reaping, in a hand as small as seed.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

She wants every tooth to have its mouth. She wants, privately, her own boy's set, and has never found it, because the Gavage did not Reap him; it fed him.

**A secret she keeps:** in the first milling of Carving 641 she sieved a set that matched Pell Hogget: the chipped front tooth, the crooked canine he got falling off his barn roof as a lad. She has not told him. She has strung them on a wire of their own, by the door.

**A secret she does not know:** this Carving the sieve has given up more sets of the living than there are living in the four parishes. Some of the teeth are from people who have not been born.

#### Ties

- **Pell Hogget** — her best customer and her friend; his teeth are on her wire.
- **Bailiff Dunmow Fatt** — brings her bread each Grace, a kindness; she does not forgive the sash.
- **Corder Lank** — she was Thin before he was born, and tells him he does it wrong.
- **The Scarecrow** — she has asked for its hat. The teeth in the band are on her wires already, waiting for the rest.
- **Jem Crackle** — buys a pinch of her bone-flour for his spice mixture, and pays in gossip.

#### In Play

PCs come to her mill for a matched set, for a prediction, or to ask her what is in a Chute barrel's teeth. She wants news, company and new teeth; foreign teeth fascinate her.

- **The Wire by the Door.** The PCs see the new set strung apart, and she tells them whose, and asks them not to tell him.
- **Unborn.** The impossible sets. Granny asks the PCs to find out whose children these will be.

Threatened, she laughs, and the wires rattle, and the mill-race speeds up. Kindness she rewards with a loaf. The god she calls *the big mouth*. If nobody intervenes, she dies in her chair in Lack 642, and her family serves her at a fine wake, and the next morning there is a set of small, worn, broken stumps in the sieve.

**Secret she keeps:** she memorises Tullow's murmured figures and sends them to Pell Hogget, which is why the Long Count matches the Board's own. **Secret she does not know:** the cress is rooting her; there are threads in her soles, and they point toward the dray.

#### Ties

- **Chairman Obb Tullow** — her charge; he trusts her as he trusts a chair.
- **Pell Hogget** — her cousin and her cause.
- **Garrick Tripe** — pays her for gossip; she gives him little.
- **Pudding Hesk** — has asked her, kindly, how much the Chairman weighs today.

#### In Play

The PCs need someone close to the Chairman. Hooks: Nell needs a courier to Kettle Furrow; Tullow murmurs a PC's name with the figures; Nell does not come down from the dray one evening, and is found asleep on it with her feet in the folds. If nobody intervenes, she roots beside the Chairman by 643, and the Board agrees not to notice.

### The Three Most Explosive

**Garrick Tripe, Chairman Tullow and Jem Crackle.** The Chairman's seat was bought with sixty pounds of lead in 608, and the two men who know have kept each other's secret for thirty-three years. Tullow no longer needs Garrick; he needs a scandal that will void a third of the Board and leave him the only honest weight in the house, and a knife to tidy up after. Jem has been asked to be that knife and, separately, to kill Tullow himself, kindly, before the hill takes him. Pudding Hesk has been asked to cook the result of both. If the PCs warn any one of these four, the whole arrangement comes apart at once, in public, at the Weighing.

**Sal Trotter, Tobias Wether and the lead steer.** Sal culled her son for a debt by never looking back, and named a stranger after him, and walks to market a beast with his plot-number on its flank. Tobias wants Sal's approval more than food. The lead steer hums her song a beat ahead of her. When the steer asks its question, Sal will turn around; when Tobias learns whose name he wears, he will go looking for the herd; and a man with an empty room inside him and a beast made of a culled boy will meet on the Drove Road at dusk.

**Dunmow Fatt, Sorrel Fatt and Callow Pudge.** The gentlest Bailiff in Sated does not know his daughter is Thin. The gentlest feeder in the Gavage leaves one door unbarred each Lack. Corder Lank's cells have marked Ruddle Hollowell for this year's door, and Sorrel is the messenger. If Sorrel is caught, her father takes her to the chair, and Callow feeds her, and the door Callow leaves open will be the one Dunmow is standing in.
