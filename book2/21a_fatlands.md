# The Fatlands: A Country at Table

> Eat while it's warm. It won't be.
>> — Fatland proverb, stitched on samplers over every hob from Sated to the sea

> Five meals a day by law, and a sixth for the nerves, and a seventh for the dead. And still you wake in the night with your mouth open.
>> — Dr. Emmerich Paunch, *Notes Toward a Measure of the Southern Appetite*, unpublished, 640 A.G.

[fiction]
Firstbite is at the fourth bell, before light, and Dilly Murch is up for it because the hunger is up for it. It is always up first. She lies on her pallet in the back room on Tallow Stair with her mouth already working on nothing, the taste of soil on her tongue from a dream she cannot remember, and listens to the whole of Sated waking in the dark around her: four hundred thousand bellies grumbling in their beds like a field of frogs.
Her mam has the pot on. The pot is always on. It has not been off since Gran's wake, nine years ago, and there is a scrap of Gran in it still, the family says, somewhere down in the black crust at the bottom where the spoon cannot reach. Dilly eats standing: a bowl of dripping-porridge with a skin on it, two slices of fried bread with the teeth sieved out, a cup of well-broth that tastes of beef and pennies. She thanks Granny Hessop's Rise, because that is the family field, and she thanks Long Acre, because the flour came from there, and she thanks her Gran, because the pot is Gran's. She wipes the bowl with the last of the bread. She is still hungry. Of course she is.
She is a Washer, second grade, in the Fold. She has a bucket of warm water with skim floating on it, a pail of lye-soap, a bag of soft sponges, a long-handled brush like a chimney-sweep's, a tin of zinc salve, and a pair of tweezers on a ribbon round her neck, and she walks to work through streets that are already crowded and already eating. The cookshops along Ladle Lanes never shut. The tallow lamps over them have been burning all night and the light is the colour of a cooked onion. A man at a brazier is turning sausages as thick as a child's wrist and singing to them. A Bailiff in a blue sash stands at the corner with his ladle of office, counting the plates going by. A drunk is asleep face-down on the warm cobbles, and the cobbles, Dilly would swear, are rising and falling a little beneath him, like a chest.
Her lady is Madam Gorrel Fenn, of the Fenns of the Fold, who weighs eleven hundred pounds and has not left her bed in four years. The house is all ramps and double doors and the floors are joisted like a bridge, and the smell when the Carriers open the bedroom is the smell of every Laden bedroom on the plain: warm loam, rosewater, old milk, and underneath, very faint, the smell of a field after rain. Dilly loves the smell. She is ashamed of how much.
The Carriers turn Madam on her great quilted pallet with ropes and pulleys, four of them heaving and grunting and saying *up she comes, beauty, up she comes*, and the flesh rolls over like a landslide in silk, and Dilly goes in with the sponge. She washes the folds one by one, lifting each with the brush-handle, sometimes with both arms. Inside the folds the skin is darker, red-brown and mottled like turned soil, and soft as proving dough, and it keeps the shape of her fingers for an hour. In the deeper creases, behind the knees and under the great overhang of the belly, there are the shoots. Little green threads, pale at the base. They come up every night. Dilly plucks them with the tweezers, one by one, and drops them in a dish. Madam Fenn says *ah* at each one, softly, the way you say *ah* when a splinter comes out. Seventy-three this morning. Yesterday it was sixty-eight. Dilly does not write the numbers down, because that is what the Long Count people do and she does not want the Bailiff looking at her, but she remembers them. She cannot help it.
At Elevenses Madam is fed by her daughters, who take it in turns. At Midday Dilly eats in the kitchen with the Carriers: pie with a crust like crackling, a bowl of veined turnips in butter, a wedge of orchard-fruit with the bone pit left in for sucking. Old Wem the head Carrier tells the joke about the word for *full*, and everyone laughs, as they always do, with their mouths full.
In the afternoon Madam's feet need doing. Dilly does not like the feet. They are on an iron board at the end of the pallet, because iron is the only thing the roots will not take, and the soles are matted with fine white threads that have spent the night trying to find the floor. They have worked into the seams of the iron like hair into a comb. Dilly teases them free with a bone pick and a dab of salve and wipes them, and they curl round her fingers, warm and blind and searching, like the hands of a baby. One of them, this afternoon, does not let go. It wraps her smallest finger twice and squeezes. Dilly sits very still. Madam Fenn, far away up the slope of herself, says in a dreamy voice: *You're a good girl, Dilly. You're a heavy girl. You'd make a lovely bit of ground.*
At Fourth Plate the Bailiff comes round to see the house is keeping the Laws. At Supper there is roast. On the way home in the blue dusk Dilly passes the Weighhouse, under its dome of glazed bone, and she stops, as everyone stops, at the iron grating in the pavement on Scale Street where you can feel the warm air coming up from the cellars. It smells of earth and gravy. If you put your ear to the grating you can hear, far down, something like a slow tide, something like a great man breathing through his nose while he chews. People say it is the Chairman. People say a lot of things.
She goes home. She has a bowl from the pot. She lies down with her mouth working. And at the second bell of the night she wakes, hungry, with the taste of soil on her tongue, and the smallest finger of her left hand, where the root held it, itching and itching and itching, as though something had been planted there.

>> — told to a Cutwright surveyor in Sated, Lack 640; the surveyor noted that the girl's left little finger "showed a fine pale filament at the nail-bed, which she bit off while we spoke, and ate"

This chapter goes down to the ground. The core book told you what the Fatlands are: the land that ate Ummer the Laden, where everything is meat and nobody is ever full, where the fields take people at dusk in Carving and the great grow into hills. Here we walk the streets and furrows, price the bread, sit in the courts, kneel at the field-shrines, and listen to what ordinary people say when the Bailiff has gone. Then we meet, at length, the people who hold the country up and the people it is slowly eating.

Remember three things when you run the Fatlands, and everything else follows. First, it is *warm*. It is comfortable. It is the most hospitable land on the Table, and the horror arrives buttered and smiling and asking if you would like more. Second, everybody is always eating, in every scene, as a background noise like weather. Third, everyone knows. There is no conspiracy of silence in the Fatlands, only a conspiracy of politeness, which is stronger.

## Districts and Settlements

### Sated: The City Built Around a Scale

Sated sits on the Great Gravy, the slow brown Brawn, in nine rough rings around the Weighhouse. The rings are not walls; they are roads, broad as fields, laid down as the city swelled outward, and each was paved in turn with stone that has since sunk, tilted and been relaid so often that the oldest streets ripple like a quilt thrown over sleepers. Nothing in Sated is tall. The city is built low and wide on purpose, because height means stairs and stairs mean that the great cannot reach you, and in Sated to be beyond the reach of the great is to be beyond the law. Every building is a hall, every door double, every upper floor reached by a ramp of packed brick. From the Rim Road on a clear evening the city looks like a heap of loaves left to cool on a board, smoking.

It is never quiet and it is never dark. Tallow lamps burn on every corner all night, and street-cooks work under them, and the Bailiffs walk the rings counting plates. The drains, which are open stone gutters down the middle of every street, run warm with dripping and drip and dishwater down to the river, and the whole city smells of gravy at every hour: brown, rich, salt, faintly burnt, the smell of a kitchen on a feast-day that never ends. Visitors stop noticing it on the third day. On the tenth day they find they are always hungry.

#### The Scales

The innermost ring around the Weighhouse, where the Board's clerks, scale-wrights, Cutwrights and Weighers live and work. Its streets are named for the parts of a balance (Beam Street, Pan Row, Fulcrum Yard, Scale Street) and its houses are faced with the same glazed bone tile as the dome, so that on a sunny day the whole district gleams like a plate of fat. The people are clerks and the great's servants: neat, plump, quiet, well-fed on Board rations, who walk with their hands folded on their bellies. It smells of ink, lamp-oil, and the warm air rising through the iron gratings set in the pavements, which open onto the Weighhouse cellars and breathe. Trade here is in paper: meat scrip is printed in Fulcrum Yard on greased sheets, and every lease and Weighing certificate on the plain is copied in Pan Row.

**Custom.** Nobody in the Scales whistles, because the Chairman, rooted under the dome, is said to dislike it, and the gratings carry sound down. **Danger.** The gratings. Children dared to put their arms down them come up with fine white threads round their wrists. In 639 a clerk who slept drunk on a grating was found in the morning rooted to it through the cheek. **Notable.** **Ambry Coil**, keeper of the scrip-press, who prints a little more scrip in heavy Reaping years on the Board's quiet instruction, and has noticed that the press's rollers, which are greased with Weighhouse dripping, have begun to grow a soft green fur.

#### The Fold

The great houses of the Gut-lines in town, in a ring of broad gardens and ramped mansions on the slight rise east of the Weighhouse. Here live the Laden families who still keep a seat in the city: the Fenns, the Gammons, the Chitterlings, the Loins, the Tullows' town-branch. Their houses are the size of granaries and built like them, with iron-floored inner bedrooms and drains under the beds. The Fold is the district of the **Carriers** and the **Washers**, who live in narrow tenements behind the mansions and come and go through the service ramps. It sounds like a hospital: ropes creaking in pulleys, soft voices, the great sighing in their sleep, and, on the hour, the bells that call the Carriers to turn their masters. It smells of rosewater, warm loam and milk.

**Custom.** On the night a Laden master roots for good, the household opens every window and the Washers put their sponges out on the sills, so the neighbours know. **Danger.** The gardens. Several Fold gardens contain a relative who was allowed to plant, and the grass in them is long and warm and not safe after dark. **Notable.** **Wem Haddle**, head Carrier of the Fenn house, sixty, enormous in the shoulders, who has turned the same mistress four times a day for nineteen years and calls her *beauty*, and who has begun quietly asking the Washers whether anyone knows how long it takes a hill to forget its Carrier.

#### Ladle Lanes

The cookshop district, west of the Scales, a tangle of narrow lanes (narrow by Sated's standard, which means two drays abreast) lined with ovens, braziers, spits, pie-shops, sausage-stalls, pudding-houses, gravy-sellers and dripping-merchants, all of them open all night. Here the poor of the city come to buy the meals the Feasting Laws require of them, and here the Bailiffs do most of their counting. The noise is constant: the hiss of fat, the cries of the sellers (*Hot, hot, toothless and hot! Pie of the parish, pie of the plain!*), the clatter of plates, a thousand mouths chewing. The smell is the smell of Sated concentrated to a syrup. The cobbles are slick with grease and sanded every morning.

**Custom.** The **ten-crumb plate**: by Board ordinance every cookshop must sell a full meal for ten crumbs, a lack, to anyone at any hour, so that no one in the city can claim poverty as a reason to miss a meal. The ten-crumb plate is mostly potato, gristle and gravy, and it is better than most of what is eaten in Oathen. **Danger.** Jem Crackle works kitchens, and Ladle Lanes is all kitchens. More ordinarily, the cookshops buy meat at night from the back doors of the Dusk Acres carts, unstamped, and you do not ask. **Notable.** **Mother Suckling**, who keeps the oldest pie-shop on the Lanes, the Bottomless Crust, and who has never once refused to buy meat at her back door or to say whose it was if asked kindly.

#### Wakeside

The jointers' and wake-cooks' quarter, south of the Lanes along a curve of the Brawn, where the trades of the dead are carried on. Coppersmiths here make the great rendering-coppers every family needs for a wake, and hire them out by the day; carpenters build the long boards on which the dead are laid; knife-grinders sharpen the jointers' blades on wheels of bone. It is a respectable, pious, house-proud district, full of whitewash and flower-boxes, and every second door has a painted sign showing a crossed knife and ladle and the words WAKES DONE DECENT. Wakemother Pudding Hesk keeps her kitchen here. It smells of applewood smoke and roasting and, on a busy day, of the particular sweet heavy smell that every Fatlander knows and no Fatlander names.

**Custom.** A jointer's apprentice is not let to carve a stranger until they have carved their own grandparent; families in Wakeside save their old folk for their apprentices. **Danger.** Grief. Wakeside has the highest rate of the Long Pig in the city, because its people are in rooms with good meat all day, and the Bailiffs patrol it with a peculiar watchfulness. **Notable.** **Corby Sallet**, a coppersmith, who rents out the copper that rendered Hester Gaunt after she burst in 455 and charges triple for it, and who has noticed it has begun, when it is heated, to sigh.

#### Gravy Wharf

The river docks along the Brawn, where the barges come up from the coast and down from the Chute, and where the city's drains empty into the Great Gravy through a hundred stone mouths. The river here is thick and warm and brown and moves very slowly, and its surface is a slick of floating fat that the **skimmers** gather with flat nets from little round boats and sell to the cookshops as skim. The wharfmen are broad, wet, cheerful, foul-mouthed Union men; the dockside inns are the only places in Sated where foreigners are common, and the only places where you can buy Rim bread, grey and joyless and safe, for those who must. It sounds of winches, oxen, shouting, the slap of fat on hulls. It smells of river, which is to say of gravy gone cold.

**Custom.** Every barge leaving the Wharf throws one loaf into the river as it casts off, for the Gravy, and the loaf never floats far. **Danger.** The river takes swimmers like the fields take labourers, more slowly: they lie back in the warm brown water and stop wanting to get out. Drowned men in the Gravy are found weeks later floating on their backs, smiling, enormously fat. **Notable.** **Hasp Wantley**, a skimmer, who fishes things out of the Gravy besides fat and keeps them in a shed: so far a boot with a foot in it, eleven wedding rings, and a cabbage with a face that he insists is his father.

#### The Leanings

The poorest ring, on the low ground west of the river, where the city's Scrapling labourers live in long timber tenements that lean on one another like drunks, because the ground beneath them is soft and gives a little more each year. The Leanings are crowded, warm, damp and loud. Every tenement has a shared pot in the yard, kept full by subscription, and the Bailiffs check it daily. The people are field-hands who walk out to the near acres each dawn, rendering-yard labourers, Carriers between houses, and the families of the dusk-shifted. It smells of boiled potato, wet timber, and the sharp ammonia of too many people. The Leanings is where the Thin recruit in Sated, and where the Long Count has its city copyists, and where the Bailiffs walk in threes.

**Custom.** **The Empty Bowl**: on the anniversary of a dusk-shifting, the family sets the culled person's bowl on the yard wall upside down. Some walls in the Leanings carry nine or ten bowls. **Danger.** The ground. Tenements in the Leanings do not fall; they sink, slowly, a storey a generation, and the bottom floors of the oldest are below the street and full of roots and warm dark and, it is said, people who went down to sleep there and stayed. **Notable.** **Sister Nettle Brack**, a Thin cell-leader of the rank of Gaunt, who runs a soup kitchen as a cover and serves broth so thin it is nearly water, and who is very good at seeming fat: she wears padding.

#### Registry Close

A walled quarter just north of the Scales where the cross-border powers keep their southern offices: the Cutwrights' College's southern registry, a squat bone-tiled hall whose vaults hold three centuries of weighing records; the Rim Road Company's Sated house, with its notary's bench and its strongroom of platters; and a dozen foreign merchants' halls, from a Vigil salt-beef buyer's lodge (lamps lit all night, which in Sated goes unnoticed) to an Oathen contract-house where nobody speaks above a whisper. Registry Close is the only district of Sated with stairs. The Fatlanders who work there are thin by local standards, sharp, and travelled. It smells of paper, wax, and the cedar perfume foreigners wear to cover the smell of the city.

**Custom.** Foreigners in the Close are permitted by Board licence to eat three meals rather than five, provided they pay the **Guest's Fine** of two lacks a day for the missing two. **Danger.** The Cutwrights. A registry surveyor will examine anyone they please under Board warrant, and a high reading means an invitation to the Fold that cannot be refused. **Notable.** **Surveyor Ottilie Vance**, of the College, who keeps the southern registry's projection of which Gut-line houses will Seat in the next decade, and has been offered, by three different families, enough money to retire on in exchange for moving their names down the list.

#### Funnel Street

The Bailiffs' quarter, on the city's southern edge on the road to the Gavage: barracks, a feeding-school, the Board's main granary, and the long whitewashed hall where Bailiffs are trained in the use of the ladle and the funnel. Bailiffs live here with their families, and it is the cleanest, kindest, most orderly district of the city, full of singing and well-kept gardens. It smells of porridge. Every morning the recruits practise on each other with warm water, choking and laughing.

**Custom.** A new Bailiff is given their ladle of office at a supper where they must feed their own mother by hand, and she must say *there's a good belly* when she has had enough. **Danger.** Being thin in Funnel Street is like being on fire in a granary. **Notable.** **Under-Bailiff Mossop Tench**, Dunmow Fatt's deputy, a young, efficient, unsmiling man who does not weep over prisoners and who keeps a list of every Bailiff who does.

### Beyond Sated: Towns and Villages of the Plain

The Fatlands have no walled towns and few large ones. Outside Sated the country is a quilt of villages, each of a few hundred souls, each surrounded by its fields, each field named and thanked at every meal. Villages are built in rings around a central yard where the common pot stands and the wakes are held. The houses are long and low and timber-framed and roofed with thick yellow thatch that must be replaced every year because it sprouts. The villages are joined by causeways of packed brick and bone laid over the soft ground, and between them is wheat, and wheat, and wheat, and the low green rises that are people.

#### Kettle Furrow (Low Tilth)

The village of Pell Hogget, of Hogget's Barn, of Old Mother Rind, of Tansy Lard and of the Scarecrow, and therefore one of the most visited small places on the Table. Kettle Furrow lies in the deep black heart of Low Tilth, in a fold of land so rich the wheat is red at the root, and it is quiet in the way of every Low Tilth village: well-fed, polite, and missing people. It has two hundred and eleven souls by the Board's census and a hundred and ninety-four by Pell's, and the difference is the Count. The houses have no locks. The doors stand open at night in Carving, because a closed door, people say, only means the field has to come round to the window. It smells of black soil, baking, and the faint vinegar-sweetness of Old Mother Rind's pears.

**Trade.** Wheat, the reddest and most toothed in the country, sold to the mills on the Little Gravy and stamped *Low Tilth Prime*. **Custom.** Every Carving evening the whole village eats Supper outdoors in the yard facing the far field, so that if anyone is Reaped, they will not be alone. **Danger.** The Scarecrow, of course, in the far field; and the pilgrims who come to Hogget's Barn and do not all go home. **Notable.** **Gaffer Tolly Mumbray**, the Wolf-Warden for the district, eighty, toothless, who files the annual wolf report and has written, for nineteen years, the single word *wolves* in the column for cause, and who has started crying when he does it.

#### Bramble Furrow (Low Tilth)

Wren Hollowell's birthplace, five miles from Kettle Furrow along the Tilth causeway, a ragged poor village on the edge of the Dusk Acres, where the Cullmasters' numbered stones begin. It has more empty houses than full ones, most of them belonging to families who were listed and walked the row together. Bramble Furrow is the village that sent the most names to the dusk-shift in the last ten years, and it knows why: it is poor, it owes, and it produced a Blank. The Board's agents and the Second Table's agents and three kinds of bounty-taker have all been through it looking for the girl, and the villagers have learned to answer every question about her with a plate of food. It smells of bramble-flowers, which here bloom pink and smell of ham.

**Trade.** Bramble-jelly, sold in Sated as a delicacy; brambles here grow thorns like small curved teeth and the jelly sets with a faint pulse. **Custom.** **Weighing the Babies**: every infant is weighed at the yard-pot on its first day and a pebble put in a jar for each pound, and the jars are kept on a shelf in the common house. Wren Hollowell's jar is still there, and it is fuller than the others, because her mother kept putting pebbles in to hide how little she ate. **Danger.** The Dusk Acres are a field away. **Notable.** **Gammer Hollowell**, Wren's grandmother, who has been questioned eleven times and fed by Bailiffs twice, and who says, every time, that her granddaughter died of the wasting at five and was served at a wake, and lists the dishes.

[quick] Gammer Hollowell — Threat 1 · Flesh 9 · Guard 10 · Attack +1 (knife 2) · Armor 0 · Dread 0 · Deceive 4; will not break

#### Gristmoot

The great market town on the Rim Road at the Fatlands' eastern gate, where the Road comes down out of the dry country of Oathen and meets the first green fields. Gristmoot is a Company town in all but name: inns, warehouses, stockyards, toll-houses, and the longest market on the Rim, three miles of stalls along the Road where Fatlands meat and bread are sold to every land. Mardy Haunch keeps her sausage-stall here, the widest on the Rim. It is loud, crowded, mercenary and cheerful, full of foreigners and foreign money, and it is the place where most travellers first taste Fatlands bread and first stop being able to leave. It smells of woodsmoke, sausage, horse, and, from the east, dust.

**Trade.** Everything that crosses the eastern border: meat and flour going out, sworn notes and Oathen silver and Vigil glass coming in. **Custom.** The **Stranger's Loaf**: every inn gives a newcomer a hot loaf free at the door, and the town takes a quiet wager on whether they will be out of Gristmoot in a week. The house usually wins. **Danger.** The Gristmoot cure-sellers, who sell foreigners "safe" food that is anything but, and the bounty-takers who work the Road looking for Blanks and Unfed. Kale Dragoman drinks here. **Notable.** **Toll-Reeve Bastable Crumb**, a Company man, Fatland-born, who weighs every foreigner who enters the town on a public scale at the toll-house and again when they leave, and posts the difference on a board outside. It is always a gain.

#### Barrelside

The town that has grown up around the Chute at the Fatlands' northern edge: a mile-long sprawl of coopers' yards, salting-sheds, rendering-houses, tanneries, stock-pens and the tenements of the people who work in them. Barrelside is Renderers' Union country absolutely; the Board's Bailiffs come here by invitation and the Union's own **yard-wardens** keep the peace. Its people are red-armed, salt-chapped, foul-mouthed, proud and well-paid by Fatland standards, and they have the most honest faces in the south, in the sense that nothing that happens there surprises them. It sounds of hammers on hoops, the bawling of stock, the roll of barrels down the long stone ramp, all day and all night. It smells, the whole of it, of brine and hot fat and blood, so strongly that on the Belch the smell reaches eleven miles and children in the next district are sent indoors.

**Trade.** Barrels. Everything that leaves the Fatlands by the Rim Road leaves here, in oak, stamped with an origin and then stamped over twice. **Custom.** A new Hook is initiated by being sealed in an empty barrel for a night in the salting-shed, and let out at dawn by the whole yard, who sing. **Danger.** The barrels that should not have been filled; the Stampers who know which; the people who do not want the Stampers to talk. **Notable.** **Ivo Stockpot**, Quartermaster, who stamps the export barrels, and has begun to notch some of them. #### Dimble

A dying village on the western plain between Sated and the Trencher, built around the ruins of a granary that was old when Ummer walked. Over the granary's root-cellar is the oldest carving in the Fatlands, the procession of the Providers, with the Eighth chiselled away and a little of the long open hand left where the chisel slipped. Dimble is poor and getting poorer; its fields are close to the Trencher and grow wild and fleshy and hard to cut, and its young people go to Sated and do not come back. It is visited by Cutwrights, College historians, Second Helping pilgrims on their way to the Trencher, and the occasional Second Table noble who comes at night to look at the hand and goes away pale. It smells of wild mint, which here grows furred, and old stone.

**Trade.** Very little: wild-grown roots from the Trencher's edge, which sell in Sated as a delicacy to those who like their food strong, and guiding. **Custom.** The people of Dimble do not say *Is there any more?* at the end of a meal, as Fatlanders do everywhere else as a pleasantry. They say it is unlucky, and will not explain. **Danger.** The Trencher is two miles off, and its creepers have been creeping. **Notable.** **Elder Gammet Rake**, keeper of the granary key, who lets visitors see the carving for a lack and will not let anyone stay in the cellar after dark, because, he says, the hand in the carving is longer in the mornings than it was the night before.

#### Sapwell (the Bleeding Orchards)

The chief village of the Orchards, in the middle of its ten thousand acres, a village of the deaf. Sapwell's orchard-keepers go deaf young with heated needles, by choice, so that they will not hear the trees scream at pruning, and the whole village speaks in a quick, expressive hand-language that outsiders cannot follow and the Thin have learned. The houses are built of pear-wood and apple-wood, which never quite dries and leaks a little sap in hot weather. In the pruning season the village is utterly silent to its people and utterly, unbearably loud to everyone else; outside the pruning season it is the quietest place in the Fatlands, and visitors find the quiet unnerving, because they can hear the trees breathing. It smells of blossom in Grace, fruit in Plenty, and blood in Carving.

**Trade.** Bleeding-orchard fruit; orchard sap, collected in buckets at pruning for blood-pudding and for the apothecaries; pear-wood. **Custom.** Children in Sapwell are allowed to hear until their tenth year, and on the night before the needles they are taken into the oldest grove and let to listen to the trees for an hour, so that they will know what they are choosing. **Danger.** Marigold Chine's grove, which sings. And the pruning hooks; the trees remember them. **Notable.** **Keeper Aldo Pippin**, sixty, deaf since nine, who leads the Orchard-keepers' quiet council and who has been sheltering Thin fugitives in the hollow trunks of the oldest trees for twenty years, and has begun to notice the trees closing over them a little more each season.

#### Millsop (on the Little Gravy)

A mill village on the Little Gravy east of Sated, where the toothed wheat of Low Tilth and the eastern plains comes to be ground. Seven water-mills stand on the slow brown stream, their wheels turning thick and dripping, and the oldest of them is Granny Cracknel's, its eaves strung with eleven hundred sets of teeth that rattle in the wind like a hailstorm. The millers of Millsop sieve the teeth from the flour, and the teeth are sold by the bushel for buttons, beads, dice, and children's necklaces. The village is white with flour and loud with water and the grinding of stones and the constant rattling of teeth. It smells of fresh flour and, faintly, of a dentist's.

**Trade.** Flour, stamped and sacked for the Chute; teeth. **Custom.** When a miller sieves a tooth with a filling in it, the sieve is stopped and the tooth is taken to the village yard and set on a stone, and anyone may come to look at it in case it is someone they knew. **Danger.** The mill-wheels have teeth now too, in the paddles, small ones, and the Little Gravy's eddies below the wheels are where the village's drowned are found. **Notable.** **Granny Cracknel**, who is in another part of this book; and her great-grandson **Wick Cracknel**, a sullen boy of fifteen who sieves the teeth and has started keeping the ones that belong to people who are still alive, in a tin, in case they want them back.

#### Gladbelly

The commune of the Second Helping, in a meadow beside the Little Gravy, already described in the core book: long open tables, continuous eating, singing, a slaughter-pavilion hung with garlands. What visitors notice first at ground level is the noise, which never stops: three hundred people at table, laughing and singing hymns about gravy, a fiddle somewhere, the clatter of plates. What they notice second is the children, of whom there are many, all plump and loved and well looked-after, and many of whom have no parents left. What they notice last, if they stay past dark, is the meadow, which is the lushest grass in the Fatlands, thick and warm and starred with little white flowers that open at night and smell of roasting. Brother Glut lives here, in a pavilion of his own.

**Trade.** Gladbelly sells nothing. It is fed by donations from a hundred households of the Second Helping across the plain, and by the Offered. **Custom.** Every newcomer is offered a second helping of everything, and the cult counts how many take it. **Danger.** Joy. The Second Helping does not need to compel anyone. **Notable.** **Plateholder Juniper Mallow**, who tends the Offered in their last week, bathes them in dripping and garlands them, and who keeps the shoes of every Offered in a long row along the back wall of the pavilion, because the children always ask for them.

#### Hessop's Rise

Not a village but a hill, three miles south of Sated beside the Gravy road, and worth a stop because it is the model of what a Fatland family hill is. Granny Hessop rooted in 512 and Seated in 549, and the Hessop family has farmed her ever since: her slopes are terraced with purple turnips and her crown is a little orchard. The family lives in a cottage at her foot and brings her the best cut of every wake. She has no mouth. She has, the family will tell you proudly, a heartbeat that can be felt by lying on her with your ear to the turf, once every minute, and on Tablenight it quickens. Dozens of families across the plain thank Granny Hessop's Rise at every meal, because her turnips are in every market in Sated. **Notable.** **Coll Hessop**, the present head of the family, who has started sleeping on his great-great-great-grandmother in the warm months, as his father did, and his father before him, and who has noticed that he wakes each morning a little nearer to her crown.

## Roads and Ways

The Fatlands are flat and soft, and travel across them is slow, warm, and never quite comfortable. The ground gives. Cartwheels sink. A loaded wagon on an unmade track in late Plenty can sink to the axles in an afternoon and have to be dug out, and the ruts it leaves behind are found next morning healed over and slightly raised, like a scar. For this reason the Fatlands are threaded with **causeways**: raised roads of packed brick and crushed bone, laid over beds of salt and iron filings, which the ground does not like and will not take in. The causeways are the arteries of the country. They are maintained by the Board's road-gangs, who are paid in scrip and fed at the roadside, and by the dusk-shifted, who work the causeway repairs in Lack when the fields are quiet.

**On foot**, a traveller on a causeway makes the usual twenty miles a day, but must stop five times for the mandatory meals, which in practice brings it nearer fifteen; a traveller off the causeways, across fields, makes ten at best, and should not be in a field at dusk in Carving. **By cart**, the causeways are good going, twenty-five miles a day, but Fatland oxen are slow and stop to eat whenever they are allowed and sometimes when they are not. **By barge**, on the Great Gravy and the Little Gravies, travel is slowest of all, perhaps twelve miles a day downstream and eight up, but it is restful, it is fed, and the bargemen will not let anyone off at dusk. The great travel by **dray**: a wide, iron-floored, eight-ox platform that moves at walking pace and is preceded by Bailiffs who clear the road.

Fatlanders reckon distance not in miles but in **meals**: a village is "two meals off" if a person setting out after Firstbite will arrive at Midday. The Board's mile-stones are carved with a plate and a number of notches.

[box: Travelling the Fatlands in Play]
Each day on the causeways, a Fatland journey is safe and fed and slow. Each day off them, roll Lung + Wayfaring (Hard 14). On a failure, the party loses half a day to the soft ground; on a Lack, a cart, an ox or a person sinks to the waist and must be dug out (Gut + Labor, Hard 14, an hour's work, and Dread 1 for what the spade turns up). Travellers who skip the mandatory meals on a Board road are likely to meet a Bailiff (one in three chance each day) and will be fined, fed, or both. In Carving, the GM should ask every evening where everyone is at dusk, and make the players answer.
[/box]

**Inns.** Every village has an inn, and every Fatland inn is the same in its essentials: a long low hall, a fire, a pot that is never empty, benches wide enough for two Rim travellers or one Fatlander, and a landlord or landlady who will feed you until you cannot move and then express concern that you have not eaten. A bed with five meals costs three lacks. A foreigner who asks for less food will be charged the same. The inns on the Board roads post the Feasting Laws on the door, and Bailiffs inspect them. **Waystations** of the Rim Road Company, at Gristmoot, Barrelside and on the southern Rim, are the only places in the Fatlands where a traveller can eat three meals rather than five without a fine, because the Company has bought an exemption for its guests at a price the Board has never disclosed.

**The Drovers' Ways.** There is another network, older than the causeways, which is not on any Board map: the wide green droving-roads by which the herds come across the plain to the Chute. They are unmarked and unmade, a hundred yards wide in places, cropped short by ten thousand hooves, and they run from nowhere in particular to Barrelside by routes known only to drovers. Sal Trotter walks them. At night, travellers camped by a Drovers' Way sometimes hear a herd going past in the dark, and the sound is wrong: not the thud and shuffle of cattle, but a soft, upright, two-footed tread, hundreds of feet together, and very low, a humming.
