# Oathen: The Country That Keeps the Change

> Fewer words, more fingers. Fewer fingers, fewer words.
>> — a Shadeward proverb, signed with the left hand, which is the hand you can spare

> Every sentence in Tacit is bought on credit. The canyon is the lender. It has never once forgotten a debt, and it charges by the echo.
>> — Envoy Dorian Sayce, in a letter home that he did not send, because a letter may now count

[fiction]
Wenn Corley wakes before the Plate is down, because the water does not wait. She does not say good morning. Nobody in the Shadeward Galleries says good morning; you do not promise a morning until you have had it. She touches her husband's shoulder twice, which means *I am going*, and he touches her wrist once, which means *come back*, and that is the closest anyone in the house comes to a vow before noon. The boy sleeps with his bit on. It is red leather with blue beads along the strap, and his grandmother's teeth have worn two shallow grooves in it, and his own teeth are wearing them deeper.
The yoke is cedar. The two buckets are felted on the outside so they will not knock. Wenn goes down the Cistern Stair in the dark with four hundred other carriers, and you can hear all of them: the soft fall of felt soles, the creak of yokes, breathing. That is all. Four hundred people on a stair cut into a cliff, and the loudest thing on it is a woman's cough, and the woman who coughed lays her hand flat over her own mouth afterward, as if she had said something.
At the cistern mouth the Share-Keeper sits at his slate with his tally-sticks. He does not ask her name. He looks at her thumb-ring, the brass one with the notch, which is her household's cistern-oath, sworn on the first day of Grace, *eleven buckets a day and not one more, barring fire*. He cuts a nick in the stick. She fills. There is a man two places ahead of her whose hands are bound in rags to the second knuckle, and she knows without asking what he did last year and what the oath was, and she does not look at him, which is a kindness. The water is green and cold and tastes of stone. It is the Kept Rain, five weeks old. Last year at this time the cistern was a hand's breadth higher. Everyone on the stair is thinking it. Nobody signs it.
By full light she has carried nine loads up the Galleries to the houses that pay her: a crumb a bucket, two crumbs to the top tier. She does not talk to the customers. She signs the price with her left hand, the hand she could spare, and they sign the coin back, and the whole of the morning's commerce is conducted in a silence so total that she can hear, from three tiers up, the scratch of a Clausewright's quill drafting somebody's marriage. At the Felt Market she buys flatbread and a wedge of green cheese with her last two crumbs. The bread-woman's hands are fast and beautiful. Then, in the middle of a sign for *fresh this morning*, the bread-woman's smallest finger goes back on itself with a sound like a pea-pod opened, and hangs there, pointing at her elbow.
The whole market stops. Nobody makes a sound. The bread-woman looks at her hand. She had been baking since before dawn; the bread *was* fresh this morning; but she had baked the cheese-loaves yesterday, and her hand had been talking about all of it. Wenn takes her bread. She leaves an extra crumb on the board, which in Tacit means *I saw, and I am sorry, and I will not tell*. The bread-woman's mouth is trembling under her veil. She does not cry out. People who cry out say things.
In the afternoon the sun reaches the canyon floor for its two hours, and from the bridges you can hear the Breakdowns singing. They sing filth. They sing the oaths they broke, in rounds, and the canyon throws it all back up at the Sunward Wall nine times over, and none of it can hurt them. Wenn stands on the Tenth Bridge with her empty buckets and listens, the way everyone on the bridges listens, with a face that says nothing, and a feeling under her breastbone that she does not have a word for and would not use one if she had.
At dusk she goes home. Her husband has made lizard stew. They eat in silence, because meals are eaten in silence, and the boy watches her across the table with his grandmother's bit in his mouth and makes, with his small hands, the nursery-sign for *the lady's finger*, which he has heard about already, because children hear everything first. Wenn takes his hands in hers and folds them closed. It is not a sign. It is a mother holding her son's hands still. He understands it anyway.
Before sleep she goes to the Ear Shrine at the end of the gallery, where a stone ear the size of a cart-wheel is carved into the rock and worn smooth at the lobe by a thousand foreheads. She leans her mouth close to the hole in its centre. She says, very quietly, a thing that is true and costs her nothing: "I am afraid of the water." The stone ear takes it in. Somewhere inside the cliff, faint and flat and half a second late, a voice that is nearly hers says it back.
[/fiction]

## The Country at Ground Level

The core book tells you that Oathen is a land where words break bones. That is true, and it is the least of it. What a traveller notices first, coming down off the Rim Road at the Treaty Stair, is not the Forsworn or the Ledger or the gold of the Sayers. It is the noise that is not there. Oathen is a nation of half a million in its capital alone, a country with markets and smithies and drunks and lovers and children, and it has built every one of those things to be quiet. Hinges are greased with goat-fat twice a day. Pots are lined with felt. Market scales are weighted with lead shot sewn into leather so the pans do not clink. Dogs are debarked as pups, by a cut under the jaw that Oathen vets learned from surgeons who learned it from the Bench. A traveller from the Fatlands, used to a world of shouting, sizzling, belching and bargaining, can spend his first night in a Tacit inn lying awake in a sweat, convinced that everyone in the building has died.

Everything below follows from that silence: how people make a living without haggling aloud, how they court without promising, how they punish without prisons, how they pray to a god who is listening, and how they get from one canyon to the next without hearing their own voices come back. This chapter goes down into it, gallery by gallery.

[box: The Three Hands of Oathen]
Oatheners divide every act of communication into three hands. **The Right Hand** is speech: binding, expensive, used for contracts, sentences, vows and the few truths worth their cost. **The Left Hand** is handtalk: cheap, quick, used for prices, gossip, jokes and affection, and believed, until last year, to cost nothing. **The Closed Hand** is silence, which in Oathen is not an absence but a statement: a closed hand laid on the table means *I will not answer*, and it is a legal plea in every court in the land. The great fear of 641 A.G. is that the Left Hand has begun to bind, and that one day soon the Closed Hand will too.
[/box]

## Districts and Settlements

### Tacit, the Held Tongue

Tacit is cut into both walls of the deepest canyon in Oathen, a gash a thousand feet deep and in places no more than four hundred feet across, with the green thread of the Hushwater running at its floor. The **Sunward Wall** faces north and catches the sun; the **Shadeward Wall** faces south and does not. The two are tied together by two hundred rope bridges at nine levels, and the whole city reads, from the canyon floor, like two enormous ledgers held open facing each other, every line of them a gallery of doors.

The city has no streets. It has galleries, which run sideways along the cliff; stairs, which run up and down it; bridges, which cross it; and lifts, which are wicker baskets hauled on felted ropes by teams of haulers who chant their counts in handtalk. Addresses are given by wall, tier and door: *Shadeward, the sixth, the forty-first door from the Fig Stair.* Tacit is divided by its people into nine districts, though the Bench recognises only four, and the Breakdowns, officially, are not part of the city at all.

#### The Heights (Sunward, tiers one to three)

The highest galleries of the Sunward Wall belong to the Tongue-lines: the Hushwells, the Daths, the Esks, the Sarrows, the Vanthes, and two dozen lesser houses whose ancestors ate some portion of the god's tongue in the third night of the Silent Supper. Their houses are cut deep into the rock, fronted with screens of pierced red stone, and furnished entirely in felt and cedar. The Heights smell of cedar oil, which the rich rub into their bits to keep the leather soft and the iron from rusting; of rosewater; and, underneath, of the slate-dust that drifts down from the Ledger scaffolds a mile east. You can hear almost nothing here. The Heights employ servants whose entire work is silence: door-holders who open doors so slowly the hinges do not sigh; floor-walkers who go ahead of a lady with a soft broom to brush away the grit that might crunch.

*Trade:* the Heights trade in marriages, which is to say in bloodline, which is to say in the god. A Tongue-line girl's marriage contract is the largest document most families will ever own, and the Cutwrights' office on the third tier does more business here than anywhere outside Lastgate.

*Custom:* **the Veiled Hour.** Between noon and the first bell after, no one of the Tongue-lines speaks at all, even in private, even in emergencies. It began as a courtesy to the Mute Kings and is now the hour in which the Heights do their real business, by touch, by glance, and by small objects left on trays.

*Danger:* the Heights are the most godsign-ridden place on the Table outside a Seating. Any household may contain someone at the Course, bitted in silver and kept to the inner rooms, and a careless guest who asks the wrong question of the wrong cousin may find the answer comes true. Servants of the Heights learn early never to say *you look tired*, *I could die*, or *that will be the end of me* within earshot of the family.

*Notable:* **Old Pell Sarrow**, the floor-walker of the Dath house, who has swept ahead of three generations of Dath women and is the only person living who heard Lady Imre's last sentence at dinner before the gold went in. He swept the blood off the floor afterward. He has not been asked about it, and so he has not said.

#### The Bench Terrace (Sunward, tier four)

At the heart of the Sunward Wall, cut back into the rock behind a façade of nine pillars carved as closed hands, is the **Hall of Conditions**, where the Bench of Clauses sits on a floor of fine red sand so that nothing said there echoes. Around it, along the whole of the fourth tier, cluster the institutions of law: the drafting-rooms, the Clause Library, the lodging-halls of the Slate-Clerks, the Archive of Readings (where every ruling the Bench has ever made about what Tolm counts is copied and cross-referenced), and the small, felt-hung chapel where Clausewrights go before a hard day to say nothing to the god in private.

The terrace smells of ink, sealing wax, and nervous sweat. The sound is the sound of quills: hundreds of them, scratching in long rooms, and the tiny dry tap of slate-pencils as clerks pass drafts back and forth for checking. There are always petitioners on the terrace steps, waiting to have something drafted: a marriage, a sale, an apology, a will. They sit with their hands folded in their laps and their mouths closed, sometimes for days.

*Trade:* drafting, at five lacks the page, and the Clausewright's daily review, at a lack a day, in which a junior lawyer goes over everything a client intends to say in the next twenty-four hours and marks it for danger.

*Custom:* **the Sand-Kiss.** A Clausewright entering the Hall of Conditions for the first time kneels and puts their lips to the sand of the floor, to taste what a word tastes like when it has nowhere to go. It tastes of dust and old blood. The sand is changed every Tablenight; the old sand is sold, quietly, to the rich, who keep a pinch of it in their bit-boxes against bad luck.

*Danger:* **the Readings.** The Bench has, over six hundred years, ruled on what the god counts. Some of those rulings were wrong, and the people who relied on them broke. A petitioner who acts on a ruling the god has since stopped honouring has no defence; the Bench does not refund.

*Notable:* **Under-Drafter Lise Amberd**, twenty-four, who has noticed that the drafts of the Sayer's sentence on Sorrin Vael's desk have begun to correct themselves overnight. A word moved here. A comma added there. She checks each morning. She has told no one, because she does not know how to say it without asserting that a document is alive.

#### The Galleries (Shadeward, tiers two to seven)

Most of Tacit lives here, on the wall that never sees the sun: carriers, weavers, felt-makers, lift-haulers, carvers, potters, goatherds who drive their flocks up the plateau stairs at dawn, and the families of the Canyon Watch. The galleries are four feet wide, with a waist-high parapet and a drop of several hundred feet beyond it, and they are crowded. People pass each other sideways, eyes down. Children run them in packs, bitted, signing to each other so fast their hands blur.

The Galleries smell of goat, lamp-oil, wet wool and the sour green cheese the whole of Tacit eats, which ripens in caves cut into the back of the gallery-houses and gives the Shadeward Wall a breath like an old boot. The sound, if you stand still, is a vast soft rustle: felt on stone, cloth on cloth, breath, and the occasional thin, cut-off cry of a baby nursed quiet before it can find its voice.

*Trade:* labour of every kind, and the goods that keep a silent city running: felt in bolts, leather bits, wax tablets, tally-sticks, hand-chimes muffled with wool for the deaf.

*Custom:* **the Extra Crumb.** Leaving one crumb more than the price on a counter means *I saw what happened to you, and I will not tell*. It is the commonest kindness in Tacit, and the commonest bribe.

*Danger:* **gallery falls**, which are frequent, and not always accidents. A Shadeward man who knows his neighbour has seen him break can solve the problem with a shoulder at the parapet, and nobody screams on the way down, because nobody screams.

*Notable:* **Fennick Darrow**, the Share-Keeper of the Cistern Stair, who has tallied the Shadeward water for twenty-two years and knows, to the bucket, that the cisterns are lower this year than in any year on record, and that someone on the Sunward Wall has been drawing double. He has the tally-sticks to prove it. He is very afraid of them.

#### The Felt Market (Shadeward, tier five, the Great Gallery)

Where the Shadeward Wall bows outward in a great natural ledge, the city has cut a market three hundred yards long and fifty deep, roofed with stretched canvas and floored with felt that is beaten clean every night. Here is where Tacit buys and sells. There is no calling of wares. Every stall has a slate on which prices are chalked, and every customer haggles by hand, and every bargain is struck by the buyer laying coin on the counter and the seller drawing it in, without a word. When foreigners come, they bring price-witnesses: licensed Oathen go-betweens with brass collar-plates, who charge a crumb a word and can conduct a whole sale in conditionals so tangled that nobody in it has promised anything.

The market smells of flatbread on hot stone, dried figs, lizard roasting on spits, dyed wool, and rendered goat-fat. The sound is a hiss of handtalk, which is not silent at all once you learn to hear it: the slap of a palm, the click of a fingernail on a thumbnail, the soft snap of fingers that is the Left Hand's question mark.

*Trade:* everything. Food, cloth, tools, bits, felt, tally-sticks, sworn notes (at the paper-brokers' stalls at the eastern end), and, under the counters, relics: echo-stones, Sayer's sand, and lately, unsworn speech-scrolls, which are contraband since 640 and sell for their weight in lacks.

*Custom:* **the Shut Stall.** A merchant who covers her slate with a cloth is not closed; she is in mourning for a word, and will not sell until sunset. Markets have shut for whole days when a beloved bread-woman broke.

*Danger:* since 640, **handtalk breaks.** The Felt Market has seen nineteen fingers go this year. Merchants have begun haggling in tally-sticks again, the old way, laying out notched sticks and pushing them back and forth, and the price of sticks has tripled.

*Notable:* **Silas Amend**, the paper-broker (see the Rim Market chapter), keeps his stall at the eastern end of the Felt Market, under a striped awning, with a spyglass on a brass stand trained on the Sunward Wall.

#### The Bridges (all tiers, the Span)

The two hundred rope bridges of Tacit are a district of their own, with its own people: the **Rope-Folk**, riggers and bridge-wardens who live in little houses cut into the cliff at each bridge's anchor and who swear, on taking a bridge, to keep it. That oath is Weight 4. A Rope-Folk family whose bridge fails breaks with it, all of them, at whatever part they swore by, and the old bridge-families have the stooped, careful bodies of people who once swore by their spines. The bridges are felted along the footboards and wrapped at the handropes, and they move constantly in the canyon wind with a slow sigh that is the only sound in Tacit people are not afraid of.

The bridges smell of tar, hemp and the river four hundred feet below. They sound of wind, and of the Tattler when it comes, which sets them all humming like the strings of an instrument.

*Trade:* bridge-tolls (a crumb a crossing on the high bridges, nothing on the low), rigging, and the hauling of goods across in wicker cradles.

*Custom:* **the Held Breath.** Crossing a bridge, Oatheners hold their breath at the middle, where the sag is deepest and the drop is longest, and let it out only on the far side. Children are taught that the middle of a bridge is where words fall out of you.

*Danger:* the lower bridges were cut in the Forsworn Riots of 506, and the stumps still hang from the walls like the ends of severed tendons. Brakk shouts across the canyon, through his trumpet, that they could be cut again by noon. The Rope-Folk do not know whether to fear him or join him.

*Notable:* **Marrit Holloway**, Bridge-Warden of the Tenth Bridge, whose family has kept it for nine generations and who has noticed that the old felting on the footboards has begun to wear through in the shape of feet. Not shoes. Bare feet, very large, all walking east, toward the Ledger, at night.

#### The Ledger Foot (east end of the canyon, floor and lower tiers)

Where the canyon bends east and the Sunward Wall becomes the four-mile cliff of the Ledger, the carvers live: in shanties and caves at its foot, under the scaffolds, among heaps of chippings that the wind lifts into a fine pink haze. The **Carvers' Rows** are a working district of stoneworkers, scaffold-riggers, lamp-boys, ink-grinders and copyists, and they are the only people in Oathen whose entire trade is writing words down.

Since 640 they are terrified. Ottoline Vant's miscut, and the eleven others she found, have made every chisel in the Rows a potential knife. The Bench insists that carving does not count, and has posted a notice to that effect at the foot of the scaffolds, carved, and the carvers look at it every morning and wonder whether it is binding.

The Ledger Foot smells of stone-dust, lamp-smoke and the vinegar the carvers use to rinse their eyes. It sounds of chisels: the light, patient tick of a thousand of them, all day and, in the Carving season, all night.

*Trade:* carving, at the Bench's rate (a lack a letter for public record); copying; and the sale of chippings, which are believed to hold a little of the oaths they were cut from and are ground into charms.

*Custom:* **the Blind Hour.** Before a carver cuts a name, he closes his eyes and touches the letters of the draft with his fingertips, to be sure that what his hand knows is what the Bench wrote. Since last year, many carve the whole day with their eyes closed.

*Danger:* the scaffolds, which are rope and plank and hang from the clifftop eight hundred feet up, and the fact that in a dry wind the dust gets into the lungs and stays. Old carvers cough grit. Some of them cough it up in shapes.

*Notable:* **Master Carver Ebbe Lantry**, who taught Ottoline Vant and who has quietly taken her list of the eleven miscuts and gone looking for a twelfth. He has found it. It is one of his own, from 609, and the man it names is a Bencher who has never broken. Ebbe Lantry does not understand why, and is beginning to suspect that the Bencher had the stone corrected, at night, by someone else.

#### The Murmuring Tier (Shadeward, tier eight)

Below the Galleries, where the Shadeward Wall is dampest, the city keeps those it cannot afford to hear. The **Murmuring House** occupies most of the tier (see the core book), but around it has grown a district of the half-kept: families of the Appetite-struck who cannot afford the House and nurse their swearers at home; bitmakers' shops; gag-nurses who hire out by the night; and the hospices of the Quiet, which take the dying and promise them nothing. Garl Tome's forge is on the tier's western end, and its glow is the only fire visible from the canyon floor at night.

The Murmuring Tier smells of broth, iron, old blood and wet felt. The sound gives the place its name: from behind every third door, a low, wet hum, people trying to swear through iron, and the hum is so constant that the people who live here no longer hear it, and are frightened, on Tablenight, when it stops.

*Trade:* bits, gags, splints, broth, night-nursing, and the hire of **sitters**, who sit up with a swearer in the night with a hand ready to clamp over the grille.

*Custom:* **the Listening Supper.** Once a month, families of the tier gather and eat with their bitted kin at a long table in a cleared gallery, and nobody speaks, and nobody tries to, and the bitted are, for one meal, simply people at a table. It is the gentlest thing in Tacit, and the Bench has twice tried to ban it.

*Danger:* a determined swearer. Bits slip. Grilles rust. A swearer who gets a sentence out on the Murmuring Tier may bind a whole gallery, and the tier has a word, *loose*, that means only one thing.

*Notable:* **Dacey Furl**, the Gag-Nurse (see the core book), is the most trusted person on the tier.

#### The Breakdowns (the canyon floor)

The slum of the Forsworn has been described in the core book; at ground level it is a city of its own, perhaps a hundred thousand people in a mile and a half of canyon floor, living in lean-tos against the cliff foot, in houses built from the wreckage of the cut bridges, in caves the river has scoured, and in the hulls of flat-boats hauled out of the Hushwater and turned keel-up. The sun reaches here for two hours a day. In those two hours the Breakdowns come out, and sit in it, and sing.

The Breakdowns smell of river mud, goat-dung fires, cheap fig-spirit, wound-dressing and the oiled cloth the Forsworn wear over what broke. They are loud. The loudness is shocking after the city above: people shout, laugh, swear, curse Tolm (carefully, the ones who still have tongues), lie for sport, and haggle aloud in plain sentences, because what they could break is already broken. The lanes are named for the oaths their founders broke: Fair Weight Row, Back-Before-Dark, the Never Alley, I'll-Stand-By-You, Honest Measure, Till-Death Court.

*Trade:* lying, for hire; loud-crying (shouting news, insults or warnings in places a whole Oathener cannot); smuggling; the selling of fig-spirit, which is illegal in Tacit above; and the oldest trade of all, which in the Breakdowns is conducted openly, because the women and men who practise it broke at the heart long ago and promise nothing they cannot give. What happens in the **Lantern Lanes** is between adults, and the Breakdowns, which have few laws, have one about that, enforced with a cleaver.

*Custom:* **the Showing.** A newcomer to the Breakdowns, freshly broken, is brought to the river at sunrise and their cloth is taken off, and the whole lane looks at what broke, and then the lane shows them theirs, one by one. Then someone feeds them. It is the closest thing the Forsworn have to a christening.

*Danger:* the Canyon Watch, which comes down on the lifts in force when the Bench is nervous; the Echo-Wardens, who hate how the Breakdowns' noise goes up the walls; and the Breakdowns' own politics, which are getting violent as the population doubles with handtalkers and scribes who broke at the fingers and the palms and are not yet broken enough to be safe.

*Notable:* **Mags Carrow** and the eleven strapped survivors of Wending (see the core book) live at the eastern end, in Till-Death Court.

#### The Witnessing Vaults (beneath both walls)

Beneath Tacit, reached by a dozen stairs from both walls and one from the Breakdowns, lie the catacombs where the dead stand upright in their niches, faces outward. The Vaults are older than the city above: the first niches were cut in the generation after the Supper, and the deepest galleries hold the Scraplings who licked the god's fluid off the Gullet stones. There are believed to be four million dead in the Vaults. The Keeners, who tend them, have mapped about a third.

The Vaults smell of dry stone, myrrh, lamp-oil, and the faint sweetness of the canyon dead, who in that dry air do not so much rot as cure, the skin drawing back from the teeth to give every face the same look of having just heard something. The sound is silence, absolute, the deepest in Tacit, broken only by the slap of the Keeners' sandals and, nine times this year, by the voice of a corpse giving testimony.

*Trade:* niche-rent (paid yearly by the family; unpaid niches are emptied into the Ossuary Wells), and the swearing of oaths on the dead, for which the Keeners charge a witnessing-fee of two lacks and a candle.

*Custom:* **the Reckoning Visit.** On the anniversary of a death, the family goes down into the Vaults and stands before the niche and says nothing for an hour, and then each of them lays a hand on the bones and says, *I have kept what I could*. It is a true statement, which is why it was chosen.

*Danger:* the dead who Testify. And the **Drop**, a shaft in the oldest gallery that is said to go down to the god's bones in the Gullet, three miles west, through solid rock. Keeners have lowered lamps into it on chains a thousand feet long. The lamps came up warm.

*Notable:* **Old Sabra Wends**, Keener of Oaths (see the core book).

### Beyond Tacit

#### Low Dray

A market town of eleven thousand in a broad, shallow canyon two days down the Hushwater from Tacit, where the walls are low enough that the sun reaches the floor all day and orchards of figs and pomegranates can grow on terraces watered by the cisterns. Low Dray is where Tacit sends its orphans to be fostered, its widows to remarry, and its children to grow up somewhere with light. It is the most cheerful town in Oathen, by which Oatheners mean that people there have been known to smile in the street.

It smells of figs drying on rooftops and goats in the lanes and the bitter smoke of pomegranate-wood ovens. It sounds of bees, which Oatheners love because bees cannot promise, and of children's bits clicking as they run.

*Trade:* figs, pomegranates, honey, goat-cheese, fostering (a Low Dray foster-house takes a child for two lacks a week), and **the Dray Bit**, a child's muzzle of soft white kid, beaded in the town's own red-and-white patterns, considered the finest in the land.

*Custom:* **the Fig-Promise.** Lovers in Low Dray do not swear. They exchange a fig, which is understood to mean everything a vow would mean, and which binds nothing, and which the Bench has twice investigated and twice declared harmless. This year a girl in Low Dray who gave a fig to one young man and married another broke at the breastbone. The orchards have gone quiet.

*Danger:* the fostered children. A child fostered in Low Dray has a bit, a foster-mother, and a family in Tacit who will one day want them back, and some of those children have made promises to their foster-mothers in nursery-sign that their real mothers do not know about.

*Notable:* **Hedda Sele**, aunt and foster-mother to Wenna Sele, whose father was broken by Jude Crake last Carving. Hedda has heard the story of the man in Low Dray who was not Wenna's uncle. She has started watching every man who looks at the girl. She has a cleaver, and she is not Forsworn, and she has made no promises about what she will do with it.

#### Treaty Stair

The town where the Rim Road comes down off the plateau into the canyons: a long switchback stair of eight hundred steps, cut into a cliff face, with a town built along it on terraces. Treaty Stair is the door of Oathen. Every foreign envoy, every Company caravan bound for Tacit, every pilgrim to the Ledger and every buyer of sworn treaties comes through it, and the town exists to receive them, house them, and teach them to shut up. The **Hall of First Silence** at the top of the stair is where foreigners are made to sit for an hour while an Oathen clerk explains, on slate, that everything they say from the next step down will count.

It smells of mules, foreign perfume, spiced Fatlands sausage sold at the top step to travellers who have not yet learned better, and the vinegar-soaked kerchiefs that Oathen officials hold to their noses when foreigners boast. It sounds, at the top, like any Rim town; and then, step by step down the stair, it goes quiet, until at the foot you can hear the river.

*Trade:* inns, interpreters, price-witnesses, Clausewrights for hire, and the private surgeries of the **Lower Terraces**, where foreign physicians take rooms because Oathen law has nothing to say about medicine, and where, according to the Blank Trade, a surgery has been scheduled to take a kidney out of Cassian Wry.

*Custom:* **the Last Word.** At the top of the stair is a stone basin. Foreigners are invited, before they descend, to say into it everything they want to say while it is still free, and many do: the boasts, the curses, the lies, the love-talk. The basin is emptied at dusk by a Quiet sister who pours sand into it and carries the sand away.

*Danger:* foreigners who do not believe the clerk. Every season someone comes down Treaty Stair laughing, says something big in the inn, and is carried back up on a litter.

*Notable:* **Clerk of First Silence Tamsk Orrey**, who has explained the Rule to forty thousand foreigners and has a private bet with himself about which of them will break first. He is right four times in five. He has never told anyone, because a bet is a kind of oath.

#### Highwell, the Rain Basin

High in the western canyons, above the Gullet, the land opens into a bowl of red rock a mile across, ringed with cisterns cut into the basin walls like the cells of a honeycomb. This is where Tolm's great slow heart was eaten, by the families who became the Rain-keepers, and it is where the Kept Rain falls first and hardest. The **Rain Shrine** stands at the centre of the basin: a tall, roofless ring of standing stones, each carved with a year and a tally of rain, six hundred and forty of them, the newest still raw.

Highwell smells of wet stone even in the dry months, because the cisterns breathe. It sounds of water: the drip in the cisterns, the trickle of the conduits, the slow pulse of the heart-line families, whose blood beats so heavily that you can hear it in a quiet room, like a drum under a blanket.

*Trade:* water. Highwell sells Kept Rain by the flask to the whole of Oathen and the Rim (5 L a flask, blessed), and sells cistern-shares to the towns downstream, and every share is sworn at the Shrine on the first day of Grace.

*Custom:* **the Standing.** On the morning of the Kept Rain, the whole of Highwell stands in the basin with faces turned up, mouths bitted, and waits. This year they stood for three days. Children fainted. An old man died on his feet and was held upright by his sons until the rain came, because to fall would have been to stop waiting.

*Danger:* the flood. On Rain day the basin fills in an hour and the overflow goes down the Gullet in a wall of red water, and every year someone is in the wrong place.

*Notable:* **Yusra Thole**, the Rainwarden (see the core book), who is silent. Her deputy, **Ash Merrow**, is not, and is frightened enough of the silence to talk to strangers.

#### Knot Canyon

A labyrinth of slot-canyons and dry cisterns half a day south of Tacit, where the rock has been worn by water into passages a shoulder wide and hundreds of feet deep, so twisted that sound gets lost in them. It is the one place near the capital where you can shout without being heard twice. Smugglers, Bait-doctrine preachers, unlicensed Sureties and people with things to say come here, and Grandam Cess lives at its heart in a warren of cisterns.

Knot Canyon smells of bat-dung and still water and the incense Cess burns. It sounds of nothing; your own voice comes back to you not at all, which is so strange to Oatheners that first-time visitors weep.

*Trade:* oath-release (Cess's); unlicensed Surety work; contraband; the swearing of oaths no one wants recorded.

*Custom:* **the Knot.** Visitors tie a knot in a cord strung across the canyon entrance, one for each oath they have come to be rid of. The cord is thick as a man's arm with knots, and some of them have begun to move.

*Danger:* getting lost, which is easy; and Cess's nine hundred, who owe her *to come when called*, and who have begun, without being called, to come and sit at the canyon entrance in the evenings, waiting.

*Notable:* **Grandam Cess**, the Vow-Eater (see the core book).

#### Sheathmouth

A village at the downstream end of the **Sheath**, a narrow canyon fourteen miles long where the walls are so close and so smooth that an echo can run nine times before it dies. Sheathmouth lives on guiding travellers through the Sheath in perfect silence, roped together, with felt over their mouths and wax in their ears, communicating by tugs on the rope. The village is a single gallery of houses at the canyon mouth, and its people are the most silent in Oathen; it is said a Sheathmouth wife can go a year without speaking and that her husband would not notice, because he would be doing the same.

Sheathmouth smells of goat and lamp-black (the guides blacken their faces against the glare). It sounds of the river and the Sheath, which, at the end of a still day, gives up very faint scraps of things said in it years ago.

*Trade:* guiding, at 5 L a traveller; felt mouth-pads and ear-wax; and the recovery of the broken, who are carried out of the Sheath on litters by the village's guides for a fee paid by the family.

*Custom:* **the Rope Oath.** A Sheathmouth guide swears nothing to a client. Instead, guide and client each tie the other's rope, and that is understood. A guide who cuts a client's rope in the Sheath does not break, but no guide in Sheathmouth will ever tie a rope for him again, which in Sheathmouth is a death sentence of the slow kind.

*Danger:* **the nine-times echo.** The debtor who swore in the Sheath to pay by the new Plate (see the core book) is buried at Sheathmouth, in nine pieces, one for each Breaking.

*Notable:* **Rope-Mother Huld**, oldest of the guides, who has walked the Sheath eleven hundred times and has heard, on the last three walks, a voice in the walls that is hers, saying something she never said.

#### Drover's Rest, below Wending

A huddle of stone corrals and goat-hair tents at the foot of the Unmade Road, where drovers rest their herds before the climb to Wending Pass. Drover's Rest exists because the pass is the only road east out of Oathen into the high plateau and the Fast's borderlands, and because every drover who climbs it must first decide what he will swear to Captain Renn Hold at the top. The town's one inn, the **Halfway**, keeps a board on which drovers chalk the forms of words that have got people past the captain and back down whole.

Drover's Rest smells of goat, dung-smoke and the rendered fat drovers carry up as offerings. It sounds of bells; the drovers' goats are belled, the only belled animals in Oathen, because the drovers say the Captain likes to hear them coming.

*Trade:* goats, wool, hides, and the hire of **road-walkers**, children of the town who lead herds up the exact centre of the Unmade Road where the bones are thinnest.

*Custom:* **the Leaving.** Every drover leaves something at the foot of the road: a cake of fat, a flask of water, a twist of salt. They say it is for the Captain. It is collected at night, and the drovers have stopped asking by whom.

*Danger:* the road, at night. The strapped men are not all in Till-Death Court.

*Notable:* **Old Corran Hask**, who keeps the Halfway and was a drover himself for forty years, and who has sworn to the Captain twelve times and been sent down twelve times, and who is the only person in Drover's Rest who knows that the Captain weeps when he sends them away.

#### Hollin's Stair

The cliff-cells of the Quiet, along the upper Gullet: a vertical monastery of three hundred cells cut into a sheer cliff, connected by ladders and rope, with no stair at all despite the name. (The name is a joke of the founders, who held that a stair was a kind of promise to go up.) The Quiet live here in pairs, one cell above another, communicating by slate lowered on string. The cells look down on the red sand of the Gullet, over Tolm's bones, and the Quiet say that from their windows, on still nights, they can see the sand rise and fall.

Hollin's Stair smells of chalk-dust, lamp-oil, and the brine of Brother Ansgar Mote's vault, where four hundred tongues are kept. It sounds of chalk on slate, and of wind, and lately, of something under the sand.

*Trade:* none. The Quiet are fed by alms and by the families of those who come to them. They sell slates and chalk, and they take the knife for those who ask, free.

*Custom:* **the Lowered Slate.** Any visitor may tie a written question to the end of the rope at the cliff's foot. In the morning the rope is drawn up. Sometimes there is an answer, by evening. Sometimes the slate comes down wiped clean, which is also an answer.

*Danger:* the Quiet's teachings, which spread; and the cliff itself, which visitors must climb by rope if they want a face-to-face, and which the Quiet do not help them up.

*Notable:* **Sister Verity Amn** and **Brother Ansgar Mote** (see the core book).

#### Sowe

The dead hamlet at the mouth of Sworn Gorge, abandoned since 420 A.G., when its people were found broken in the way the Echo-Wardens' charter forbids describing. Sowe is twenty stone houses and a well, and nobody has lived in it for two hundred years, and its doors are still shut from the inside. The Wardens keep a post here, against the forty-foot wall packed with wool.

Sowe smells of nothing; the wool behind the wall has absorbed every smell for a mile. It sounds, on still nights, of the murmur behind the wall, and this Carving, for the first time in two hundred years, of nothing at all.

*Notable:* the post's commander, **Gorge-Warden Tacet Morrow**, who replaced Halvar Stane as Warden of the Wall in 639 and who wants Halvar kept away from it, because the night before Halvar was relieved, Morrow found him at the wall with his good ear pressed to the stone and his lips moving.

## Roads and Ways

Nobody in Oathen travels far if they can help it. Every road is a canyon, and every canyon is a mouth. The traveller's first rule is the oldest proverb in the land, *listen twice*: once before you speak, to hear whether the walls will answer, and once after, to hear whether they did.

There are four ways to move. **The floor-paths** follow the rivers along the canyon bottoms; they are flat, shaded and well watered, and they flood on Rain day and wash out every year. **The rim-tracks** run across the plateau above, scrub and scoured stone under the white sky, where there is no echo at all but also no water and no shade, and where the Tattler wind can carry a word ten miles. **The galleries and lifts** connect the tiers of the cliff-towns and, in a few places, run for miles along a canyon wall, a road a yard wide with a drop on one side. And **the Rim Road** itself, the Company's highway, crosses the north of the country from Treaty Stair to Quillhouse on a great embanked causeway laid along the plateau, felted at its bridges, with Company waystations every fifteen miles where Oathen clerks sit at the doors and remind every traveller, by slate, what country they are in.

Travellers in posted gorges go roped and gagged, with Sheathmouth guides or Echo-Wardens. Merchants hire **mutes**, porters who have taken the knife and carry goods through echoing places for double wages, and who cannot be made to swear anything by an echo because they cannot hear it come back in their own voice. The rich travel in **felt litters**, enclosed boxes carried by eight bearers, lined so thickly that a lady inside can say what she likes and be heard by no one, including the god, or so the litter-makers claim. The Bench has never ruled on felt litters. It is rumoured that several Benchers own one.

## Bread and Coin

Oathen is a poor land that is very rich. The canyons grow little: figs and pomegranates in the low valleys, goats and lizards on the plateau, cheese, a sparse grain in the terraces of Highwell. Water is a yearly ration. Wood is so scarce that cedar is a luxury and most cooking is done on dung-fires or on stones heated in the sun. Yet Tacit is one of the great money-cities of the Table, because Oathen's real export is the only thing the rest of the world cannot make: a word that cannot be broken.

Every treaty between lands, every Rim Road Company contract, every bond of every large merchant house on the Table comes east to be sworn. The Bench charges the **Page-Tithe**: one lack per sworn page, paid to the crown, on every contract sworn in Oathen. A treaty between Brinehollow and the Vigil may run to six hundred pages. A Company charter renewal, sworn every seven years, runs to four thousand. The Page-Tithe pays for the Bench, the Canyon Watch, the Ledger and the king, and leaves a great deal over, which the Tongue-lines who sit on the Bench distribute among themselves by a formula drafted in 344 A.G. and never once amended.

Below the Tongue-lines the money moves through **drafting** (Clausewrights and their clerks, perhaps twelve thousand people in Tacit alone), **surety** (the Compact's four hundred licensed bondsmen and their unlicensed rivals), **paper** (the speculators who buy and sell sworn notes and watch issuers through spyglasses), and **silence** (the felt-makers, bitmakers, litter-bearers, mutes and guides whose trade is keeping people from being heard). The common people of the Galleries live as they live everywhere: by carrying, building, herding, weaving and selling food. They are paid in lacks and crumbs, and they rarely see paper.

## Law and the Knife

Oathen has no prisons, no gaols, no stocks and no gallows. It has a sentence, and the sentence is in you. The machinery of justice is therefore almost entirely made of words: the drafting of sentences, the swearing of them by the convicted, and the watching. Enforcement is done by the convict's own body, and only when a crime is not an oath, or a sentence is too dangerous to trust to the god, does anyone in Oathen need to lay a hand on anyone else.

### Who Enforces

**The Canyon Watch** keeps the peace on the galleries and bridges, carries out arrests, escorts the accused to court, and guards the Hall of Conditions, the Ledger and the Gilded Cells. It is small (about three thousand in Tacit), well paid, and absolutely reliable, because every soldier has sworn an eleven-page oath of obedience. **The Sentence-Readers**, Slate-Clerks attached to the courts, keep the record of every sworn sentence and periodically visit convicts to read them their terms aloud, which renews nothing but reminds a great deal. **The Echo-Wardens** police posted gorges and may fine anyone caught speaking in one. **The Breaker**, Jude Crake, carries out death sentences. And in the Breakdowns, **the Splints**, Brakk's council, keep an order of their own with cleavers and plain speech.

### How a Trial Works

Every trial in Oathen begins with the **Closed Hand**: the accused is brought before the court, bitted, and the bit is removed only when the judge signs that the court is ready to hear. The accused may lay their hand closed on the rail, which means *I will not answer*, and is a lawful plea; but a closed hand is taken as an admission in all but the gravest cases, and the court will proceed to sentence.

There are two kinds of trial. In a **Breaking Trial**, for oath-crimes (default, fraud, desertion, infidelity, perjury), the accused repeats aloud, in its original words, the promise they are said to have broken, and the court watches. The original words are read from the Ledger or from the parties' sworn copies. The accused may argue the reading first, through a Clausewright, but once they speak the oath, the god decides. If nothing happens, they are acquitted and the accuser must pay the court's fee. If the oath was broken, it breaks again, then and there, at the same part, and that is the verdict and most of the sentence.

In a **Question Trial**, for crimes that are not oaths (murder, theft, assault, arson, water-theft), the accused is asked one question, *Did you do it?*, and must answer aloud, yes or no. A closed hand is permitted, and leads to sentence. A *no* that is a lie breaks the mouth, at Weight 3, before the whole court, and is a verdict. A *no* that is true is acquittal, and Oathen has never in its history convicted an innocent person who was willing to speak. The Bench is very proud of this, and does not discuss the Forsworn whose mouths have already broken, who can answer *no* to anything.

Sentences are then drafted by a Clausewright of the court, read to the convict, and sworn by the convict aloud, usually with the Weight raised to 4 by the court's witness. A convict who refuses to swear their sentence is sent to the Breaker.

### The Gravest Sentences

For crimes the Bench considers too dangerous to leave to a convict's body (murder of a Tongue-line, treason, the unbitting of a Sayer), there are two sentences. The first is **Sworn Exile on the Dead**: the convict lays a hand on the victim's bones in the Witnessing Vaults and swears to leave Oathen and never return, at Weight 5, with the dead as second witness. If they return, the dead Testify. The second is **the Hour**: Jude Crake, a pair of chairs, figs, and an hour-glass.

## The Faithful

Every Oathener believes in Tolm, in the sense that every Oathener believes in the ground. The god is not a matter of faith; it is a matter of record. What Oatheners argue about is the god's *meaning*, and that argument runs along the lines of the Three Doctrines, which in Oathen have taken local names and local habits.

**The Record** is Oathen's Theft doctrine, held by most of the Galleries and by the Bench officially. *I am listening* is a sentence passed: we are watched until we pay, and every Breaking is a line in the god's account. Followers of the Record keep household tallies of their oaths, kept and broken, notched on sticks hung by the door, and believe that at the end of the Long Grace the account will be read aloud. They are careful, anxious, honest people, and they make up most of the country.

**Go On** is the Gift doctrine, strong in Low Dray, among the Sureties and among the old. *Go on* was permission and forgiveness: eat, live, keep your word as I kept mine. Go-On households hang a fig on the door at Tablenight and believe the Kept Rain is a gift renewed. They are gentler and, the Record says, lazier. Since the rain came late, a great many of them have gone quiet.

**The Plain Reading** is the Bait doctrine, illegal in Oathen since 290 A.G. The god said it was listening. It has not said it has stopped. The Plain Readers hold that Tolm was never eaten, only overheard: that it lay down in silence because a silent meal was the safest place to wait, and that every word spoken in Oathen since has been the god's own nourishment. They are found among the Forsworn, the Quiet, the scribes broken since 640, and in Knot Canyon. They leave written prayers at the feet of the frozen herdsman in Low Shale, and they are growing.

### Shrines

**The Ear Shrines** are carved stone ears, from a hand's breadth to a cart-wheel across, set into the cliff at the end of galleries throughout Tacit and the towns. They are for saying true things aloud to the god: griefs, fears, confessions of past fact. True statements cost nothing, and Oatheners find great relief in saying, into the stone, *I hated my father* or *I took the bread* or *I am afraid*. The ears are worn smooth at the lobes by foreheads. Since this Carving, several have begun to say things back, half a second late, in the speaker's voice.

**The Rain Shrine** at Highwell is the holiest place in Oathen after the Gullet, where cistern-shares are sworn and where the Rainwarden keeps the tally of every year's fall.

**The Gullet** itself is not a shrine but a grave, and is visited only on the eve of Tablenight, barefoot.

### Rites

- **The Hearing.** When a child is born, the midwife holds a felted hand over its mouth during the first cry, and a Keener, standing in the doorway, says *heard*. It is the child's first contact with the god, and is believed to teach the child, before it knows anything else, that its voice is a thing someone is listening to.
- **The Bitting.** At the first tooth, the child is fitted with its first bit, usually an heirloom, in a family ceremony conducted entirely in handtalk. The family gives the child a name-sign, which is the name it will be called by until it is twelve.
- **The Unbitting.** At twelve, in the Hall of Conditions, before a Clausewright. The first words are chosen in advance. *I am here* is the commonest. *I was heard* is the most beautiful, and is used in the old families.
- **The Swearing of Shares.** On the first day of Grace, every householder swears their cistern-share at the Rain Shrine or at a town's share-stone. It is the first oath of the year and the most important.
- **The Tongue Supper.** Among the old Tongue-line houses, when a member of the family dies at the Brink or after, the heir is served, at the funeral meal, a single thin slice of the dead one's tongue, raw, and eats it in silence, so that the god's portion stays in the house. The Bench has never ruled on it. The Cutwrights' College knows of it, and records which houses do it, and has noticed that those houses reach the Course faster. The houses know this too. They do it anyway, because the alternative is to let the god go to someone else.
- **The Placing.** The dead are set upright in their niche in the Witnessing Vaults; every promise made to them in life is spoken aloud; the Keener asks the corpse whether each was kept.

### Holy Days

To the four festivals given in the core book (the Kept Rain, the Unbitting, the Carving of the Year and Tablenight), the canyons add two lesser days. **The Night of Ears**, on the eve of Tablenight, is when pilgrims walk the Gullet barefoot and lay their ears to the warm sand; it is the only night of the year when the Quiet come down from Hollin's Stair. **Share-Eve**, the night before the Kept Rain, is spent in households making the year's vows to each other in whispers, so that the rain, falling the next day, can wash the sound out of the canyon. This year, Share-Eve lasted four nights, and the whispered vows were made, and the rain did not come, and some of the whispers, the old women say, are still in the walls.

### The Litany of Exits

The most common prayer in Oathen is spoken at dawn by the Record faithful, and is constructed so that it promises nothing. Children learn it by sign before they are unbitted, and say it aloud for the first time on the morning after the Unbitting.

[fiction]
Witness, if you are listening, and I believe you are:
I intend, as I now intend, to speak little today.
If I speak, I hope to speak true.

## How Power Moves

Power in Oathen flows through words, and therefore through the people who own the words: who drafts them, who witnesses them, who carries their risk, and who may speak them at all. The Mute King is the source of all legitimate authority because he has never said anything, and therefore owes no one. Everyone below him owes somebody.

The Bench of Clauses governs, in theory by interpreting the king, in practice by drafting everything. Its power is the drafting of sentences: the sworn sentences of the courts, the oaths of the Canyon Watch, the cistern-shares, the treaties, and, once a year, the sentence a Sayer speaks. The Bench depends on the Tongue-lines for its members, and the Tongue-lines depend on the Bench for the Page-Tithe that keeps them rich. Both depend on the Rim Road Company, which brings the contracts that pay the Tithe; and the Company depends on one old woman in Quillhouse, whose witness holds half the trade of the Table together. The Sureties stand underneath everyone's words, carrying the risk of the rich in their own bodies, and are paid well for it, and are dying of it. The Forsworn have nothing left to break, and they are the only power in Oathen that can lie, threaten and promise freely: which is why the Bench fears them more than any foreign army.

Underneath all of it are the Sayers, who are not a faction, have no votes, and could end any of the others with a sentence.

#### Who They Are

Ket the Ninth is slight and pale, with the grey Hushwell eyes, very large in a narrow face, and hair cut close to the skull so that the strap of the bit lies flat. The bit is silver, chased in gold with the closed-hand crest of his house, and it has been resized eleven times; he has worn some version of it since the eleventh day of his life. The corners of his mouth have the deep grooves every Oathener carries, but his are cut down almost to the jaw. He smells of cedar oil and of the faint metallic sourness of a man who breathes through a grille. He moves very slowly, because a king's every gesture is minuted, and he has learned to make his body as quiet as his mouth. When he is moved, the only sign is in his hands, which close, slowly, one finger at a time, as though counting something.

Those who meet him say the same thing: that he listens harder than anyone they have ever met. His eyes go to each speaker and stay there, and he seems to take every word in and weigh it, and the weight is visible. Courtiers find it unbearable. Commoners who have been brought before him for royal mercy say it was like being heard for the first time in their lives.

#### Their Story

Ket was born in the Hushwell house in 610 A.G., the third child of a minor branch, and was chosen at eleven days old by the Bench's examiners, who test every Tongue-line infant of the right generation by laying it, swaddled, at the foot of the Ledger at dawn and watching which of them does not cry. Ket did not cry. The silver bit went in that afternoon. His mother was given a sworn note for a very large sum and the right to see him once a year, by lamplight, through a screen; she used it for nine years and then stopped, and Ket has never been told why.

He was raised in the **Muted House**, the palace at the summit of the Sunward Wall, by a tongueless sister of the Quiet called Ansa, who taught him the royal handtalk and the three thousand gestures of the **Book of Nods**, and who held her hand over his mouth whenever he laughed, so gently that he did not know until he was nine that other children's laughter made a sound. King Orvel the Eighth died in 611, and Ket was crowned at a year old, bitted, in the Hall of Conditions, and the Bench ruled as regent until 629, when he was judged able to gesture for himself. He has ruled since by the nod, the open hand, the closed hand, the turned palm, and by a hundred small refinements of his own that the Bench has had to add to the Book.

He reads everything: the Bench's minutes, the treaties, the Ledger's copy-books, foreign histories, the Cutwrights' reports. He has written nothing, ever. In 637 the Cutwrights' College delivered him a sealed report, by his own gesture, and he learned from it that his Regrowth stood at the Course, without a single word having passed his lips, and that the pressure he had felt behind his teeth since adolescence was not the ordinary desire of a man to speak. The god in the Hushwell blood had nowhere to go. It had been gathering.

In Carving of 640, alone at night in the felt room behind his bedchamber, Ket took the bit out, which he is permitted to do for cleaning, and hummed. One note, low, for the length of a breath, to know what it was. Every candle in the room leaned toward him. A Bench clerk named Odo Fane, asleep on duty behind a screen, heard it. The Bench had Fane bitted for life before noon the next day, in iron, by his own sworn consent, and lodged him in a felt room on the Muted House's lower tier. The king visits him every seventh day and sits with him for an hour, in silence. Two floors beneath the Ledger, in the Gilded Cells, Lady Imre Dath began scratching her first sentence into the lead of her cell wall the same night.

#### Their Place in the Land

Ket is the source of all lawful authority in Oathen. Every sentence of the courts is passed in his name, every treaty is witnessed in his presence, and every soldier of the Canyon Watch swears to him. Because he has sworn nothing, he owes nothing, and an oath sworn to him is the heaviest a commoner can make. His daily routine is audiences in the morning (petitioners, foreign envoys, Benchers), reading in the afternoon, and the Veiled Hour, which he began. He gestures; the Bench interprets; the interpretation is drafted; he nods to the draft or does not. His power is real but narrow: he can refuse. A king's closed hand on a draft kills it, and the Bench has learned, in twelve years, that this king closes his hand more often than any of the eight before him.

#### What They Carry

- **The silver bit** — gold-chased, cast by Garl Tome, resized in 639 because the tongue behind it has begun to thicken. The plate presses down a tongue that is now mottled the colour of raw liver.
- **A tuning fork** — steel, from a foreign musician's case, given to him by Envoy Dorian Sayce. He strikes it on the table and holds the stem against his teeth, so that the note goes into the bone of his skull. It is the nearest he comes to sound.
- **The Book of Nods** — his own copy, three thousand gestures, annotated in the hands of four Chief Clausewrights. He has never written in it.
- **The sealed report** — the Cutwrights' assessment of 637. He keeps it under the felt of his bed. It ends with the sentence: *His Majesty is to be considered, for the purposes of the Bench's planning, a Sayer who has not yet spoken.*
- **Odo Fane's leather bit** — the soft one the clerk wore as a boy, which Fane's mother gave the king when her son was bitted in iron. Ket keeps it in his sleeve.
- **No thumb-rings.** His hands are the only bare adult hands in the Heights. Oatheners find the sight of them unsettling, like a man without a shadow.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

Ket wants to say something. He does not know what. He fears that the sentence pressing behind his teeth is not his. He has begun to dream in words, which he had never done, and in the dreams he is standing on the Ledger scaffold and reading the cliff aloud from west to east, and every vow he reads comes due. **Secret (GM may reveal):** the hum. Every candle leaned, the clerk was bitted, and the Sayer began to write. **Secret he does not know:** sealed in the Archive of Readings is a drafted sentence, the **Silence Clause**, prepared by the Bench in 637 for a Sayer to speak if the king ever does: *The king has said nothing.* No one knows whether it would unmake his words, his mouth, or his reign. Sorrin Vael drafted it, and has kept it updated.

#### Ties

- **Sorrin Vael** — his interpreter, his regent in all but name. Ket respects him and does not trust him, and has gestured so, in a sign the Book does not contain.
- **Lady Imre Dath** — he has visited her once. All her ears turned to him. He believes she heard his hum. He is right.
- **Cassian Wry** — the only person in Oathen who can tell the king a comforting lie. Ket has him brought in some evenings and gestures for him to say pleasant false things. *You will be free.* *Your mother asked after you.*
- **Odo Fane** — the clerk he silenced, by no order of his own. The king's seventh-day visits are the closest thing he has to a friendship.
- **Judge Thane Urrow** — sentences in the king's name. Ket has watched three hundred of his trials from behind a screen and has begun, privately, to close his hand at the end of each one.
- **Garl Tome** — the Bitmaker, who fitted his bit, and whose daughter is twelve this year.
- **Halvar Stane** — Ket has read every report Halvar ever filed about Sworn Gorge, twice.

#### In Play

PCs meet Ket at audience, as envoys, petitioners or the Bench's agents; or at night, brought secretly by Cassian Wry. He will hold their gaze a long time. He may make a gesture no one on the Bench has seen before, meant only for them: two fingers laid across his own bitted mouth and then pointed, very deliberately, down, toward the Ledger and the Cells beneath. **Seeds:** the king wants the PCs to find out what the Bench has drafted for the Sayer in his name (and discovers the Silence Clause); the king wants Odo Fane freed and cannot say so; the king wants to visit the Gilded Cells alone, for one hour, and needs the guards' rotation altered. Threatened, he does nothing; there is nothing he can safely do. To kindness, he answers with the open hand, which means *received*. Two Sayers will be in one room, and neither will have said anything yet.

#### Who They Are

Sorrin Vael is tall, narrow and bald, with long yellow fingers stained grey at the tips by slate-dust and blue at the nails by ink. His voice is dry, papery, pitched just loud enough to carry across a small table and no further; men lean toward him to hear, which is how he likes it. He smells of sealing wax and sand. He blinks seldom. Every sentence he speaks has the structure of a staircase with a landing at every second step, and he speaks them so fluently that listeners do not notice, until they try to repeat what he said, that he has said nothing at all. He has a tic that only his wife and his clerks know: when he is tired, his lips move along with other people's words, a half-second behind, like a man taking dictation.

#### Their Story

Born in 581 to the Vael house, a middling Tongue-line of drafters, Sorrin was unbitted in 593 with the first words *I am here, I believe*, which became a family joke and then a family motto. He entered the Bench as a Slate-Clerk at fourteen under Chief Clausewright Annick Morrow, who taught him that a sentence is a building and that the god lives in the cracks. He drafted his first marriage at nineteen. At twenty, in 601, he drafted the Notary's Oath that Jessamy Quill swore before the Company, including the clause, at the Company's request, that no other notary of the Rim would be sworn while she lived. He was proud of it then. He has drafted, by his own count, eleven thousand four hundred sworn documents, among them the Canyon Watch oath after Wending, the anonymous Surety bond Ona Fell carries for Quill (615), and the sentences Lady Imre Dath has spoken in each year of her Seating. He became Chief Clausewright in 628.

He married Merit Ashmore, a Galleries-born archivist, in 606, under a contract of thirty-one pages that he drafted himself and is still, he says, the work he is proudest of. He has never said *yes* to her without an Exit. Their marriage is tender and conducted largely by touch. In 639 he began to swear in his sleep. Merit, who sleeps light, began to write it down.

#### Their Place in the Land

Sorrin interprets the king to the Bench and the Bench to the king, which makes him the most powerful man in Oathen and the most constrained. He chairs the Bench's sessions, assigns its drafting, and approves every sentence of public record before it goes to the carvers. He sets the Readings: the Bench's official judgments of what the god counts. He drafts the Sayer's sentence each year. Everyone with power in Oathen depends on him, and he depends on the clause; he has no army, no fortune beyond his salary, and no friends who are not clients. His limit is the god. He can make a sentence safe; he cannot make the god read it his way.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

He wants the perfect sentence: one that binds exactly what is intended and nothing more. He fears it does not exist, and that the god has been reading his drafts more generously than they deserve, for forty years, for reasons of its own. **Secret (GM may reveal):** Merit's record of his sleep-swearing. The treaty grants the second party "a chair, and the first portion." Sorrin has read it. He has made no sense of it, and he cannot stop. **Secret (GM may reveal):** he drafted the Silence Clause for the king (see Ket the Ninth), and updates it monthly. **Secret he does not know:** the knot at the centre of Oathen. He drafted Quill's oath, Ona's bond, and the Company's instruction to find a successor, as three separate commissions over forty years, and has never laid them side by side.

#### Ties

- **Ket the Ninth** — Sorrin has read the king's every gesture for twelve years and does not know what he thinks. It is the only text that has ever defeated him.
- **Lady Imre Dath** — he visits the Gilded Cells weekly. She turns her ears to him and he reads her his drafts. She has never responded. He suspects she is laughing.
- **Merit Vael** — his wife, his record-keeper, the only person who has heard him say anything unqualified, and only in his sleep.
- **Jessamy Quill** — they have known each other since 601. He drafted her oath. She has never thanked him, which he understands.
- **Judge Thane Urrow** — old allies. Sorrin drafts the sentences Urrow's court imposes. Each thinks the other has the cleaner hands.
- **Brakk** — Brakk calls him "the man who's never said anything" across the canyon, through a speaking-trumpet. Sorrin keeps a file.
- **Varro Esk** — a junior Bencher's nephew who has asked, very charmingly, to see the drafts. Sorrin has said that he would consider it, if able. He will not.

#### In Play

PCs come to Sorrin when they need an oath drafted that will get them into a place or out of one, a Reading on what the god counts, or the Bench's permission for anything. Toward the god he is a scholar toward a text he loves and fears. **If nobody intervenes:** Sorrin finishes draft one hundred of the Sayer's sentence on the eve of Tablenight, and reads it aloud to Merit, in his sleep, in full.

#### Who They Are

The gold bit covers her face from nostrils to chin, soft gold over lead, cast to her mouth and resized every few months, and it is the most beautiful thing in the Cells and the most terrible. Above it, her face has grown long and smooth, the bones softened under it like wax near a flame. Her eyes have closed over with skin, faintly pink, through which, in lamplight, you can see the eyes moving. Small ears, pink, folded, perfectly formed, have bloomed across her cheeks, temples, throat and the backs of her hands like moss on a north wall, and they turn, all together, toward any sound. She sits upright on a felt pallet in a gown of undyed wool. She smells of lead, warm gold, and the thin barley broth she is fed through a reed. When she breathes, the grille of the bit whistles very faintly on a note that the guards, who are deaf, cannot hear, and that the Clausewrights who visit say is always the same note.

#### Their Story

Imre Dath was born in 613, the second child of Lady Senna Dath, into an old house that had eaten the root of Tolm's tongue and married itself close for three hundred years to keep it. She was clever and talkative behind her bit, signing so fast and so freely that her mother had her hands bound at meals, and after her Unbitting in 625 (her first words were *I was heard*), she was the most talkative girl in the Heights, which in Oathen is an insult. Her mother punished her with silence: days at a time in which no one in the house would sign to her. She learned to talk to the servants, and to the floor-walker, Old Pell Sarrow, who taught her the quiet ways through the house.

At twenty-five, at dinner, in Plenty of 638, her brother Corwin told their mother that he had not been to the Lantern Lanes. Imre said, "My brother is a liar." It was a statement of fact, and at the Course it would have stung him. She was further along than anyone knew. Corwin's mouth opened back to his ears with the sound of wet cloth tearing, and it has never closed: he lives in the Dath house still, with a face that cannot hold food, fed through a tube, and it was her sentence, not his lie, that did it. Imre understood at once. She did not speak again. The Bench came for her that night; Garl Tome fitted the first gold bit at dawn; Old Pell swept the floor.

She has been unmuzzled twice, each time on the Bench's vote, to say a sentence drafted by Sorrin Vael. In Grace 639: *The bridges of Tacit shall hold through Lack.* They held. In Carving 640: *The canyon wells shall not run dry this Lack.* They have not. In Carving 640, on the night the king hummed, she began to write.

#### Their Place in the Land

Imre is the most powerful person in Oathen and has no power at all. She is the Bench's instrument, its deterrent and its terror; a sentence a year from her mouth is the greatest act of government in the land, and every land on the Table now wants one. Her existence holds up the Bench's authority, because the Bench is the only body that can unbit her, and her cell is guarded by the Canyon Watch's best, deafened by surgery and bitted. Garl Tome resizes her bit; Sorrin Vael reads her drafts; a Bench physician checks her weekly and does not stay. No one has ever asked her what she wants.

- **A reed** — for broth through the grille.

### Halvar Stane — Echo-Warden

[stat: Halvar Stane — Echo-Warden]

#### Who They Are

Halvar is gaunt and bow-legged, burned dark by thirty years in the gorges, with a long grey face and a habit of tilting his head to the left like a listening dog, because the right ear is gone: cut away at his initiation in 603, healed into a pale knot of scar. The left is failing with age. He smells of felt, quicklime and goat-tallow. He moves without a sound, even indoors, and people find him behind them. He speaks rarely, and in handtalk by touch when he can.

#### Their Story

Born in 583 in Sheathmouth, Halvar walked the Sheath with his mother as a boy, roped and gagged, and learned to hear the scraps of old words in the walls. He joined the Wardens at twenty and took the knife to his right ear. From 611 he served at Sworn Gorge, and in 626 he became Warden of the Wall. He listened to the murmur for thirteen years, and in 634, pressing his good ear to the stone on a still night, made out two words: *set* and *again*. He reported it and would not repeat them. In 639 he began to sleepwalk, and Tacet Morrow found him at the wall one night with his lips moving. He was relieved and sent to track echoes in the eastern canyons. This Carving the murmur stopped. Three herders near Wending broke the way the people of Sowe broke.

#### Their Place in the Land

Halvar is the Wardens' most experienced tracker, a roving Tracker answerable to no Gorge-Warden, hired by towns, drovers and the Bench to hunt and silence old words (30 L a silencing, as the Rim Market chapter lists). He walks a circuit of the eastern canyons that takes a season, sleeping in Warden huts, listening at every narrows. He buys echo-stones of Sworn Gorge wherever they are sold and grinds them to sand, which he pours into the river. Every Warden defers to him; Bafflers fight to be assigned to his rope. The Bench reads his reports. His limit is that the Wardens' charter forbids him the one place he knows best.

#### What They Carry

- **Mortar and pestle** — for grinding echo-stones to sand.
- **A knot-map** — a cord of the posted gorges, by touch.
- **A tether-rope** — he ties himself to his bed at night.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

He wants to go back to the wall. He fears that he is already bound, and that this is how it feels. **Secret (GM may reveal):** the two words. **Secret he does not know:** the gorge went quiet because the echo is no longer in the rock. It came out in his sleep, through his mouth, on the last night at the wall, and it travels in him. The herders who broke had shared his fire.

#### Ties

- **Ona Fell** — his cousin, whom he visits for no reason.

Threatened, he vanishes into stone. To kindness he answers by touch. **If nobody intervenes:** he walks into Tacit in his sleep on Tablenight eve, and says it.

### Mirren Osk — the Handtalker

> (signed, with the left hand only, the right in splints) Thirty years I made things that were beautiful and cost nothing. I always suspected that was too good to be true.
>> — Mirren Osk, to a student, Grace 641

[stat: Mirren Osk — the Handtalker]
Land & Cut: Oathen · Cut 3 (Tongue-line, the lesser Osk house) · Regrowth 3 (the Taste) · Hunger 3
Age & Station: 40; the greatest poet in handtalk; mistress of the School of Hands
Calling & Standing: none (a poet; the handtalk schools count her first among them)

#### Who They Are

Mirren is elegant and dark, tall for an Oathener, with a long neck she holds very straight and long hands she oils nightly with almond-oil, and three fingers of the right hand in ivory splints, knit crooked so that the hand rests in a half-open shape like a question never finished. She signs with the left now, more slowly, and the slowness makes her poems sadder. She smells of almond. Her eyes are restless; they go to everyone's hands before their faces. Her speech, when she uses it, is clipped and sparing; she has always thought spoken words were ugly. She is frightened, angry, and guiltily excited, because if handtalk binds, then it was always a real language, and her poems were always true.

#### Their Story

Born 601 to the lesser Osk house, Mirren was signing poems behind her bit at six, nursery verses that went around Tacit's children like a fever. She founded the School of Hands in 625 and has composed some four thousand poems, many of them vows: of love, of loyalty, of forever. In Plenty of 640, at a party in the Dath house, her patron Lady Senna Dath commissioned a love poem to herself. Mirren signed it before four hundred guests. She did not mean it. Three fingers snapped mid-line.

#### Their Place in the Land

Mirren is the voice of the Left Hand: the proof that Oathen could be lovely without risk. Her School of Hands on the Shadeward fifth tier teaches two hundred students the high handtalk; her poems are signed at weddings, funerals, Unbittings and in the Felt Market by people who cannot afford to say anything aloud. The Tongue-lines paid her for verses, and she lived well on it. Since her fingers broke, handtalk breaks everywhere, the School has half its students, and the Galleries blame her, as though she taught it to bind. Strangers stop her on the bridges and sign *was it you?* She does not answer, because she does not know.

#### What They Carry

- **Ivory splints** — on three fingers.
- **Almond-oil** — nightly.
- **The Book of Hands** — her poems in written notation, now possibly binding.
- **Kid gloves** — she wears them in public, to stop herself.
- **A sworn note** — Senna Dath's fee, uncashed.
- **Cassian's letters** — full of lies, she knows, and she treasures them.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

She wants to know whether her old poems are coming due. **Secret (GM may reveal):** the poem was not for Senna. In her heart it was for Cassian Wry, the crown's Blank, whom she has loved for three years, and that is why it broke. **Secret she does not know:** Sorrin Vael's Bench is drafting a Reading of Hands that would rule all handtalk to have bound always; thirty years of poems would come due in a night.

#### Ties

- **Cassian Wry** — her lover, the one man whose words mean nothing, and the only one she believes.
- **Lady Senna Dath** — her patron; humiliated; dangerous.
- **Lady Imre Dath** — heard the poem through the rock and knows who it was for.
- **Little Rue** — subject of a poem Rue has asked her never to sign.
- **Sorrin Vael** — he wants her at the Bench for the Reading. She has refused.

#### In Play

PCs meet her seeking a translator, a witness, or someone who understands what the Left Hand means now. **Seeds:** she hires the PCs to find every living person she ever signed a vow-poem to, in thirty years, and learn whether any of them have broken; Lady Senna Dath demands the poem be signed again, correctly, before the same four hundred guests, and Mirren needs someone to stand beside her when she does; Cassian asks the PCs to tell her something true for him, since nothing he says can be. Threatened, she signs something devastating with one hand. To kindness she answers with a poem, slowly, and it costs her. Toward the god she is, for the first time, curious. **If nobody intervenes:** the Reading of Hands passes in Lack, and on the night it is carved, Mirren's left hand begins, one finger at a time, to come due.

Handsome in a hungry way, Cassian is lean and quick, with black curls, a crooked smile, and a laugh that makes Oatheners flinch, because it is loud and he does not care where it echoes. #### Their Story

He was born in 607 in Low Dray to Scrapling fig-pickers, as Tam Hollis. At six, during a nursery cleaning, his bit came off and he said, "I'll eat the moon," and his mother screamed, and nothing happened. A Cutwright survey confirmed it within the month: no Taste, no echo, no tell. The crown paid his parents four thousand lacks and a sworn note for more, and he has not seen them since; he does not know whether they are alive, and nobody will tell him, because what they would tell him would be true. He was raised in the Muted House alongside the young king, the only child in it who was allowed to talk, trained in spycraft by a foreign tutor, and has been sent abroad and among the Tongue-lines for twenty years to say what Oatheners cannot: to bluff envoys, frighten defaulters, seduce secrets out of the Heights, and swear to anything. He is the crown's most valuable tool: the only Oathener who can lie, bluff, threaten and promise without cost. Abroad, he is a diplomat's knife; at home, he is the Bench's interrogator of last resort, the man who can say *your wife has confessed* to a suspect when she has not. He lives in a gilded, felt-walled apartment on the second tier of the Sunward Wall with two bitted guards and a Clausewright minder, Annet Gorse, who records everything he says in a ledger, with a column for *true* that is almost empty. He goes nowhere unwatched. ### Judge Thane Urrow — the Court of the Sound

> "The accused will repeat the oath, in its words, from the beginning. The court will listen. The court will not interrupt. The god does not need the court's help."
>> — Judge Thane Urrow, opening his eleven thousandth trial, Lack 640

[stat: Judge Thane Urrow — the Court of the Sound]
Land & Cut: Oathen · Cut 3 (Tongue-line, Urrow) · Regrowth 6 (the Appetite) · Hunger 5
Age & Station: 70; presiding judge of the Breaking courts since 601
Calling & Standing: Clausewright · Standing 4 (Senior of the Bench)

Skills: Clause 6, Lore 4, Instinct 4, Resolve 4, Reckoning 2, Persuade 2
Flesh 10 · Guard 11 · Armor 0 · Fray 3

Gifts: Echo-Ear. Hold to It. The Judge's Ear (he hears a Breaking coming a breath before it lands, as a creak in the accused's bones; no roll).

Knacks: Butcher's Calm, Unflinching
Derangements: none admitted. He has begun to count the sounds.
Carries: red robes; the Book of Breakings; a closed-hand stone; a cloth; a thumb-ring; an ear-trumpet.
Dread: 1
[/stat]

#### Who They Are

Small and neat, with a white beard trimmed to a point and a scalp spotted brown by age, Thane Urrow wears red, because his robes are spattered so often that the court gave up on black in 604. His voice is like a knife drawn slowly from a sheath: unhurried, quiet, and impossible not to listen to. He smells of vinegar, which his clerk sponges on the bench after each session, and of the clove-pomander he holds to his nose when a Breaking is a bad one. His hands are perfectly still. His face, during a trial, is the face of a man listening to music, and at the moment the oath is finished he leans very slightly forward, ear-trumpet raised, and waits.

#### Their Story

Born in 571 to the Urrow house, a minor Tongue-line of court clerks, Thane trained under the Bench and was raised to the Breaking courts at thirty, in 601, when the old judge, Hesper Kell, could no longer bear the sound. He swore his judicial oath before the Mute King Orvel: *I will judge by the god's hand and not my own, and I will take neither pleasure nor pity from it.* He has presided over eleven thousand trials since, at a rate of five or six a day, and has never once reversed a verdict, because he has never once given one. The god gives them. He writes them down.
In 618 his only son Davin, a soldier of the Canyon Watch, was accused of deserting a post he had sworn to keep. He came before his father's court. He repeated the oath. His knees went backward like a heron's, on the sand, in front of his father, and Urrow wrote it in the Book of Breakings in the same hand as every other. Davin went down to the Breakdowns on a litter. His father has not spoken his name since. Every night he reads that line of the book again.

#### Their Place in the Land

His court is the only perfectly just court on the Table, and he is right, and he hates it. Every Breaking trial in Tacit comes before him, and the Bench sends him the Question trials of the great houses too, because a Tongue-line will answer *did you do it?* only to a judge of rank. He sits from dawn to noon, six days in seven, on a stone chair in a small square court on the Bench Terrace, floored in sand like the Hall. The Canyon Watch brings the accused; the Sentence-Readers take the convicted away to have their sentences drafted. His power is total and entirely passive: he decides nothing. What he decides is what the court will listen to, and when, and the Bench has learned that the order in which Judge Urrow hears his cases is itself a kind of politics.

#### What They Carry

- **Red robes** — three sets, vinegar-washed.
- **The Book of Breakings** — every trial, every part.
- **A closed-hand stone** — the court's symbol, worn smooth by his palm.
- **A linen cloth** — for the blood.
- **One thumb-ring** — the judicial oath.
- **An ear-trumpet** — brass, for his failing hearing; he will not miss a sound.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

He wants to know whether the god still means it: whether the late rain, the slipping seal and the breaking fingers are the god growing careless, and therefore whether his eleven thousand verdicts were justice or only habit. He fears the day he is asked to repeat his own judicial oath before his own court. **Secret (GM may reveal):** he has come to take pleasure in the sound. Not in the pain; in the sound, the exact, apt, final crack of a truth arriving in a body. If he repeated his oath he would break, at Weight 4, and the part that would answer is the ear. **Secret he does not know:** Davin is now one of Brakk's Splints, and has asked Brakk for one thing in return for his service: to be the one who petitions the court.

#### Ties

- **Ket the Ninth** — watches his trials from behind a screen, and has begun to close his hand at the end of each. Urrow has noticed, and does not know what it means.
- **Jessamy Quill** — has declined, twice, to repeat her Notary's oath in his court as a demonstration for students. He suspects why, and admires her for it.
- **Brakk** — the Forsworn voice that shouts his name through a trumpet each trial-day. Urrow does not know Brakk shelters his son.
- **Davin Urrow** — his son, broken in his court in 618. He reads the line every night.

#### In Play

Threatened, he is unmoved; he has seen everything a body can do. To kindness he is courteous and puzzled. Toward the god he is a believer who has begun, appallingly, to enjoy his faith. **If nobody intervenes:** at the trial of a Forsworn in Lack, as the Breaking lands, Thane Urrow smiles, openly, in front of the court, and his own ribs creak.
