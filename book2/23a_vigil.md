# The Vigil: Every Hour Still Behind the Eyes

> Count your nudger's blinks. Then count your own. Then stop counting, because the counting is soft, and soft is how it gets you.
>> — Slope advice, given to every child at the Coming-Heavy

> We do not have days here, Mistress. We have one day. It began in 341 and it has not ended. Everything since is a long afternoon.
>> — Hourkeeper Maud Sexton, to a Rim Road factor who asked what day it was

The twenty-four bells of Wenna Startle, mirror-climber of the Lamplighters' Guild, Second Rank, as she told them to a Company clerk at Lampwick Halt who had asked what a working day was like up there, and wrote them down because he could not believe her.
First bell. You come off the roof with the rag-bag and the polish-pot and your knees going, and your brother Florian is waiting at the foot of the ladder with his hand out for the pot, because it's his shift on the mirrors now and mine on the house. He pinches my wrist and I pinch his. That's hello and goodbye. We haven't been in the same room sitting still since we were six. Nobody sits.
Second bell. Rouse. Half a tab under the lip, the Bitterhouse stamp facing my gum so it's the eye that dissolves first, because my gran said that was luckier. Bread with grit in it so it crunches. Vinegar. The Waker on our corner is old Hesk with the bad hip. He looks at my face the way you look at a pot to see if it's boiling. I say "awake" before he can ask. He nods. He has the needle out anyway. He always has the needle out.
Third bell to fifth. House. Ma's on the stool at the hackle, the one-leg stool, and she sways a little when she works and you watch the sway. If the sway goes slow you shout. The baby is a starer still, three, lies in the basket with her eyes open looking at the lamp, and doesn't need anything, and you pray to whatever's left that she doesn't need anything for another year. You don't look at her too long. Looking too long at a quiet thing is how it starts.
Sixth bell. The Shouting. Every house on Cinder Row opens its shutters and shouts for a count of thirty. Not words. Just noise. My ma does it like she's angry. My da used to do it like a song. He's in fourteen thousand and something. I know the number. I'm not going to say it to you, because you'd write it down.
Seventh bell to the twelfth. Back up. The mirrors on our stretch are forty-one big and two hundred small, and the smoke puts a skin on them every hour, brown and greasy, and if you don't take it off the light in the lane goes amber and soft, and amber and soft puts people down. So you take it off. You're on the tin on your knees with the wind at your back and the Bonfire in Ludmere Square making the whole sky the colour of a scab, and you polish till you see your own face and then you polish your own face off it, because you don't want to look at that either.
Thirteenth bell. Something walks across the roof three houses down. Tall. Grey coat. I didn't see it. I'm telling you I didn't see it, because I wasn't one of the thirty-one, so it didn't happen.
Fourteenth bell. Florian goes grey. You get to know the look: the jaw goes, then the shoulders, then the eyes stop going anywhere. I'm off shift but I'm on the roof anyway, because you are, because you don't leave a climber on a roof alone. I get my pin into the back of his hand. He comes back swearing, and I love him so much I could be sick.
Fifteenth to the nineteenth. Market. The Pinchmarket never shuts, so it never opens either; you just go into it like going into weather. Oil's doubled. Tabs are restamped again, the eye on them's a little smaller. Ma wants burr-cloth for the baby, who will need it soon, because they all do. A nudger-child at the east end has her board up: NOT ONE BLINK IN SIX MONTHS. She's maybe eight. She's swaying.
Twentieth bell. Board in the lane. Old Pell from the corner is on it, laid out flat in his good coat, rouse-heart, and we all queue to touch his eyes. You touch them and you think, he's lying down. He's lying down and nobody can say anything. I'll tell you the truth, mister, because you've slept and you'll sleep again tonight in that shed down the road: everyone in the queue was jealous. Everyone. The children too.
Twenty-first to twenty-fourth. You wait for first bell. You don't do anything else. You stand at the window and pinch, and count the lamps on the Row, and count the lamps again, and somewhere around the third time through you notice you've started counting something else, slow, and it isn't lamps, and it isn't in your voice.
Then it's first bell again, and Florian's at the ladder with his hand out.

The core chapter tells what the Vigil is: a city that ate the god of sleep, and that since 341 A.G. has lost to a long warm room everyone who closes their eyes. This chapter is about what that means on an ordinary street at the fourteenth bell. It is about who empties the night-pots in a city with no night, what a nudger earns, how a man is tried for a crime he committed in a two-second sleep, what the Pilgrim Road smells like in Plenty, and who on the Lid can make whom do what. The Vigil is not a city under siege. It is a city that has made the siege its economy, its manners and its religion, and has been living in it for three hundred years.

[box: How the Vigil Tells Time]
There is no night on the Lid, so the Vigil keeps time by bells and nothing else. The civil day is twenty-four bells long, struck from the Pendulum Hall and repeated by every tower in the city; the quarters between them are struck too, in a lighter tone called the *pinch*. The Hourkeeper's office divides the day into six watches of four bells each (first watch, second watch and so on up to the sixth), and this is how the bell-sheets, the Seeming record and the Dormitory register are dated. The Wakers, the Bitterhouse and the law, however, reckon in three *long watches* of eight bells, because a shift is eight bells and a sentence in the Rattlehouse is served in long watches. Visitors are always confused. Vigilants are always confused too, but are too tired to mention it. A Vigilant asked the time will tell you the bell and the quarter; asked the date, they will usually tell you the day of the season and then, after a pause, check.
[/box]

## Districts and Settlements

The city on the Lid has no name of its own. Before 342 it was Ludmere. Since then the city and the land have been one word, *the Vigil*, and Vigilants who must distinguish them say *up top* for the city and *the Lid* for the stone it stands on. It is home to some four hundred thousand waking souls, and to the sixty thousand who are not, and it is so tightly built that from the roof of the Nodding Stair it looks like one enormous lit machine. Within it there are districts, but the boundaries between them are drawn in light: every quarter has its own Bonfire, its own bell-tower and its own Waker station, and a Vigilant names their home by the fire they live under.

### The City Up Top

#### The Height

The highest ground on the Lid, at its northern end, where the plateau lifts into a low hump of grey stone and the Eye-line houses stand on it like teeth. The Height is the brightest-lit quarter after Lampwrights' Row, and the quietest: a hush of a different kind from the dangerous hush of a grey afternoon, the hush of people who do not need to shout to stay awake because they cannot do anything else. The lidless live here, some four thousand of them, with their households of eye-wetters, nudgers, clerks and valets, perhaps forty thousand souls in all. Its streets are wide and paved with ridged granite so that carriage-bearers cannot drowse into a shuffle; its houses are tall and narrow and glazed on every side, because the lidless like to see. The Nodding Stair rises at its summit, the Pendulum Hall beside it, and the Cutwrights' College keeps its Vigil house on a corner of Regard Street, with the shutters always open.

It smells of rosewater warmed to blood heat, of beeswax, and under that of the faint stale sweetness of eyes that are always wet; the gutters of the Height carry more rosewater than rain. Its sound is the soft clink of silver bowls and the whisper of sable brushes, every half minute, on every corner, in every carriage, like a city of painters. It trades in nothing; it owns things instead. The great houses hold the leases on the Bitterhouse's staremoss cliffs, the patents of the lamp-burners, and the debt of the Slope.

**Custom: the Regard.** The lidless do not bow. They greet one another by holding each other's gaze for a full count of ten, which neither can break, and which the lower orders are not permitted to attempt. To be *held in regard* by a lidless noble is an honour; to be held for longer than ten is a warning. Servants on the Height learn to look at the collarbone.

**Danger: being remembered.** Every lidless person on the Height recalls everything they have seen. A stranger who misbehaves on Regard Street is not arrested. They are simply remembered, perfectly, by everyone who saw, and in thirty years some one of them will mention it, word for word, at the worst moment of the stranger's life.

#### Ludmere Old Town

The oldest quarter, south of the Height and spilling around Ludmere Square, where the city's first Bonfire burned the beds in 342 and has burned ever since. The streets here are the twisted, soft-cornered lanes of the old pilgrim town, built for people who walked slowly and quietly, and the Vigil has spent three centuries making them loud. Every lintel is hung with bells. Every step has been recut so that no two are the same height. The old dream-houses, the oracles' halls where pilgrims once paid to sleep near Iss, have been gutted and turned into rouse-houses, counting-houses and Waker stations, and the old carvings over their doors (sleeping lambs, closed eyes, a long low shape with its knees drawn up) have been chiselled off or hung with tin.

Ludmere smells of the Bonfire: hot iron, pitch, the burnt-sugar reek of rouse-houses and the vinegar the cookshops splash on everything. It sounds like a riot that has gone on for three hundred years and has become a habit. The Pinchmarket fills three of its squares; the great tower of the Ludmere bells, where the deaf ringer Thaddeus Knell works, stands over the fourth. Here are the guildhalls, the bell-sheet printers, the rouse-houses where clerks stand at chest-high counters arguing to stay awake, and the cheapest decent lodging in the city: the *waking-houses*, long rooms with sloped floors and hanging straps, where a traveller may rent a strap and a share of a hired nudger by the bell.

**Custom: the Argument.** Rouse-houses in Ludmere keep a slate on the wall with a topic chalked on it, changed every bell (*Was the Lack a punishment? Is fish meat? Does the Warden-Prime weep?*), and patrons are expected to take a side and shout. A man who agrees with everyone in a rouse-house is thrown out as a danger to the room. Many lifelong friendships in the Vigil began as a bell-long quarrel about whether the moon has a back.

**Danger: the old stones.** Ludmere is built on the original bedrock of the pilgrim town, and in some cellars and courtyards the stone is still faintly warm, the way the Pillow is warm. Nobody knows which. A courtyard where people keep pausing, putting a hand on the wall and smiling, gets a Waker post and then, when that does not work, a brick floor.

#### Lampwrights' Row and the Burners' Yards

The core book describes the Row as the brightest street in the world, and from the front it is: a mile of lit shopfronts and mirrored roofs where a walker casts no shadow. Behind the shopfronts, on both sides, are the Burners' Yards, and nobody takes their constitutional there. The Yards are a maze of brick sheds, soot-black chimneys, tallow vats, oil-cisterns and the nine great furnaces where the city burns its dead, and where, since about 636, the Lamplighters' Guild has been doing something else with them. The Yards are walled. Guild climbers patrol the wall-tops with lit poles. The men and women who work the furnaces are called Burners, wear leather to the eyes, and are paid better than anyone in the city below a Watcher, and do not talk in rouse-houses.

The front of the Row smells of hot glass, metal polish and lamp-oil. The Yards smell of fat. It is the smell a Fatlander knows from the rendering-sheds at Sated, thick and yellow and somehow sweet, and it has been creeping out over the wall into the Row for five years, so that the fine ladies of the Height taking their constitutionals find themselves, without knowing why, hungry. The Row trades in every kind of light: mirrors, lenses, burners, wicks, lamp-glass, mirror-lanterns at twenty-five lacks apiece. The Yards trade in oil and tallow, and the Guild's books show every barrel as *imported*.

**Custom: lighting the lamp.** When a citizen is burned on the Row, the family may pay to have a particular lamp lit from the ashes, and the Guild keeps a register of which lamp holds whom. Families walk past *their* lamp on the way to work and touch the post. The poor go into the general flame and have no lamp, and say instead that their dead are *everywhere on the Row*, which is a comfort, until you think about it.

**Danger: the Guild's economy.** Bodies brought to the Yards for burning are no longer always burned. The unclaimed, the poor, the foreign and the Rattlehouse dead go into the vats. A family that comes back to ask for ashes is given ashes. Nobody has asked whose.

#### Bedside

The district that has grown up around the Dormitory on the lower south side of the Lid. It is the newest quarter of the city and the most orderly, a grid of straight streets laid out by Benedikt Lour to carry Dormitory carts as quickly as possible from anywhere in the city to the Receiving Gate, and its people are the people the Dormitory employs: orderlies, sheet-washers, bed-smiths, register clerks, carters, and the felt-makers whose slippers keep the halls silent. Around them live the families of the Counted who could afford to move closer, in tall narrow houses with windows that look onto the Dormitory's long grey flank, so that they can see the building their loved one breathes in.

Bedside smells of lye and boiled linen, from the great laundries that change sixty thousand sheets every Plate, and, close to the walls, of the Dormitory itself: clean linen, warm skin, and the faint milky sweetness of bodies that do not sweat. Its sound is unusual for the Vigil. It is quieter. The Watch has ruled that the Counted must not be disturbed by noise, in case noise disturbs the number, and so Bedside alone of all the districts has fewer bells and a law against shouting within fifty paces of the walls. The result is that Bedside has the highest rate of blinking in the city, and its Waker station, the Felt House, is staffed by the hardest Needles in the force. The district trades in visiting: stools for an hour (illegal to sit on for longer), cut flowers brought up from the terraces, paper charms, letters read aloud at the foot of a bed by hired readers, and the copying of bed-numbers onto brass tags that families wear around their necks.

**Custom: the Visiting Hour.** Every family with a sleeper has a visiting bell, assigned by the Dormitory clerks and stamped on their brass tag, and at that bell they may enter by the Visitors' Gate and stand at the foot of their bed for an hour. They talk. Most of what is said in the Dormitory in a given watch is people telling the Counted what they had for breakfast, who has married, whether the roof leaks. The orderlies hear it all and do not repeat it. The Visiting Hour is the one hour of the day when Vigilants are permitted, by custom, to cry without being slapped.

**Danger: the breathing.** Anyone who spends long near the Dormitory's walls finds their breath slowing to match the four-to-the-minute tide of the Counted. A Bedside householder who leaves a window open on the Dormitory side can lose a whole family in an afternoon, matched, slowing, smiling, one after another.

#### The Slope

The core book gives the Slope as the slum, built of tin and plank down the steep south side of the Lid below the Dormitory. On the ground it is more than that: it is forty thousand households in a hundred lanes, a city within a city with its own law, its own rouse, its own bell-names and its own pride. The lanes are named for trades: Thimble Lane (seamstresses and rope-twisters), Lanternside (wick-braiders), Gallow Street (laundresses), Cinder Row, Gutter Lane, Pinchbeck Steps, Tallow Hollow. They are too steep and narrow for Waker carts, and so the Slope has its own carts, small two-wheeled barrows with wooden wheels that rattle on purpose, pushed by children. The houses are built one on top of the next, so that the floor of one family is the roof of the family below, and every roof is tin, so that the Slope in rain sounds like a war.

The Slope smells of lamp-smoke from bad oil, of dung burned in the Bonfires because there is not enough wood, of sweat and piss and wet tin, and of cut rouse, which smells of chalk and ash and, this year, of the sea. It sounds of children. Nudger-children shout the bell on the quarter, all over the Slope at once, a thin high wave of voices calling *awake, awake, the pinch, awake*, which Slope people find more reassuring than any tower. It trades in labour (rope, wick, laundry, rag, the hauling of anything) and in what the Height does not admit to wanting: cheap nudgers, cut rouse, Tuckers' minutes, and stolen sleep.

**Custom: the Ring.** Slope families nudge in a ring. Everyone in the household has someone to watch and someone watching them, and the ring is set every first bell with the youngest who can nudge watching the oldest, so that grandmothers are kept awake by children, and children learn early what a grown face looks like when it goes. If a ring breaks, the household calls *ring!* down the stairs and the family below sends someone up. Nobody on the Slope is permitted to be unwatched in their own home, by custom older than law.

**Danger: the afternoon.** When a grey, windless, overcast afternoon settles on the Slope, whole families go down one after another, the last awake moving between the bodies slapping and screaming until they go quiet too. Slope people fear the soft weather the way the fen fears floods. They also fear the Dormitory carts, which come down to collect and, on a bad afternoon, have been known to load people who were only very, very still.

#### Blackmouth

The north side of the Lid, around the Bitterhouse: a district of drying-sheds, roasting-ovens, grinding-mills and the tenements of the rouse-workers who staff them. It is called Blackmouth for its people's teeth. A Blackmouth worker's mouth is black by twenty and empty by twenty-five, and the district's children, who sweep the drying-floors for crumbs, have teeth the colour of tar before their second set is in. The workers are the most alert people in the city, quick, twitching, sharp-tongued, and the shortest-lived, their hearts giving out by forty; the rouse-heart funerals in Blackmouth come so often that the street-boards where the dead are laid out are never empty, and the neighbours queue to touch the eyes of one corpse while the next is being carried down.

Blackmouth smells of burnt sugar and iron, and makes visitors' teeth ache within an hour. The roasting-ovens give off a heat that the Watch considers dangerous, and so every oven-house has a Waker on the door and a bucket of cliff-water on a rope over the stokers' heads, tipped at random. Its sound is the grinding of the mills, a continuous low roar like a river, and the coughing. Blackmouth coughs black; the rouse-dust gets into the lungs. It trades in rouse, of course, in every grade, and in staremoss, which comes in by rope-lift from the cliffs below in bales of grey lichen that smell of wet stone and pepper.

**Custom: the Black Kiss.** Blackmouth people greet each other with a smile that shows the gums, and a person with all their teeth is treated with suspicion as either a spy from the Height or a liar who does not take his rouse. When a Blackmouth worker loses the last tooth, the family holds a small party, the *Black Kiss*, where everyone puts a pinch of raw rouse on the new-bare gum, and it is considered the beginning of adulthood.

**Danger: the Proving Rooms.** Under the Bitterhouse, Dr. Crane's chemists test new strains on prisoners from the Rattlehouse. Blackmouth knows. On some watches the ground over the Proving Rooms shakes faintly, as though something underneath were thumping on it with both fists, very fast, the way a heart does near the end.

#### Needlegate

The Wakers' quarter, at the foot of the Height around the Rattlehouse. It is a district of barracks, schools, standing-stalls, armouries, and the houses of Waker families, who intermarry and send their children to the Rattlehouse as their parents did, so that most Wakers in the Vigil are related to each other and all of them were trained by Sister Agathe Pinch. Needlegate is the loudest district after the Rattlehouse itself: every house has a rattle on its door, and Waker children play at patrols in the street, shaking their wooden toys at each other and screaming *thank you!* when they are pricked.

It smells of oiled steel and carbolic, and of the blood that is always on a Waker's hands, a little, from the morning's work. It sounds of rattles, and of the Rattlehouse's chimes, which sound every few breaths day and night through its tin-lined walls. It trades in needles (a Waker's long needle costs six lacks and is ground in Needlegate's own forges), in rattles, in the Atlas of the Quick in its several editions, and in pain, which off-duty Wakers sell privately as *a good waking* to households that cannot afford Jack Tenterhook.

**Custom: the Thanking.** When a Waker's needle brings a citizen back from a blink, the citizen is expected to say *thank you* as soon as they can speak, and the Waker answers *awake*. In Needlegate this is not a courtesy but a rite. Waker children are pricked by their parents on their fourth birthday, at the Coming-Heavy, and must say *thank you* through the scream, and the whole street applauds. A Waker who has been thanked ten thousand times receives a small brass needle for the collar. There are very few.

**Danger: off-duty Wakers.** A Waker on a rouse binge after a long watch sees drowsiness everywhere. The district's taverns post a sign, *Needles at the Door*, and most Wakers comply. Most.

#### The Glass Spur

A bare finger of rock projecting from the windward edge of the Lid, where the cold Grace winds hit hardest and nobody wants to live. The Glass House stands here: the long greenhouse of green glass divided into lamplit cells, where the contagious yawners are kept. Around it a small, odd district has grown up of the people who serve it: speaking-tube fitters, muzzle-makers, the keepers' families, and the Lamplighters' Glass Crew, who keep the Spur lit more brightly than any street but the Row, on a standing order that nobody can find the origin of. The houses on the Spur are built with their backs to the Glass House and no windows on that side.

The Spur smells of cold wind, wet stone and saddle-leather from the muzzle-makers' benches, and of the lamp-oil that is burned there in quantities that the Guild's accounts quietly treat as a sacred expense. Its sound is the wind, which howls along the glass, and the tubes, which carry voices from the cells in a thin, tinny murmur that the Spur's people learn not to hear. It trades in muzzles, mostly, and in visits: families from all over the city come to the Glass House to speak through the tubes, and pay a keeper a crumb to stand beside them and slap them if they begin to yawn.

**Custom: the Turned Back.** Spur people never look directly at the Glass House if they can help it, and when they must pass it they walk backwards. Children are taught that if a yawner catches your eye through the glass you will *catch the jaw*. It is not quite true. It is not quite false.

**Danger: the cells.** A cell door left unlatched, a muzzle worn through, a tube-cap left off, and a yawn may get out into the Spur on the wind. The Spur's houses each keep a pot and a spoon by the door for the alarm, and when the Glass House bell rings the whole district bangs pots and runs upwind.

### Beyond the City

The plateau itself is all city, but the Vigil is more than the plateau. Its cliffs are farmed and mined, its lower slopes are terraced, the heather country below the border stones belongs to it, and the long Pilgrim Road from the Rim is lined with the little places that live on the traffic. Perhaps sixty thousand Vigilants live outside the city, and the city does not think much about any of them except when it needs something.

#### Lampwick Halt and the Last Beds

The Rim Road Company's waystation at the foot of the Pilgrim Road, where the plateau road leaves the Rim. It is two places a quarter mile apart. Lampwick Halt itself is a walled inn-town with a hundred lamps burning on its walls day and night, built to accommodate Vigilant merchants who will not stop where they cannot see: here is the Company toll-gate, the stables, the rouse-sellers and the bone-slate news board. A quarter mile down the road, on the Rim side of a stone marker that Vigilant law treats as the edge of the world, stand the Sleeping Sheds, long low shuttered dormitories full of beds, which the travellers of the Rim call *the Last Beds* and which are run by Factor Joss Mallory for the Company. Foreigners bound up the Pilgrim Road take their last sleep here before months awake. Vigilants coming down stand in the Sheds' doorway, sometimes for hours, looking.

The Halt smells of horse, oil and Company bread. The Sheds smell of straw ticking, wool blankets, other people's sleep, and Mallory's pipe; and to a Vigilant, that smell is the most obscene and beautiful thing on the Table. The Halt's sound is the bustle of any Rim station; the Sheds' sound is snoring, which Vigilant travellers have been known to weep at. The Halt trades in passage, rouse, nudgers for hire, mirror-lanterns and maps of the Sleepers' Mile. The Sheds trade in beds, at a lack a night, and in the porters who drag Vigilants away from them.

**Custom: the Last Look.** Vigilants travelling home stop at the Sheds' door to look at the sleepers inside, and the Company charges them a crumb for the privilege. It is considered bad luck to leave without paying, and worse luck to stay longer than a count of a hundred.

**Danger: the doorway.** Every few weeks a Vigilant at the Sheds' door simply walks in and lies down. Mallory's porters are paid to stop them, and usually do. The innkeeper's book of those they did not stop has two hundred and nine names in it.

#### Pilgrim's Stoop

Halfway up the Pilgrim Road, where the road climbs from the heather onto the first shoulder of the Lid, stands the Stoop: a village of three hundred around a drovers' changing-post, an old pilgrims' chapel with its carvings chiselled away, and a long stone wall built at exactly the height of a tired man's shoulders and studded on top with iron. The wall is the Stoop's whole reason for being. In the old days pilgrims climbing to Ludmere would lean on it to rest. Now Vigilant caravan-drivers coming up from the Rim, who have been awake since the Halt, lean on it for the count of ten that the law allows, with a nudger beside them, and it is the last place on the road where leaning is legal.

The Stoop smells of wet heather, mule-dung and rouse brewed in great iron cauldrons, sold by the cup at a crumb. It sounds of mule-bells, and of the Stoop's dogs, which are bred and paid to bark; every household keeps one, and the village pays a tithe of meat to keep them hungry and loud. It trades in fresh mules, rouse, salt mutton, and the services of its nudgers, who are mostly old women who walk beside a caravan for a day for two lacks and then walk home.

**Custom: the Count of Ten.** At the Stoop wall a traveller leans, and the nudger counts to ten aloud, and at ten slaps them. A Stoop nudger who counts slowly is a murderer. A Stoop nudger who counts fast is a cheat. They are paid by the lean, and argue about the speed of ten constantly.

**Danger: the drift.** Fog comes up out of the heather on still mornings in Carving, soft and white and silent, and on fog days the Stoop locks its dogs out to bark at the road. Caravans caught in the fog between the Stoop and the plateau have been found standing in perfect order on the road, every mule and every driver, upright and asleep.

#### Sorrel Steps

The terraced farmland on the long southern slope of the Lid below the Slope, where the crops rose in a week on the morning after the Nine Nights and have been raised ever since. The Steps are a staircase of narrow fields, walled with dry stone, running down the hillside for three miles, growing sorrel, turnips, bitter kale, rye, and the hard short barley the Vigil makes its gritty bread from, with cliff-goats grazing the wall-tops. Some twelve thousand people live here in stone farmhouses with tin roofs, and the Steps feed perhaps a third of the city; the rest comes up the Pilgrim Road in Fatlands barrels.

The Steps smell of manure, crushed sorrel and goat; the air is cleaner here than up top, and city people come down on their rest-bells to breathe it, and are warned not to stay. Their sound is the farm-bells hung on the wall at the end of every row, rung by whoever reaches them, and the songs the farmers sing at the plough, which are the loudest and least tuneful work-songs on the Table, sung deliberately off-key, because a song in tune is soothing. They trade in food, mostly sold up the hill to the city's commissaries at prices set by the Watch, and in flowers, grown in a few sheltered terraces for the Visiting Hour at Bedside.

**Custom: the Tether-Plough.** Steps farmers work in pairs, tied together at the waist by a rope of twenty feet, so that if one goes down in the furrow the other feels the tug. Marriage on the Steps is called *taking the rope*.

**Danger: the sun.** Plenty on the Steps is the most dangerous season anywhere in the Vigil, because of the sun: warm on the back, on a south slope, in the long afternoons, with the bees going. Steps farmers work Plenty in the cold hours only, which are few. The bodies the Dormitory carts take from the Steps in Plenty are brown and sun-dusted and have earth under their nails, and they are smiling more widely than anyone.

#### Hangwell

The staremoss-gatherers' village on the north cliffs of the Lid, below Blackmouth: some two thousand people living in a vertical town of rope, plank and iron pegs hammered into the cliff face, with platforms, ladders and swinging rope-bridges stacked a thousand feet up the rock. Staremoss grows only on these cliffs, in grey scabs on the wet north faces where the sun never comes, and only the cliff-folk know how to gather it. They live hanging. They work hanging. They have no flat floors in their homes, because they have no flat ground, and the Waking Laws in Hangwell are unnecessary: a gatherer who blinks on a rope falls.

Hangwell smells of wet stone, pepper (staremoss smells of pepper when cut) and the tar they rub on their ropes. Its sound is the wind, the creak of ropes, and the clatter of the rope-lifts that winch bales up to the Bitterhouse; and, when a gatherer falls, a long cry that the village answers from every platform at once, a call-and-answer that goes on until the body hits the scree and everyone falls silent. They trade in staremoss and nothing else, sold by the bale to the Bitterhouse at prices that have not risen in thirty years. The cliff-folk are the poorest people in the Vigil and the only ones who take their rouse raw, chewing the moss straight off the rock.

**Custom: the Answer.** When a gatherer falls, every platform cries out in turn until they reach the bottom, so that the falling one hears, all the way down, that they are being watched. It is the Vigil's most famous custom, and its saddest.

**Danger: the moss itself.** Staremoss is the source of rouse, and the cliff-folk who chew it raw are the most awake people on the Lid and the most deranged. Hangwell's Seeming is constant and shared; whole platforms see the same faces in the rock. The cliff-folk say that the cliffs are watching back, and that the moss is the god's eyelashes, growing.

[quick] Tansel Vetch, rope-master of Hangwell (thirty-one, a lean brown woman with rope-burned palms and three fingers on her left hand, all of whom she lost to the cliff in one fall that she survived; she has chewed raw moss since she was five, has never had a blink in her life, and sees, at every waking moment, a great grey eyelid in the rock face, a mile wide, which she says has lately begun to tremble) — Threat 3 · Flesh 13 · Guard 16 · Attack +5 (gathering-hook 3, Hooking) · Armor 0 · Dread 0

#### Eastlip

A hamlet of forty souls on the eastern rim of the plateau, perched on the cliff edge over the warm plain, where the Yawn first rises over the Lid. Eastlip is the Watch's weather-station. It keeps five great horns of brass, each eight feet long, mounted on the cliff-edge pointing back toward the city, and when the wind-watchers feel the Yawn coming up the cliff, smelling of hay and warm stone, they blow them, and the city's bell-towers take up the warning, and the Wakers double their patrols. The horn-blowers of Eastlip are the first Vigilants to feel the Yawn every year, and they feel it worst. The hamlet's population turns over every few years. The Watch pays well.

Eastlip smells, on most days, of cold rock and sheep. On Yawn days it smells of everything that the Vigil fears: sunlight, cut hay, a warm field, a summer afternoon a long way off. Its sound is the wind, and the horns, which are deafening at close range and which the watchers blow in turn, all through a Yawn, so that no one in Eastlip is ever more than a few breaths from a noise that hurts. It trades in warnings, nothing else, and is supplied by mule from the city.

**Custom: the Horn-Oath.** New watchers swear an oath at the horns on a dead watcher's name, *on Hilde, who blew till she fell*, and the dead of Eastlip are named, in order, on a brass plate bolted to the first horn. There are three hundred and ninety-one names. Every one of them went to the room with a horn at their lips.

**Danger: being first.** The Yawn reaches Eastlip a quarter-bell before it reaches anyone else. In that quarter-bell, the watchers must decide whether this is truly a Yawn, and blow; or a false alarm, and not. A watcher who blows for a false alarm is fined a season's pay. A watcher who does not blow for a true one is not there to fine.

[quick] Hornmaster Edda Larkspur (forty, lean, wind-cracked, with her ears stuffed with tarred wool and a scar across both lids where she once tried, at nineteen, to sew them open; she has blown for eleven Yawns in twenty years and has begun, this year, to feel the Yawn coming from the west, from behind her, from the city, which is not where the wind comes from) — Threat 3 · Flesh 12 · Guard 13 · Attack +4 (horn-staff 3) · Armor 1 · Dread 0

#### Clatterpit

The Vigil's tin and slate quarry, in a deep cut on the western shoulder of the Lid, two miles from the city wall along a mule-track. The tin of every roof in the city, the slate of every bell-sheet printer's slab, and the grit baked into every clatter-cake come from Clatterpit, where some three thousand quarrymen and their families live in terraces cut into the pit walls. The pit is loud. It is supposed to be. The miners work to the sound of their own picks, the crash of falling spoil, and a great iron gong at the pit-head beaten without stopping by a team of boys, in shifts, every breath of every watch. Nobody in Clatterpit has heard silence since the pit was opened in 344.

It smells of wet rock dust and smelt-smoke; the tin is smelted at the pit-head in charcoal furnaces, and the slag-heaps steam. Its sound is the gong, which can be heard in the city on a still day. Its people are deaf by thirty, as the bell-ringer Knell is, and like him they find the deafness no comfort at all: the soft inside a deaf head is as dangerous as any lullaby. They trade in tin sheet, slate, grit and lead, and they are paid by the cartload.

**Custom: the Gong-Watch.** In Clatterpit the gong-boys are the most important people in the village, and the pit's oldest saying is *the gong is your mother*. A miner who hears the gong stop drops everything and runs to the pit-head, and the whole village with him, because the only thing that stops the gong is a boy gone down.

**Danger: the Deep Cut.** At the bottom of the pit, in the lowest gallery, the miners struck a seam in 611 that was warm. They closed it. In 639 they struck it again, from another side. The rock in that gallery is pale, soft-grained, faintly downy to the touch, and it breathes, very slowly, four times to the minute.

[quick] Pit-Boss Grisel Hammond (fifty-five, broad, stone-deaf, missing an eye to a spoil-chip, communicates by slate and the flat of her hand; she sealed the Deep Cut herself both times; she has sent a sample of the pale rock to the Cutwrights' house on the Height and has received, by return, an offer to buy the whole gallery at a price that made her very afraid) — Threat 3 · Flesh 15 · Guard 12 · Attack +5 (pick 4) · Armor 1 · Dread 0

#### Crookheather

A shepherds' hamlet in the heather country below the border stones, a mile north of the Sleepers' Mile, inside the Vigil's borders by perhaps a hundred yards. Its sixty people graze sheep and cliff-goats on the heather and sell wool to the burr-cloth weavers up top, and their village sits so near the Mile that from Crookheather's one street you can see the nearest of the old refugees lying in the heather, breathing, moss-furred, heather growing through their ribs. Crookheather is a place where the Vigil's rule and the Rim's meet, and both are bent: the border stones are a hundred yards off, and Crookheather children have, every generation, run across them on a dare and lain down on the Rim side, and none of them have woken either.

The hamlet smells of sheep, peat-smoke and wet wool, and of the heather, which in Plenty is purple, humming with bees, and lethal. Its sound is the dogs, the sheep-bells, and the shepherd-pipes, which Crookheather plays in a shrill, wailing, deliberately ugly mode that carries for miles over the moor. It trades in wool, mutton, and guiding: Crookheather shepherds know every body on the Sleepers' Mile by sight, and for a lack will lead a traveller along the Mile to a particular sleeper, if the family has the name.

**Custom: the Shearing of the Mile.** Every Plate, by an old village vow nobody can explain, Crookheather's shepherds walk the Sleepers' Mile with shears and cut the heather back from the sleepers' faces, so that they can breathe. They do not touch the bodies otherwise. They do it without speaking.

**Danger: the Mile's attention.** Shepherds who shear the Mile say that lately, when they cut the heather from a sleeper's face, the sleeper's eyes are open. They were not open last Plate. They close again before the shepherds reach the next.

[quick] Old Hob Withy, shepherd of Crookheather (eighty, the oldest Vigilant outside the city, blind in one eye and brown as a nut; he has sheared the Mile for seventy years and knows four thousand sleepers by face; he says one of them, a woman near the old milestone, has been inching east, toward home, a hand's width a year, for as long as he can remember) — Threat 2 · Flesh 11 · Guard 11 · Attack +3 (crook 2, Hooking) · Armor 0 · Dread 0

[box: GM Advice — Using the Districts]
Every district of the Vigil is built around a single way of staying awake: the Height by staring, Ludmere by shouting, the Row by light, Bedside by visiting, the Slope by the Ring, Blackmouth by rouse, Needlegate by pain, the Glass Spur by turning its back. When the party moves between districts, let them feel the change in technique as much as the change in scenery. Then let them find the place in each district where the technique fails: a Height house where the shutters are closed, a Ludmere rouse-house where the slate says nothing, a Slope ring with a gap in it. That gap is where the scene is. The settlements beyond the city do the same work on a larger scale, and each one is a place where something has started to change: the Stoop's ten is getting longer, the Steps' sleepers smile wider, the Mile's eyes are opening, the Deep Cut is breathing. Nobody up top has been told.
[/box]

## Roads and Ways

Nobody in the Vigil travels for pleasure, because travel is sitting, and sitting is the first half of lying down. Within the city, people walk; the rich are carried *standing* in Vigil sedans, tall narrow boxes with a strap to hold and a hole for the face, borne by four porters who sing in rounds to keep each other awake. Goods move in barrows and on the rope-lifts. The Dormitory carts have right of way on every street, and when one passes, by law, everyone in the street stops and counts the bodies aloud. On the roofs, the Lamplighters' climbers have their own road, the *Mirrorway*, a network of tin catwalks, ladders and plank-bridges between the mirror-racks that crosses the city faster than any street, and climbers sell passage on it, illegally, at a crumb a roof.

Beyond the city the one true road is the Pilgrim Road, twenty-six miles from Lampwick Halt on the Rim to the Gate of Ludmere, climbing the whole way. Caravans take two days on it, with a night (as the Rim counts nights) at the Stoop, where nobody sleeps. Off the Pilgrim Road there are mule-tracks to Clatterpit, Eastlip and the Steps, rope-ways down to Hangwell, and the shepherds' paths in the heather, which wind around the sleepers of the Mile.

[box: The Two Days Up]
A foreigner climbing the Pilgrim Road should have slept at the Last Beds the night before. From the moment they pass the border stones they track Weariness, and the Road is two days of it: a long, steady, uphill monotony through heather and wind, which the GM may treat as one *scene of comfort* (+1 Weariness) for each half-day unless the party does something loud. Company caravans hire Stoop nudgers and sing. Those who do not are sometimes found at the roadside, upright, asleep, their mules grazing beside them, and are delivered to the Dormitory and billed to their employers. The Company's standard charge to the Watch for carrying one sleeper up the Road is twelve lacks.
[/box]

## Bread and Coin

The Vigil's economy is the economy of a fortress that buys its walls by the day. It imports everything that burns and most of what it eats; it exports rouse, mirrors, lenses, clockwork, tin, bells and the skills of its glass-workers, which are the best on the Table; and it spends a frightening share of every lack it earns on staying awake. The Rim Road Company estimates that two parts in five of every Vigilant's wage goes on rouse and nudging. The figure rises every season.

Wages are paid by the bell, in rouse-tabs on the Slope and in lacks elsewhere. The rouse-tab, a pressed black lozenge stamped with an open eye and worth three crumbs, is the only currency on the Table that literally loses value in the body: the Apothecary-General restamps it each season, and the eye on it gets smaller. Slope workers paid in tabs are paid in their own wakefulness, and everyone knows it.

What things cost on the Lid follows the core book's Rim Market, with these differences. Rouse runs about 3 lacks a day for a modest adult at the present dose. Lamp oil is double the Rim price and rising. Bread is cheap, gritty and dense, because the Watch subsidises it; a clatter-cake is two crumbs. Fatlands salt meat costs half again what it does at Sated. Soft goods are contraband, and a pillow on the black market fetches more than a horse. A Tucker's minute costs a month's wage, which on the Slope is about ninety lacks, and Old Nod will take it in tabs at the current stamp. Lodging means a strap in a waking-house at two lacks a day, with a nudger's share included.

**Taxes.** The Watch levies three. The *Light-Rate* is paid by every household by the window, so that the rich, who have the most windows, pay the most; it funds the Lamplighters, and the Slope evades it by having almost no windows. The *Wake-Tithe* is a tenth of every rouse sale, collected at the Bitterhouse door, and funds the Wakers. The *Bed-Due* is the cruellest: the family of every Counted sleeper owes the Dormitory a lack a season for the sheets, the slippers and the orderlies, for as long as the sleeper lies, which is for ever. Families on the Slope are paying Bed-Due for great-great-grandmothers they never met. A family that falls three seasons behind loses its visiting bell. A family that falls ten behind has its sleeper *transferred to the general rows*, a phrase nobody will explain, and since 639 a clerk of the Lessening has been quietly marking those beds.

**Who is rich.** The Eye-line houses of the Height, who own the staremoss leases and the lamp patents. The Bitterhouse's contractors, who buy moss from Hangwell at thirty-year-old prices and sell rouse at this season's. The Rim Road Company, which owns the oil convoys, the Last Beds and the Watch's debt. The Tuckers, whose King keeps his money in a place nobody has found. Jack Tenterhook. And, newly and secretly, the Lamplighters' Guild, whose oil accounts show imports that have not arrived, and whose inner members have lately bought houses on the Height.

## Law and the Knife

The core book gives the Law of the Lid: lying down is a crime, closing your eyes for longer than a breath is a crime, letting someone near you nod off is negligence. On the street, the law is enforced by Wakers on patrol, by Ward Sergeants who sit in judgement at each station for minor offences, and by the Standing Bench in Needlegate for anything worse. There are no sitting judges in the Vigil, because there is no sitting.

A trial at the Standing Bench is held on foot. The accused stands in an iron hoop at the centre of a round room; the magistrate, who is always lidless, stands on a raised step facing them; witnesses stand in a ring. The trial lasts as long as the accused can stay awake without rouse, which is held to be a fair test of their sincerity, and a Waker stands by with a needle to ensure that they do. Testimony is complicated by the blink. A witness may swear they saw a thing and be told that the Seeming record shows otherwise; the record is entered as evidence and is, by law, true. The lidless magistrate's perfect recall is the record of the trial, and no clerk writes anything down; when an appeal is made to the Watch, the magistrate is summoned to the Stair and simply recites the trial, word for word, every cough included.

The newest problem before the Bench is the blink-murder. The law has not decided what to call a killing done in a sleep of two breaths by someone who was not there. In 640 the Bench tried three such killers. One was hanged in the Lantern until his heart gave out; one was acquitted as *not present*, and was found in her own kitchen a week later, lying down, with her own hands crossed on her chest; and one, a Waker's widow, was sentenced to be kept awake by the state for the rest of her life, and is still standing in the Rattlehouse, and says *thank you* every time they needle her.

## The Faithful

Iss had no priests in the ordinary sense. Its old servants were the *walkers-softly*, whose whole religion was being quiet near a sleeping god, and after 342 their order was broken, their dream-books burned and their name made a slur. Faith in the Vigil today is mostly a matter of how one answers the old question about the god that did not wake.

**The Theft** is held in the Vigil as *the Smothering*. We killed a sleeper, the Smotherers say; the most defenceless thing on the Table, a god that could not even see us coming; and every soul in the Dormitory is the god's quiet answer, taking us back one at a time as gently as we took it. It is the Watch's own doctrine, preached in the old dream-halls of Ludmere as a reason for discipline: we must stay awake because we deserve to be punished in our sleep. Its shrines are the Penitents' Posts, iron pillars at street corners studded with spikes, against which the devout press their palms on the hour.

**The Gift** is called *the Lullaby*, and is the faith of most ordinary Vigilants. Iss let itself be eaten because it was too kind to wake, and the room is its kindness still: a place where the tired go to rest, where nothing hurts. It is the Lullaby's believers who stand at the foot of the beds in Bedside and tell their dead about the weather, who light lamps for them on the Row, who believe that the Counted can hear. Lullaby faith is tolerated, barely. It is very close to the Bedded, and the Wakers know it.

**The Bait** is the forbidden faith, and in the Vigil it has a terrible plausibility. The Bait-believers say that Iss starved Ludmere and lay still to be eaten because a god of sleep breeds by being dreamed: swallow it, and in three hundred years every one of you will be dreaming the same dream, and the dream will be the god. The Dormitory, they say, is a womb, the beds are its cells, and the number is a term of pregnancy. This doctrine is illegal on the Lid, and the Bedded preach a softer form of it, and the scholars of the Cutwrights' house on Regard Street have been heard to agree with it in private.

**Shrines.** Besides the Penitents' Posts, the Vigil has the bed-foot shrines of Bedside, where families tie ribbons and brass tags to the foot-rails; the lamp-posts of the Row, where the ashes of the dead burn; the Bonfires, which every district treats as an altar; and the Pillow, which is not a shrine and which nobody worships at, and at which every night someone is found kneeling at the fence.

**Rites.** The Coming-Heavy (a child's first yawn, met with the first pinch and the words *you will never rest*); the Pinching (the wedding, at the Bonfire, with the two-word vow); the Thanking (in Needlegate, and wherever a Waker fetches someone back); the Boarding (laying a corpse on a board in the street, so the neighbours may touch the closed eyes and envy them); the Lamp-Lighting (on the Row, from the ashes); and the Standing Watch, by which a family keeps vigil at the foot of a new sleeper's bed for one full day after they are Counted, in case.

**Holy days.** The core book gives the Night of Lying Down, the Shouting Day, the Pinching Fair and Tablenight. There are two smaller days. *Starers' Day*, in early Grace, celebrates the city's infants, who do not yet need to sleep; mothers carry their starers through the streets in open baskets and the crowds throw bells into them. And on *the Day of Nine Nights*, the anniversary of the meal, the old Ludmere families of the Height go in procession to the fence of the Pillow, barefoot, in felt, in silence, and stand there for a bell, and then go home; nobody else is permitted to watch, and the Watch does not explain what it is for.

## How Power Moves

The Vigil looks like an absolute state: one council, one Warden-Prime, one law. It is in fact a ring of people each holding something the others cannot do without, each watching the next, none able to look away. The Watch rules because it cannot sleep; the Wakers rule the street because they can hurt; the Matron holds the number; the Apothecary-General holds the dose; the Lamplighter-General holds the light; the Hourkeeper holds the time; and the King of the Tuckers holds the one thing everyone in the city wants and nobody can admit to buying. Every one of them is running out of something.

The lines of the table hide a second structure. Almost everyone on it owes the Rim Road Company, and the Company owes nobody on the Lid. Almost everyone on it fears the number, and only two of them know it. And almost everyone on it has, at some time, sent a servant down the Slope with a purse to Old Nod's cellar, because even the lidless have servants with eyelids, and even the lidless have once, as a child, wanted to know what it felt like. Old Nod's register of failures is the most dangerous document in the city that is not about the Dormitory, and the second most dangerous is his list of customers, which he keeps in his head.

When the party needs something done on the Lid, ask which of the ring holds it, and what they are running out of. Crane can give the party rouse, or refuse it, or give them something worse; he wants time. Ashlock can light a street, or darken one; he wants oil. Pyre can lift a sentence or order a needling; he wants someone to tell him he did right. The Matron can open a row; she wants someone to tell her she did wrong. Sexton can strike a bell early or late, which on the Lid is a weapon no one has noticed; she wants a witness. Old Nod can hide anyone; he wants a better hook. And the Warden-Prime can do anything, and will remember everything, and wants the city to outlive her by any means, including the party.

## The People of the Vigil

> Never trust a rested face.

The cast of the Vigil are, almost all of them, people holding something up. The Warden holds the city; the Matron holds the number; the Captain holds the street; the Apothecary holds the dose; the Lamplighter holds the light; the Hourkeeper holds the hour; the King of the Tuckers holds the one minute everyone wants. #### Who They Are

She is a small woman, narrow-shouldered, straight as a lamp-post, in grey galled silk wired so finely that it whispers when she breathes. Her face is lined everywhere except around the eyes, which have no lids to crease: there is only a raw pink rim where lids should be, and inside it two enormous pale grey eyes, red-veined, always wet, never still. Every half minute one of her two eye-wetters raises a sable brush from a silver bowl of rosewater warmed to blood heat and strokes it across her eyes, left then right, and she does not flinch, because she has never been able to. The rosewater runs down her cheeks in two steady tracks, so that the Warden-Prime of the Vigil appears, at every moment of her life, to be weeping. She smells of roses and of something underneath, sweetish and stale, like water left too long in a vase.

Her voice is soft, precise and slightly hoarse. She speaks in complete sentences and never asks a question twice. She does not gesture. When she listens, she does not nod; she watches the speaker's mouth, and the speaker becomes aware, within a sentence or two, that every word is going somewhere it will never leave. Watchers who have served under her for twenty years still cannot speak to her without touching the pin in their collar.

#### Their Story

Ilvane Stroud was born in 583 A.G., in the Stroud house on Regard Street, staring. The midwife slapped her to make her look away from the lamp, and she could not. Her first memory is the slap. Her second is her mother's face above the cradle, and she can still see every pore of it. At five she stood at a window on the Height through the Grey Ninth of 588 and watched the carts go down to the Slope, and she remembers each of the eleven hundred, or at least each one she saw.

She was raised as all lidless heirs are, to watch: to stand on the Stair from the age of ten, to recite debates verbatim, to read a face the way a clerk reads a ledger. She was elected to the Watch at nineteen, to the Lid's Eye committees at twenty-four, and to the Warden-Prime's landing in 612, at twenty-nine, the youngest in a century. She married her cousin Florian Wakely in 610, a lidless Watcher of gentle temper; their daughter Oriel was born in 618, with eyelids. Florian died of a rouse-heart in 626, on the Stair, mid-sentence. She can still hear the sentence. It was about drains.

In 622 she stood at the deathbed of Matron Hesketh Anstey, as the law required, while the dying Matron whispered the number to her successor, Cecily Dorm. Ilvane was not meant to hear it. She did not. She *read* it, on the old woman's lips, from across the room, and has carried it since. Nobody knows.

#### Their Place in the Land

She stands on the Lid's Eye landing, halfway up the Nodding Stair, and the whole Watch of Watches passes before her every quarter hour as it climbs. She does not climb; she is the fixed point the council turns around. Every motion is spoken in her sight, and she remembers every one. She appoints the Offices: the Matron, the Apothecary-General, the Lamplighter-General, the Hourkeeper, the Captain of the Wakers, the Registrar of the Seeming. She signs every sentence to the Lantern. She holds the Rim Road Company's loans in her own name, because the Watch could not be trusted to remember the terms.

Her power is limited by three things. The Watch elects and could, in theory, depose her, though it has not deposed a Warden in two hundred years. The Company owns the oil. And she cannot be everywhere at once, so she must rely on reports, which she knows to be lies about one time in four. She governs by remembering more than anyone else and letting them know it.

#### What They Carry

- **A lash of Iss** — a black hair a yard long, stiff as wire, coiled in a phial of green glass on a chain at her throat. One of three left on the Table. She knows where the other two are: one in the reliquary of the Tether Hall, and one in Old Nod's cellar, where she has let it stay because she knows he has never used it. Taint 4 Regrowth (Iss); see the Rim Market.
- **The Lidless Seal** — a ring of black bone carved with an open eye, which makes any document the Warden-Prime's will. She seals few. She prefers to be remembered.
- **A silver bowl and two sable brushes** — carried by her eye-wetters, Prudence Hale and Ambrose Startle, who have served her for nineteen years and whom she suspects, once or twice a season, of being someone else.
- **A blank commonplace book** — bound in grey calf, given her on her election. She has never written in it. She carries it so that people will believe she needs to.
- **A child's milk tooth** — Oriel's, the first she lost, kept in a locket. Ilvane does not know why she kept it. She remembers the day exactly. Oriel cried, and blinked through the tears, and Ilvane looked at the floor for the first and only time in her life.

#### Wants, Fears and Secrets

She wants the Vigil to outlive her, by any means, and she has decided what that means. The Deadwatch is her policy: if the Regent of Sallowreach will sell the secret of Keeping, the city will be killed and kept upright, and will never need to sleep again. She has done the arithmetic, which Envoy Dray thinks she has not. If the Vigil moves north to die, the sixty thousand Counted will be left on the Lid, unwatched, in the dark. She has decided that is acceptable.

She fears her daughter, and this is the secret she has told no one. In 631, when the first restless sleeper was logged and struck from the register, Ilvane obtained the Matron's private notes and compared them, minute by minute, with her own memory of Oriel's movements. The restless sleepers turn their heads, all together, toward the Height. They do it when Oriel closes her eyes.

**The secret she does not know:** the number she read on Hesketh Anstey's lips was not the number Anstey whispered. The dying woman's lips shaped one thing; her breath, which only Cecily Dorm heard, said another. The Warden-Prime has spent nineteen years doing arithmetic on the wrong figure, and the GM decides which of them is right.

#### Ties

- **Lady Oriel Stroud** — her daughter, whom she loves with a terror she has never once allowed onto her face, and whom she cannot bear to see blink.
- **Matron Cecily Dorm** — her appointee and the only other keeper of the number; Ilvane trusts her completely and watches her constantly.
- **Envoy Castellan Dray** — her ambassador in Lastgate; she knows from the exact phrasing of his letters that he is keeping something from her.
- **Dr. Aldous Crane** — she ordered the Last Cake. She remembers the order word for word, and so does he.
- **Captain Brannoch Pyre** — the one officer who has never lied to her face. She knows Merrit lay down. She has not said so.
- **Deputy-Watcher Sabine Larch** — the Warden knows about the lists. She has not stopped them. She is waiting to see if the arithmetic is right.
- **Lady Seraphine Dalgety** — a cousin on her mother's side, and a member of the Second Table; Ilvane suspects it and has her watched.

#### In Play

PCs meet the Warden-Prime on the Lid's Eye, summoned to report, accuse or be accused. She wants of them what she wants of everyone: the truth, which she will get, and their usefulness, which she will spend. Foreigners interest her, because foreigners can sleep outside the Vigil and might carry messages, poisons, or the secret of Keeping, and because nobody knows whether an Unfed of the Fast would go to the room on the Lid.

- **The Recitation.** The Warden-Prime summons the party to the Stair and recites, word for word, a private conversation they had in a Ludmere rouse-house a week ago, which she was not present for. Someone has a lidless witness among them. Who?
- **The Second Letter.** She asks the party to carry a sealed letter to Envoy Dray in Lastgate, with orders not to open it. It says: *Tell them yes. Tell me nothing more.*
- **The Seen Blink.** On the Stair, in front of three hundred Watchers, the Warden-Prime's eyes go still and far away for two breaths. The lidless do not blink. Only the party saw it. She knows they did.

To threat she answers with memory: she recites the threatener's whole life back to them. To kindness she is puzzled and polite, and she remembers it, and will repay it at a time of her choosing. To the god she gives nothing at all; she simply watches it, and it has never been able to make her look away. If nobody intervenes, she signs the Deadwatch, leads the Vigil north to the Drop, and is killed and Kept in Lastgate, lidless for ever, remembering everything, in a fen where nothing ends.

#### Who They Are

She is small and soft-voiced, with dark hair worn loose because no one has ever thought to dress it, and ordinary hazel eyes under ordinary lids, which in her family is a deformity. In company she holds her eyes open until they stream, and the tears run down her face in two lines like her mother's rosewater, so that the two of them, side by side, look like mourners. Her skin is very pale; she has not been outdoors in eleven years. She smells of the lavender water her nudgers wash her rooms with, and of linen. She moves carefully and quietly, like someone who has learned that noise brings visitors. When she smiles it is quick and shy and gone; when she is alone and closes her eyes, she smiles slowly, and that smile is the most frightening thing on the Height.

#### Their Story

Oriel was born in 618 to the Warden-Prime and her consort Florian Wakely, both lidless, and she came out of her mother blinking. The midwife dropped her. Her birth was entered in the Cutwrights' ledger with a note, *lids present, cause unknown*, and the rumour on the Height that she was some eye-wetter's bastard has never died, though the Warden-Prime remembers her own fidelity perfectly and has never troubled to deny it.

She was raised behind locked doors at the top of the Nodding Stair, in a suite of white rooms lit by mirrors from every side, attended by deaf nudgers so that no one would hear her ask to sleep. Her father visited and read to her until his death in 626; her mother visited once a day for the count of a hundred and watched her not blink. Oriel was educated by tutors who were not told her name. She learned embroidery, Ludmere history, the Watch's procedure, and the art of keeping very still while someone looked at her.

She was eleven when she first closed her eyes on purpose for longer than a breath, alone, behind a curtain. She was twelve when she first saw what was behind them.

#### Their Place in the Land

She has none, officially. The Watch knows she exists and has agreed not to know it. She is the Warden-Prime's only heir, and the Lidless Succession forbids her to inherit, so the Stroud line passes, on Ilvane's death, to a lidless cousin, Corvin Stroud-Larch, to whom Oriel has been promised in a marriage that would make their children his. Several houses on the Height would pay a great deal to have her quietly removed. Several others would pay more to have her in their hands, because the Second Table and the Lessening have both heard the rumour of what happens in the Dormitory when she closes her eyes.

Her rooms are guarded by two Wakers who believe they are guarding a madwoman. Her nudgers are deaf and devoted. She knows every guard's step on the stairs and every eye-wetter's voice through the floor, and she has a key to the roof that no one knows she has.

#### What They Carry

- **An embroidery frame** — a long white sampler she has worked on for nine years: the long room, stitch by stitch, from the far end, with the backs of sixty thousand heads in grey thread. She has nearly finished the last row.
- **A stolen down feather** — a single grey goose-down feather, found caught in a Rim Road Company crate in the Stair's storeroom. Possessing it is a crime. She strokes her cheek with it when she cannot bear the light.
- **Letters never sent** — sixty-one letters to her mother, folded small and hidden in the hem of her gown. Most of them begin *I am sorry*.

- **A hand-mirror with its glass turned inward** — she had a glazier reset it the wrong way round, so that she can hold a mirror and not see her own eyes.

She wants to be allowed to be ordinary. She wants to close her eyes in front of someone and have them not look frightened. She wants her mother to look at her once without that face. She fears the marriage to Corvin, who has told her pleasantly that he will have her lids removed by a Seamer on their wedding night, *for the children's sake*.

She sees the long room from its far end, through a great grey lid, and every sleeper in it, standing in their silent ranks, turning their heads to look at her. She has never told anyone, because it is the only time in her life that anyone has looked at her with love.

**The secret she does not know:** when she closes her eyes, the restless sleepers in the Dormitory turn their faces toward the Height. Her mother knows. The Matron has noted it without knowing the cause. Evander Quell of the Second Table has guessed. Oriel's lids, the College's oldest walkers-softly would have said, are not a failure of the Course. #### Who They Are

A stooped little woman in a brown work-apron over a grey gown, with a jeweller's loupe screwed into her left eye socket so long that the skin has grown a pale ring around it. Her pockets clink: she carries a fistful of brass escapements, the small toothed wheels of clocks, and turns them over in her fingers like prayer beads, click, click, click, in time with whatever is ticking nearest. Her hands are perfectly steady. Her voice is dry, quiet, and unhurried, the voice of a woman who has spent forty years in a tower full of ticking and has had to speak under it. She smells of clock-oil and brass polish. She does not look at people much; she looks at their wrists, where the pulse is.

#### Their Story

Maud was born in 581 in Ludmere Old Town, the daughter of a bell-founder, and apprenticed at twelve to the Pendulum Hall, where she has been ever since. She became Hourkeeper in 618. For nineteen years the work was routine: setting the bells, adjusting the two hundred and forty clocks, logging the pendulum's swing. In 629 she noticed that the great pendulum was half a breath behind the sand-glass at the end of a day. She re-hung it. In 630 it was a breath behind. In 640, after eleven years of measuring by pendulum, sand, candle, water-clock and the pulse of her own wrist, she submitted her report to the Watch: every hour is a little longer than the last, now by two breaths a watch. The report was suppressed within the bell. She has written it again, smaller, in the margins of the bell-schedules that every tower in the city receives.

#### Their Place in the Land

She sets the time. Every bell in the city, from the Ludmere tower to the Glass House, is struck to her signal, relayed from the Pendulum Hall by flag and lamp. The Wakers' shifts, the Rattlehouse sentences, the Dormitory's visiting bells, the Bitterhouse's batches and the rouse-tab's stamped value all depend on her hours. If she struck the bells early, the city would take its rouse early; if late, the Slope would go down in its thousands. Nobody has noticed this power, least of all the Watch, which treats her as a clerk.

She depends on the deaf ringer Thaddeus Knell to carry her bells, and on the Watch to fund the Hall. She has no allies on the Stair. Her apprentices are three bored girls from Ludmere, one of whom reports to the Registrar.

#### Their Story

Born in 591 in Bedside to an orderly's family, Cecily carried sheets at eight, washed the Counted at twelve, and was a Matron's deputy at twenty-six. She answers to the Warden-Prime, who appointed her, and to a Watch oversight committee on which Sabine Larch sits.

- **A pillowslip** — from the fourth bed. She has never washed it.

She wants someone to tell her what she has done. She fears the arithmetic and she fears her hands.

**Her secret:** she smothered four of the Counted. Each time, within the bell, someone somewhere in the city blinked and did not come back, and the number was exactly what it had been. The count keeps itself. She has started to wonder whether she was the one who chose who went down.

**What she does not know:** the Warden-Prime read the number on Anstey's lips in 622. But Anstey's lips and Anstey's breath did not say the same thing, and only Cecily heard the breath.

Red-faced, barrel-chested, bald and browless, burn-scarred to both elbows in shiny pink sheets, he has a cheerful bellow that fills a room and that he has cultivated to hide that he cannot rest, ever, because he can count.
