# Plates of the Fast and the Rim Road

These seven plates cover the middle of the Table and the ring of road that goes around it. Every other land's plates were painted in the colours of rot, salt, smoke or meat. The Fast's plates are painted in **warm candle gold**, the only kind light anywhere in the series. That warmth is where the horror is. Every artist on this set must keep one rule in mind: in the Fast, nothing hurts you. The god Orrum, the Host, has never once been unkind. The fields are combed, the hedges laid, the cloths spotless, the lamps lit. The people are starving in the cold beside a feast because they will not say yes to it, and the ones who did say yes are still sitting at table, happy, centuries later. Paint the welcome so well that the viewer wants to step into it, and then put a thin, cold, upright figure in the frame, refusing.

The Rim Road plates take the Fast's gold to its edge, where it lies against the ordinary grey of the world like the light from a door left open. Keep the gold on the inner side, the side that faces the middle, and keep the outer road bone-grey and wet.

Two cautions. First, the Host is never painted whole. Show its kindnesses: a napkin, a pulled-out chair, a warm print on stone. Second, and stricter: there were eight chairs at the first table and only seven peoples, and nobody counts the chairs. Each plate holds one small thing that is a seat too many, a side too clean, a shadow for nobody. Never draw who it is for.

## HALLOWBOARD
[plate: hallowboard]
[pull] Seven sides worn, one side clean. Don't ask me whose. | — Reft Lammas, Roll-Keeper of Barrowgate
**Plate:** HALLOWBOARD · *The eight-sided table at the centre of the world* · The Fast · warm candle gold
#### The Shot
A long lens from low on the slope of the chalk hill, just above the Barrows, looking up and across at the summit so that the stone table sits on the horizon like an altar on a step. Kneeling height, the first grey-gold minute after dawn on the morning after Tablenight. In the left third, the foreground is the Barrows: long, low, grassed mounds with coarse pale grass that hangs off them like uncombed hair, and a line of the Abstinent walking down between them away from the hill in single file, roped belt to belt, their backs to the stone. They are small dark strokes, leaning forward with exhaustion. In the centre third, Hallowboard: a forty-foot slab of grey granite with eight sides, waist-high, polished to a dull gleam, set on the round bare crown of the hill. The worn chalk trench circles seven of its sides like a pale moat. The eighth side faces the camera at a slight angle, and the chalk in front of it is smooth, white and unmarked, the only clean ground in the picture. In the right third, a single figure stands close to the stone with one hand raised above its surface, not touching: **Reft Lammas**, Roll-Keeper of Barrowgate, checking for the handprints. Behind him an empty ash carrying-chair waits on the slope, its poles resting on the chalk. The background is the heartland seen from above, a gold haze of combed fields and ruled orchards, with thin straight threads of chimney smoke rising from empty farmhouses all the way to the horizon. The lower centre band, where the title sits, is the dark shadowed flank of the hill below the stone, a calm field of blue-grey chalk in shade.
#### Light and Colour
The sun comes from low and slightly too far west, which is wrong for dawn, and the Fast's light is always wrong like that. It washes the top of the hill and the upper face of the stone in **warm candle gold**, rich and soft as butter at the edge of a flame. The gold also lies on the seven warm handprints on the slab, which glow faintly, like the inside of an oven, and on the thin far chimney threads. Everything else is cold: the roped walkers are blue-grey and black, the Barrows are near-monochrome ash and bone, and Reft's coat is the colour of wet slate. The clean eighth side gets no gold at all. It is lit flat white, the light from nowhere, as though a lamp were held there that nobody can see.
#### Texture and Flesh
The chalk trench is the record of the Refusal: pressed into its hard pale floor, layered and overlapping, are the faint shapes of bare feet and knees, thousands deep, like fossil prints on a riverbed. After last night's rain a few small things have washed up out of it, and along the trench lip lie teeth, brown and grey, worn flat at the crowns, some still with a dark root. The granite is smooth enough to hold a reflection, with no moss, no lichen, no birdlime, and the handprints on it are not stains but warmth, a faint steam lifting off each one into the cold air, as from a loaf just set down. Reft's hand is the hand of an Unfed: long, knuckled, the skin thin and yellowish over the tendons, the nails ridged. His lips are parted and his tongue is held flat to keep the smell out, the Fast's always-there smell of new bread and brown butter. On the Barrows the pale grass is matted where something large lay down among the mounds overnight, and the shape it left is a person curled on one side, arms around something.
#### The Story in the Frame
Hallowboard is the centre of the world. Six hundred and forty-one years ago the Unfed stood here around their god's table for nine days and nights and said *no, thank you*, forty thousand of them, while six other peoples ate their gods in the same black season. The ones who died standing are under the Barrows. The ones who lived walked east and built a town with no hearths. The god, refused, stood up, ducked its head under every lintel on the Stoopway, and has been walking the empty heartland ever since, laying tables, mending them when they are broken, and waiting.
Every Tablenight the Abstinent climb the hill and stand around the stone with their backs to it through the longest night of the year. The empty ash chair on the slope is Elder Forbear Wend's; his bearers have already carried him down, because he could not stay to see the stone in daylight. They walk home roped together, silent, the way people leave a sickroom.
Reft Lammas stays. He is the Roll-Keeper of Barrowgate and it is his duty, every year, to count the handprints. He has counted seven every year of his life. A large hand, palm down, beside each worn side, like a host resting a hand beside a guest's plate to say *there, that's yours*. He is counting now, lips moving. He has reached seven. He is looking at the eighth side, where there is never a print, and where the chalk has never been trodden, and where the one carved panel on the stone has had its figure smoothed away by a thumb, many times, a long time ago. He does not know why nobody stands there. Nobody does. When he is asked, he will say what they all say, puzzled, that it is someone else's place. In a moment he will write *seven* in the roll, close the book, and go down the hill faster than he came up.
#### Hidden Details
- The smoothed carving on the eighth side, if looked at closely, still shows the faint outline of a chair back, and no one sitting in it.
- Among the teeth on the trench lip is one milk tooth, very small, set apart from the others on a folded white napkin; the Host has tidied it.
- Reft's roll book, open under his arm, has a column for each of the seven sides and an eighth column that has been ruled and left blank on every page.
- One rope among the walkers has an extra loop tied in it at the end of the file, a belt-knot with no belt in it.
- The ash chair's seat holds a faint warm hollow, though Wend was lifted out of it an hour ago.

## WANTING
[plate: wanting]
[pull] Carry your own. Count your chairs. Close the gate, and then go through it again. | — what a Wanting mother says at the door each morning
**Plate:** WANTING · *The town with no hearths* · The Fast · warm candle gold
#### The Shot
Street level, a 35mm lens at a child's height, in the blue dark before dawn in Lack, looking down a straight cobbled street in Pinners' Yard toward Pantry Close. The street is a corridor of long, low, grey stone houses roofed in slate and frosted white, and there is not one chimney on any roof, so the skyline is a row of flat, cold, unbroken ridges. In the left third, in the foreground, a heavy iron-strapped door hangs open outward, and on its threshold stands **Meagre Toft**, Measure-Bearer, fifteen, with a ration-cloth bundled under one arm and her palm pressed flat against the door from outside, in the middle of the ritual of closing it, opening it, going back in and coming out again. Behind her, through the gap, her mother stands against a bare inside wall in every garment she owns. In the centre third, the street runs away from us to the squat round Pantry tower, five storeys of dressed stone, windowless below the fourth floor, one lamp burning at its iron door, and in front of it the Ration Line already formed, four abreast, a dark mass of hunched wool and white breath. In the right third, doors all down the street are open at the same moment, and in each a thin figure performs its own family's way of closing, touching, returning. At the top of the right edge, sitting on a stone at the head of a rising street, is blind Old Pinch Corve, listening to everyone breathe. Far in the background, past the last roofs to the west, the sky is not dark: it is a low band of gold, the edge of the Fast. The lower centre band is the frost-black, empty cobbles of the street.
#### Light and Colour
The town is in pre-dawn blue-black, lit by the frost itself and one lamp. The cold is the main colour: bone, slate, iron, breath. The only warm thing inside the town is the small pale amber of the Pantry lamp, and it is not gold, it is weaker, a thin yellow. The real **warm candle gold** lies outside, on the western horizon over the roofs, a soft rich band where it is always an early Carving afternoon. It reaches into the town only as a faint gilt edge on the west-facing sides of the frosted ridges, like light on a knife.
#### Texture and Flesh
Frost grows on the inside of Meagre's shutter in fern shapes, and there is a clear print of her palm in it, because frost inside means the god was not near in the night. Her hand on the door is red-knuckled and chapped, the skin over her wrist bones shiny and tight, the nails bitten. Her face is young and already shows the skull: hollow at the temple, the hinges of the jaw visible under the skin, lips cracked white in the cold. The Line's breath hangs over the Close in a low fog, and the faint sweet pear-drop tang of hunger breath is in it. The walls of the houses are rough grey stone, and where each house once had a hearth there is a square of newer brick in the wall, mortared shut, scrubbed and cold. The cobbles are worn into a ring around the Pantry by centuries of slow feet, and no path crosses the Close. The doors have deep scuffs at the handle height of every member of every family, worn by the closing rituals of six hundred years.
#### The Story in the Frame
Wanting is the only real town of the Unfed, nine thousand people on a cold limestone ridge on the eastern edge of the Fast, half a day from the Rim Road. It was built in 188 A.G. to one idea: that nothing in it should look like an invitation. There are no inns, no bakeries, no kitchens, no benches. Doors open outward so that no one inside can be said to have let anyone in. Cooking is done in the Cold Yards at the edge of town, on bare earth under the open sky, and the fires are put out the moment the food is warm. There is nowhere to sit in the whole town except the floor of your own house.
Meagre Toft is the girl from the ledger of ordinary days. She woke in the dark because the cold woke her, checked the frost, touched the bricked hearth, said the Five Rules into the blanket. Her mother said *carry your own*, and Meagre said *I carry my own*. Now she is closing the door from the outside, and in a moment she will open it, go back in, close it from inside, and come out again, because that is how her family does it, and all the families have a way, and all of them are about the door. She will walk the long way around the Close, never across it, and stand in the Line, and carry thirty-one cloth bundles out to Thinwick and Sallow Hatch and the Brindle farms. Last week there were thirty-three.
The feeling at ground level is quiet and slow. People walk carefully to save their strength. Nobody says good morning. A Rim visitor would call it the most miserable town on the Table. The Unfed would agree, and take it as praise.
#### Hidden Details
- One roof in the row, far down the street, has a single thin thread of smoke rising from it, from a house that has no chimney.
- On Meagre's windowsill, under the frost, is a small dark shape: a crust left out by a child, and beside it, a ripe plum that nobody in Wanting could have.
- Old Pinch Corve's head is turned, not toward the street, but west, toward the gold, as if he hears someone breathing there.
- The scuff marks on the Toft door count four family members at four heights; there are three people in the house.
- The Ration Line is four abreast, and in one rank there are three people and a gap kept exactly the width of a fourth.

## THE LONG TABLE
[plate: longtable]
[pull] We kept you a place. We always keep you a place. It's further along now. You'll find it. | — a chorus of the Seated, eighth mile
**Plate:** THE LONG TABLE · *Nine miles of the Seated, eating nothing in good cheer* · The Fast · warm candle gold
#### The Shot
A wide lens from the far verge of the valley road, at standing height, looking diagonally across the road and down the length of the table so that it runs from the left edge into a vanishing point in the right third and never ends. The valley sides are beech-hung hills, the leaves on the turn and never falling. In the left third, in the foreground on the verge, the road between her and the cloth, stands **Sparing Lowe**, widow of Pinners' Yard, in her visiting coat with wax in her nostrils, hands folded, talking across the road to her son. In the centre third, the table: pale unidentifiable wood, a spotless white cloth unbroken into the distance, lamps lit at intervals in broad daylight, porcelain and silver, and the Seated on both sides in every stage of wasting. Her son sits directly across from her, about forty, his coat hanging off a frame, smiling courteously, calling her *madam*. Two chairs down sits **Brother Hallow Crust**, a draped coat with a head, a silver fork raised to his mouth with nothing on it. The right third is the long perspective of the table shrinking toward the valley's head, the Seated becoming smaller, paler, thinner, until the farthest are only coats holding a posture. Somewhere along it, small, clearly, one chair is pulled out from the table and empty. The background is the head of the valley in gold haze. The lower centre band is the grey flint road surface between Sparing and the table, kept dark and calm, a strip of no-man's-land.
#### Light and Colour
The light is the Fast's unending early-Carving afternoon: low, amber, coming from slightly too far west, laying long soft shadows of chair legs across the road. **Warm candle gold** lives on the table: in the lamp flames, glinting on rims of porcelain and silver, glowing through the steam over dishes, and warming the cloth to the colour of fresh cream. The Seated are lit by it kindly, the way portraits are lit. Sparing, on the verge, stands in the cool shade of a beech, desaturated, blue-grey, out of the warmth. Her side of the road has no gold at all.
#### Texture and Flesh
The Seated are clean. That is the first wrongness: collars pressed, what hair remains combed, no filth anywhere. Her son's cheeks have fallen to the teeth, the lips drawn back, skin the colour of weak tea stretched so tight over the breastbone that you can see the slow knock of the heart through it. His nails have grown thick and yellow as horn, curling round the handle of his fork like roots round a stone. Brother Hallow Crust's eyes are sunk into blue pits but wet, because a good host does not let a guest's eyes dry. Further down, a woman's grey hair has grown four feet long and down into the chair and through the weave of the cloth like a darn, binding her to the table. A loose tooth sits on a plate beside a fork. The lips of the oldest are split and white inside like bark. Steam rises off golden dishes: crackling, new peas, a pie crimped by a thumb. The air should look faintly thick, a shimmer of roast and of a thin sharp chemical sweetness like windfall apples.
#### The Story in the Frame
The Long Table runs nine miles down a valley road in the old heart of the Fast. It began, the Hostwatch think, as one table at a crossroads in the second century, and it has grown with every guest. About eleven thousand people sit at it, the newly sat flushed and chattering and eating with both hands, the oldest near the valley's head almost weightless, a smile hung between two cheekbones. They are all happy. They are eating nothing. Their murmur is all praise. *Oh, this is lovely. Have you tried the. Pass the.*
On the first day of each season a few hundred Unfed walk the road from Wax Halt and stand on the verge to visit their Seated, because the road is not the table and the verge is not a chair. Sparing Lowe has visited her son on the sixth mile every season for thirty-one years. He has not known her for twenty. He is telling her, just now, that his mother used to make a dish like this one, and that he must remember to send for her. She is about to thank him, and say she is sure his mother would come if she could, and walk home.
Brother Hallow Crust, forty years at table, a Tablebreaker who sat down on purpose to see if he could get up, has turned his head toward Sparing and is about to tell her about the syllabub, with nutmeg grated over. He remembers every dish he has been served since 601 and cannot remember his wife's name. A Crumb-Runner's cart is passing far off down the road, at a trot, eyes on the ground, ears stopped with wax, because the Seated call everyone by name.
#### Hidden Details
- The empty pulled-out chair in the distance sits a little further down the table than a pencilled mark on the milestone at the verge, where someone noted it last season.
- Sparing's own chair is laid opposite her: her name card faces the road, in her son's childhood handwriting.
- Brother Hallow Crust's sleeve is torn at the elbow where it was caught on an axe-haft, long ago, the only Tablebreaker mark left on him.
- Under the cloth near the far distance, two pairs of hands are clasped, and those two Seated are the only ones not looking toward the road.
- The table's lamps, followed into the distance, are lit in pairs, but every so often there is a single lamp lit for one more place than there are chairs.

## THE EMPTY CHAIR
[plate: emptychair]
[pull] Every morning the plate is clean. Every night I lay it again. I don't call that worship. I call it manners. | — Sober Osmond, Verger of the Empty Chair
**Plate:** THE EMPTY CHAIR · *The last temple of the Host* · The Fast · warm candle gold
#### The Shot
Interior, a wide lens on a low tripod height, at the foot of the great hall, looking up its whole length to the head of the table. The temple is a long low hall of honey-coloured stone with a slate roof carried on dark beams, built for a parish to eat together in. Down the centre runs a single great table the length of the hall, bare boards, dust-free, with hundreds of chairs along both sides all pushed in square. The left third is the long row of pushed-in chairs on one side, the stone wall above them carved with old bands of wheat and loaves, and deep tall windows letting in late gold light in long slanting shafts. The centre third is the far head of the table, where one place is laid on a white cloth folded over only that end: plate, cup, knife, fork, a loaf, a candle, and the chair at the head pulled out a hand's width. Standing beside it, bent to straighten the knife, is **Sober Osmond**, the Verger, old and thin, in a plain dark gown with a linen apron. The right third is the other row of chairs and, against the wall, an alcove where the Host's figure once stood in carved stone, now worn into an indistinct seated shape by touch, with a shelf of cracked old Host-bells. The background, behind the head of the table, is a great arched window full of the gold heartland: combed fields and smoke threads. The lower centre band is the near end of the long bare table, empty boards and dark floor, quiet.
#### Light and Colour
The light is evening and enters from the west windows in long slanting shafts, **warm candle gold** in its purest form: honeyed on the stone, catching motes, laying bars across the dark table. The single candle at the head has just been lit and its flame is the brightest gold point in the frame. The laid place glows softly, as if lit from within. The rest of the hall, the far ends of the shafts, the pushed-in chairs, Osmond's gown, fall into deep honey-brown and then black. Osmond's face is warm on one side and grey on the other.
#### Texture and Flesh
The honey stone is soft and worn smooth at shoulder height along every wall, where generations of a parish leaned at meals before the Refusal. The table boards are bare, with deep old knife-scores and a long worn trough down the middle from centuries of plates set down and lifted. The one plate is cream porcelain with a gold rim, and it is spotless, polished, as clean as it is every morning. The loaf is real Pantry oatcake on the plate's edge, grey and coarse and small, Osmond's own measure: he lays his own bread for the god and goes without. His hands are bone and liver-spotted skin, the joints swollen, the fingertips split from cold. His cheeks have fallen in; his neck is a cord of tendons. The alcove figure has been rubbed by palms until its face is gone and its lap is a smooth hollow dark with skin oils. Mouse droppings and dust are everywhere except on the table, which no dust will lie on.
#### The Story in the Frame
In the first century the Unfed pulled down every temple of Orrum in the Fast. This one, on a wooded rise six miles west of Wanting, was left, and nobody now remembers why. It was built to feed a whole parish at one table every feast day, and it is the last place on the Table where the Host was ever worshipped as a host is worshipped, with food.
It is kept by one man, Sober Osmond. Every night, against all law, he lays one place at the head of the table and lights one candle, and every morning he finds the plate clean. He does not know whether the god eats it. He does not think it matters. The Abstinent have condemned him eleven times. They have never sent anyone to stop him. Pilgrims and the curious walk out from Wanting to look, a quarter-day there, and stand at the door, and do not come in, and Osmond is always kind to them, which makes them leave faster.
The moment captured is the laying. Osmond has set down the plate and cup, lit the candle, and is straightening the knife so that it lies parallel to the table's edge, which is how the god likes it, he says, though he cannot say how he knows. He will go and sit on the floor by the door, on the cold stone, not on any chair. He will sleep there. In the morning he will come back up the hall and find the plate clean and the knife exactly where he set it, and the chair at the head pulled out a finger's width further than he left it. He will push it in. He will begin again.
The feeling of the place is hushed and warm and sad, like a family home after a death with the table still laid. It is the only room in the Fast where the Unfed's god is spoken to directly, and Osmond speaks to it the way you speak to someone old and lonely at the end of a long day.
#### Hidden Details
- On the floor by the door is Osmond's bedroll and his own empty bowl, turned upside down, and someone has laid a folded napkin on top of it.
- The pushed-in chairs, counted down one side, are one more than down the other, and the extra chair is set back a little from the table, as for someone who was never expected to eat.
- One of the cracked Host-bells on the alcove shelf has been wrapped in wool, as Hostwatch trackers do now.
- The worn alcove figure's lap holds a small fresh crumb.
- In the great west window, among the far smoke threads, one rises from the rise itself, from this temple's own roof, which has no hearth lit.

## THE PANTRY
[plate: pantry]
[pull] Four months of grain. Six months of winter. You can pray about that or you can do the arithmetic. I have done the arithmetic. | — Mother Stint Avery, Keeper of the Pantry
**Plate:** THE PANTRY · *Four months of grain for six months of winter* · The Fast · warm candle gold
#### The Shot
Interior of the weighing-hall on the ground floor of the Pantry tower, a medium-wide lens at chest height across the scales. The hall is a round windowless vault of dressed stone with eight-foot walls, sacks banked to the ceiling. The left third is the iron hatch in the north wall, its shutter open on the blue dark of Pantry Close, and through it a sliver of the Ration Line: hands reaching, chits held out, faces cut by the iron frame. Beside the hatch a young clerk, **Hesitate Brune**, Night Lister, stands at a lectern copying names, his left shoe unlaced. The centre third is **Mother Stint Avery** at the great brass scales, older than Wanting, a cloth bundle balanced in one pan against iron weights in the other, her sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms white with oat-dust, square and hard and still, saying a name aloud. The right third is the store itself, receding into dark: grain sacks stacked in courses, salt-fish casks, round cheeses on slatted shelves, and along the curving wall the ledgers, hundreds of spines back to 41 A.G. Pinned on a wall peg above the ledgers hangs a long stitched cloth, the letter to the Keeper from Avery Steading, unopened, its lines of stitching visible. Most shelves at the far end are already bare. The lower centre band is the stone floor in front of the scales, swept clean, in shadow, with only a few spilled oats.
#### Light and Colour
Inside it is warmer than anywhere else in Wanting, and it is not warm. The light is one hooded lamp hung over the scales, a dim yellow, and the frosty blue of the Close through the hatch. **Warm candle gold** appears only in the brass: the scale pans and beam catch the lamp and burn soft gold, the one rich thing in the room. A thin gold sheen also lies on the oats spilled on the floor. All else is dust-brown, sacking-beige, iron-black, and the cold blue of the hatch.
#### Texture and Flesh
The air is thick with dust motes and the room smells, visibly, of sacking and mouse and faint oats. Sacks sag and split at their seams, mended with coarse thread; there are mouse-holes chewed and plugged with wire wool. Stint Avery's forearms are a stone-mason's, ridged with old tendon, her fingers ink-stained black at the tips, nails clipped to the quick. Her face is square, lined, colourless, the skin dry and flaking at the hairline, eyes with no expression at all. The hands at the hatch are the Fast's hands: blue at the nails, knuckle bones sharp, one with two fingers bound in a rag where they broke. Hesitate Brune is gaunt and young, his cheekbones like shelves, a pencil behind his ear. The ledgers' leather is cracked; the pages that show are columns of names, many struck through with a single straight line. The stitched cloth from the steading is grey wool with many hands' worth of uneven stitching, some lines in thread, some in what looks like hair.
#### The Story in the Frame
The Pantry is the best-guarded building in the Fast and its only true seat of government. A squat round tower of dressed stone in the middle of Wanting, five storeys, no windows below the fourth floor, one iron door that opens outward with three locks held by three different people. The Unfed grow nothing. Every mouthful they eat is bought from the Rim and carried in by Crumb-Runners, and stored here, and weighed out every morning by name, and recorded, back to 41 A.G.
