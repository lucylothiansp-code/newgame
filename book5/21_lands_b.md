# Plates of the Vigil, Cradlewrack and Oathen

These fifteen plates cover the three lands where the god is still at work in the body: the Vigil, which cannot close its eyes; Cradlewrack, which cannot stop opening; and Oathen, which cannot unsay anything. Each land gets five headers. The first shows the country or its capital at full width, and the other four go down, district by district, toward the place where the rule is hardest to bear.
All three lands share one problem for the painter, which is restraint. The Vigil's horror is comfort, so its plates must look warm and dangerous at once: lamp amber smeared into a brown smoke ceiling, and the soft grey of a quiet afternoon. Cradlewrack's horror is birth, and its plates keep the camera on the adults, the way the Attendance does. We see the midwife's back, the apprentice holding the door, a seam, a stake gone from the rim, and we never see an infant's body. Oathen's horror is the word, and its plates are silent: felt, gold, ochre stone and dried blood, and mouths that have learned what a sentence costs.
Every plate has a named person in it, because these lands are made of people still trying to be kind inside an impossible rule. Every plate leaves the lower centre band dark and calm for the title. And every plate holds back one small wrongness about a table, a chair, a door or a guest, for the sharp-eyed. Paint it and do not explain it.
Accent colours: **the Vigil**, lamp amber and smoke; **Cradlewrack**, wet iron red and clay; **Oathen**, canyon ochre and dried-blood brown. One accent per plate, and everything else is near-monochrome.

## THE VIGIL
[plate: vigilcity]
[pull] Nobody born in the Vigil has ever seen a star. I have never wanted to see one. | — a mirror-climber of the Lamplighters' Guild, Second Rank
**Plate:** THE VIGIL · *The city that has not slept in three centuries* · the Vigil · lamp amber and smoke
#### The Shot
We are on the tin roof of a house on Cinder Row, high on the Slope, at the Seventh Bell. The lens is a wide 24mm held at a kneeling eye-height, so the roof's ridge runs in from the lower left like a road. The **left third** is Wenna Startle, mirror-climber of the Lamplighters' Guild, Second Rank. She kneels on the loose overlapping tin with her rag-bag and polish-pot, rubbing the greasy skin of smoke off a mirror as tall as she is. Her own face looks back from the cleaned half: hollow-eyed, mouth slack, a pin held crosswise in her teeth. The **centre third** is the city, ten thousand public lamps and a hundred thousand more in windows, falling away down the Slope and rising again in terraces of mirrors toward the Height. On the far summit, small and exact, stands the grey hollow tower of the Nodding Stair. Between the two, as a dark oval, lies the Pillow, ringed with fence-lamps on poles like a bruise circled in pins. The **right third** is the Lid's edge: the plateau stops in a sheer grey cliff, and below it is soft black heather country with the thin pale thread of the Pilgrim Road. On it lies a faint line of shapes in the grass: the Sleepers' Mile. Over everything hangs the smoke ceiling, a low brown lid lit from beneath. Foreground: wet tin and a bell-rope. Midground: the city. Background: the cliff. The lower centre band is a dark stretch of roofs below the Row, where the mirrors are unpolished, and it stays quiet for the title.
#### Light and Colour
There is no darkness anywhere, and that is the wrongness. Every light source is amber: Bonfires, street-lamps, windows and mirrors, all throwing light back up into the smoke. The smoke ceiling glows the dull orange of a scab, and the brightest lamp in the whole picture is the reflection in Wenna's mirror. No shadow falls anywhere in the city, because light comes from every direction. Shadows begin only past the cliff edge, where the Rim country sinks into true blue-black: the only night in the frame, and it belongs to the Sleepers. The palette is smoke-brown, soot-black and tin-grey, with **lamp amber** as the single accent, pooled in thousands of points and gathered most strongly on Wenna's face.
#### Texture and Flesh
The tin is pitted, buckled and sweating condensation, rivets bleeding rust. The mirror's unpolished half carries a skin of soot thick enough to take a fingerprint, and Wenna's rag has smeared it into tobacco-coloured streaks. Her knuckles are split from lye and cold. The back of her hand shows a constellation of old pin-marks, small red-brown punctures in rows, some healed white and some fresh and beaded. Her eyes are the Vigil's eyes. The whites have yellowed and threaded with red, the lower lids sag like wet paper, and a crust of salt sits at the inner corners where tears have dried without anyone noticing. On the inside of her left wrist, in lamp-black, her name is written. Her breath shows faintly. The smoke is laid in with a dry brush, layered and greasy like old cooking fat in a kitchen. Everything in the frame looks unrested. Even the tin looks tired.
#### The Story in the Frame
It is a working bell like any other. Wenna's brother Florian has the house shift, and she has the mirrors: forty-one big and two hundred small on this stretch of Cinder Row. If the smoke is not taken off them every hour, the light in the lane goes amber and soft, and amber and soft puts people down. So she polishes until she sees her own face, and then she polishes that off too. Behind her the six-bell Shouting has just ended, every shutter on the Row thrown open to shout at nothing for a count of thirty. Below her the Vigil goes about its one long day, which began in 341 and has not ended. Four hundred thousand people stand, pinch, rouse and nudge one another under the lamps, and sixty thousand lie down the hill in the Dormitory and breathe in time. This plate tells the player what the land is: a whole city kept awake by statute, by drug, by pain and by each other, never dark, never resting, drowning a little deeper each year while standing up. Nobody born here has seen a star. Wenna's grandmother told her they were holes in a lid, with the room shining through behind them. In a moment Wenna will finish this mirror and move to the next. Three houses down, something tall in a grey coat is walking across a roof. She did not see it, because she is not one of the thirty-one who did, so it did not happen.
#### Hidden Details
- On a roof three houses to the right stands a tall thin figure in a long grey coat, rendered faintly, as if half-erased. It is a Seeming the city has entered as news: seen by thirty-one, and not by Wenna.
- In the smoke ceiling directly above the Pillow there is a single tiny pinprick of pale light, like a star or a hole in a lid. No one in the Vigil would know what to call it.
- In the mirror's polished half, behind Wenna's reflection, there is an extra lamp that is not in the real city behind her. It sits low, at the height of a bed.
- A chalk tally on the bell-post reads *41 · 200 · Florian*, the mirror-count and the next shift.
- Along the Sleepers' Mile the shapes in the heather lie in a row with heads all turned the same way, toward the Lid.
#### Painter's Prompt
Dark painterly wide panorama, 3:1, of a vast never-dark city on a grey stone plateau, seen from a rusted tin rooftop. A gaunt young woman in a soot-stained smock kneels at left polishing a tall mirror. Her exhausted red-rimmed eyes are reflected in the cleaned half, a pin held in her teeth, old pin-scars on her hands. Below her spreads a sea of thousands of amber lamps and iron bonfire-furnaces, terraces of roof-mirrors, and a grey spiral tower on the far summit. A fenced dark hollow at the centre is ringed by lamps on poles. At right the plateau ends in a sheer cliff above black heather and a faint row of sleeping bodies along a road. Overhead, a low smoke ceiling glows scab-orange. Desaturated smoke-brown and tin-grey, the only accent lamp amber. Oil and ink, dry-brush smoke, heavy black at the cliff, a calm dark lower centre.
**Negative:** stars, moon, clear sky, bright saturated colours, blue daylight, cartoon, anime, clean digital gloss, modern streetlights, electric lights, cars, glass skyscrapers, text artefacts, watermark, smiling faces, cosy warmth, beds visible, lens flare

## THE DORMITORY
[plate: dormitory]
[pull] Received this bell from the Slope carts: eleven. Washed, sheeted, numbered, laid. Breathing in time by the half-bell. Smiling: all. | — the Receiving Gate day-book, Grey Ninth of Carving 588
**Plate:** THE DORMITORY · *Sixty thousand beds, breathing in time* · the Vigil · lamp amber and smoke
#### The Shot
We stand in the central hall of the Dormitory, the largest roofed space on the Table, with a long 85mm lens at the height of a standing orderly's eye. The aisle runs straight away from us and the rows of beds compress into a single endless pale field. The ceiling is low, almost oppressive, held up on squat grey pillars. In the **left third**, close to the lens, sits Bed One, which has a brass rail where every other bed has iron, and in it lies Aveline Morne: a plump fair baker's daughter, nineteen for three hundred years, flour still in the creases of her knuckles, lips parted. In the **centre third**, a little way down the aisle and lit from the side, walks Matron Cecily Dorm. She is broad and soft, in a starched grey gown and felt slippers, and one hand holds the heavy ring of bed-keys at her belt so it will not jingle. The other hand hangs at her side holding something pale and folded that might be linen and might be a pillow. Her lips are moving. She is counting. The **right third** is the rows going on: narrow iron beds, white sheets, black numbers on the foot-rails climbing into five figures, lamp after lamp on the pillars receding into a brown haze of distance until the beds become texture. The lower centre band is the dim aisle floor of scrubbed boards between two beds, dark and empty, for the title.
#### Light and Colour
There are lamps on every pillar, oil-lamps with smoked glass chimneys, and they all flicker together, dimming slightly on each long inhalation of the halls as the air is drawn past them. The light is warm, low and even, the colour of milk with a drop of tea in it, and it is the most frightening light in the series because it is *restful*. The white sheets catch it and go faintly gold. **Lamp amber** is the accent: the flames, the brass rail of Bed One, and a reflected glint on the Matron's keys. Everything else is linen-white, felt-grey and the brown smoke haze under the ceiling. Paint no hard shadows. Everything is soft-edged, as if seen through tired eyes.
#### Texture and Flesh
The Counted look perfect. That is the wrongness. Skin smooth and faintly flushed, not sweating, not ageing, no dust settling on it. Aveline's cheeks are pink. Her hair spreads across the pillow in a fan that has not moved in three centuries, and the pillowcase beneath it has a faint hollow worn into it. Under her parted lips the tongue is just visible, and the lips themselves are shaping a word, caught mid-form: the beginning of a name. The sheets are starched, changed every Plate though they never need it, folded at the corners in hard military creases. The air is visibly thick, a faint warm haze that moves in long slow drifts toward the doors and back, and the Matron's grey gown sways with it. Her hands are soft and chapped, her nails bitten to the quick. Implied smell: clean linen, and the sweetish skin-smell of bodies that do not sweat.
#### The Story in the Frame
The Dormitory was begun in 342 as a single converted granary and has been extended wing by wing for three hundred years. It holds sixty thousand beds, and every one but fewer than two thousand is full. By law every sleeper in the Vigil is collected within a bell, washed, numbered and laid here, where they breathe and do not wake. They breathe in time: sixty thousand chests rising and falling together, four breaths to the minute, so that standing in this hall is like standing inside the lung of an enormous animal. Matron Cecily Dorm is the only living person who knows the number at which the eye in the dream will open. She walks the rows every day counting from one under her breath. She loves the Counted, and knows their faces the way a mother knows her children's. Sometimes she hates them, and tells no one. She has been seen standing over a bed with a pillow for a long time. What she carries in her hand in this plate is left uncertain on purpose. Just before this moment, Bed One spoke. For three hundred years Aveline Morne made no sound, and last month her lips began to shape words. Most of them are names, one a day, and each person named has gone to the room the following day. The Matron has started writing them down. In a moment she will reach the brass rail, take out her pencil and lean in to listen. This week Aveline said a sentence instead of a name. It was about a door, and someone who has not been fed.
#### Hidden Details
- The Matron's lips, if magnified, are forming a number past fifty-eight thousand. She has not lost count.
- One of the beds in the right third holds a sleeper who looks exactly like a woman standing at its foot in visiting clothes. This is *going to look for your bed*: one of the Already-Counted has found herself.
- A slip of grey card is tucked under Bed One's rail, with the Matron's handwriting on it. It is a list of names, and the last line is longer than the others.
- Down the far aisle, one bed among thousands stands empty with its sheet turned back, and at its foot sits a single extra chair, pulled out a little, as though someone were expected.
- The lamp-flames all lean the same way, toward the doors. They are caught on the inhalation.
#### Painter's Prompt
Dark painterly wide interior, 3:1, of an endless low-ceilinged hall of narrow iron beds in perfect rows, white-sheeted, black numbers painted on the foot-rails, receding into brown haze. Close at left is a plump fair young woman asleep in a brass-railed bed, flushed and perfect, flour in her knuckle-creases, lips parted mid-word. At centre a broad matron in a starched grey gown and felt slippers walks the aisle, holding her ring of keys still, lips counting, something pale and folded in her other hand. Smoked oil-lamps on squat pillars all flicker in unison, the air a warm visible haze. The light is soft, milky and restful, which is the horror. Desaturated linen-white and felt-grey, the only accent lamp amber on flames and brass. Oil-paint brushwork, soft edges, deep shadow at the far end, a calm dark lower centre band.

## THE NODDING STAIR
[plate: noddingstair]
[pull] She looked at me for a count of eleven. One more than the Regard. I have been waiting thirty years for her to mention it. | — a Watcher of the third shift
**Plate:** THE NODDING STAIR · *A council that may never sit* · the Vigil · lamp amber and smoke
#### The Shot
We are inside the hollow grey tower at the summit of the Height, looking across the shaft from the inner rail with a 35mm lens at the height of the Lid's Eye landing, halfway up. The staircase, broad as a road, spirals around the inside of the tower wall, so that it crosses the frame in great diagonal bands rising left to right, again and again, above and below us, until it vanishes into lamplit haze at top and bottom. In the **centre third**, on the landing, stands Warden-Prime Ilvane Stroud, the only figure in the frame who is standing still. She is fifty-eight and lidless, in a high-collared grey gown, small and very straight. Her enormous pale grey eyes are red-rimmed and webbed with veins, and they look directly into the lens. At each shoulder an eye-wetter in felt holds a sable brush, and one brush is a finger's breadth from her eye, mid-stroke, glistening with rosewater. In the **left third**, the council climbs past her: Watchers of the Eye-lines, lidless, robed in grey, bodies bent forward on the uneven steps, faces wet-eyed and grimacing, some leaning on each other. One has a hand raised for *aye*. In the **right third**, the stair rises out of view, and on the wall beside the landing an old scratched inscription can just be made out. Far below is a small iron-barred door. Foreground: a worn step edge slanting outward, and a dropped pin. The lower centre band is the dark curve of the stair's underside below the landing, which the title rests against.
#### Light and Colour
Lamps in iron brackets are bolted to the inner wall at every turn, smoked and guttering, throwing a ladder of amber pools up the spiral. Between the pools the stone is grey-blue and cold. The strongest light falls on Stroud from a lamp directly above her, a hard top-light that makes her eyes huge and wet and leaves the eye-wetters half in shadow. **Lamp amber** is the accent, in the bracket-lamps and in the rosewater glint on the brush. Everything else is tower-grey, robe-grey and soot. A faint warm haze rises from the barred door far below, the only patch of real darkness in the frame.
#### Texture and Flesh
Stroud's eyes are the image. They have no lids at all: the orbit rims are smooth, slightly shiny scar where lids should be, and the eyeball sits too prominent, glossed with rosewater, the whites a map of burst capillaries. Tear-salt has crusted in a fine white line down each cheek and been brushed away so often that the skin there is raw. The sable brush is wet, its hairs parted. Her hands are folded and perfectly still, with a single silver hatpin through the cuff. The climbing Watchers are worse: lidless too, eyes clouded at the edges, one with the cornea cracked like glazed pottery, the blind white of a Giver. Their robes are dark with sweat at the spine. The stair's treads are worn into hollows by two hundred years of feet, each one a different height, so the lines of the steps never quite agree. On the wall the scratched letters have been gouged by a fingernail or a pin and blackened by the lamp.
#### The Story in the Frame
The Watch of Watches convened here in 441 and has not adjourned. Some three hundred Eye-line nobles climb the Nodding Stair in three shifts, day and night, and debate on the move. Motions are carried by those still breathing hard enough to say *aye*. Benedikt Lour built the steps so that no one could sit, lean or stand comfortably on them, and the top step returns by a hidden ramp to the bottom, so the council climbs forever and never arrives. Only the Warden-Prime stands still. Ilvane Stroud has not shut her eyes once since birth, because she has nothing to shut them with, and she forgets nothing she has seen: every execution she has signed, every lie told to her on this stair, and the exact weight of each. Every quarter hour the whole council passes before her, and she counts them, and has never stopped counting. This is the moment the player arrives on the landing. Stroud will remember them perfectly, forever. Behind her an old lord is falling behind, grey-faced, and someone will have to pin him. Far below, behind the barred door at the foot of the stair, is the only dark room on the Lid. In it lies the Coverlet, the casket where the two hundred and fourteen lids of the Giving were laid in 503 and became a single folded sheet of soft grey skin with a closed seam down its middle, breathing four times to the minute. Every new Warden-Prime goes down once, alone, to look. Stroud went down in 612 and came up saying one thing twice: *It has got bigger.* She knows the number. The Matron does not know she knows.
#### Hidden Details
- The scratched inscription on the landing wall reads, faintly, *I gave my lids for the city and the city gave me nothing to close*. It is Ottoline Quell's, from 503.
- The barred door at the base of the stair has a draught-stain around its edges, a soft grey bloom on the stone that rises and fades, as if something behind the door were breathing.
- High at the top of the tower, a single lit window holds a small dark-haired woman with ordinary hazel eyes, closed. It is Lady Oriel Stroud, alone, blinking for the joy of it.
- One Watcher in the left third has stopped climbing and stands with his eyes on the Warden. He is the one held in her Regard for a count of eleven.
#### Painter's Prompt
Dark painterly wide interior, 3:1, of a vast hollow grey stone tower with a broad uneven staircase spiralling endlessly up and down its walls. Grey-robed nobles climb it in a bent, exhausted procession, their eyes lidless, wet and clouded. At centre, on a landing, stands a small straight woman in a high grey collar who has no eyelids. Her huge pale bloodshot eyes stare straight out, glossed with rosewater, and two felt-clad attendants at her shoulders hold wet sable brushes to her eyes. Iron bracket-lamps form a spiral ladder of amber light, with cold grey stone between. Far below is a small barred iron door with a soft grey stain breathing around it. Desaturated slate and soot, the only accent lamp amber. Oil-paint, gritty texture, intimate eye horror, a calm dark lower centre band.

## THE SLOPE
[plate: slope]
[pull] Thimble Lane, two children down, a grandmother and an aunt. Lanternside, nine. Gutter Lane, the whole of number four. That's this afternoon. Pass it on. | — the Slope's cart-count, shouted from roof to roof on a grey day
**Plate:** THE SLOPE · *Where whole families go down in an afternoon* · the Vigil · lamp amber and smoke
#### The Shot
A grey afternoon in Carving on Thimble Lane, deep in the Slope. The camera is a 28mm at the height of a crouching woman, looking up the lane, which is so steep it is nearly a stair. Tin-and-plank houses are stacked so that one family's floor is the next one's roof. In the **left third**, through an open doorway, we see into a single room at the lane's edge. A household has gone down. A grandfather lies sideways on the floor by the hackle, a woman sits slumped against the twisting-post, and a younger man has fallen forward over the table, cheek on his folded arms, all of them breathing, all of them smiling. In the doorway stands Mag Startle, ring-mother of Cinder Row: forty-six, broad, a widow with a voice heard three lanes off. She has a pin-glove on her right hand and her mouth is open in a shout. She has just slapped the man at the table and he has not come back. In the **centre third** the lane climbs, and halfway up it a Dormitory cart with rattling wooden wheels is being pushed down by two Wakers in long coats with needles at their belts. In the **right third** the Slope tumbles down toward the bottom of the frame, and on the roofs nudger-children stand with their hands cupped to their mouths, shouting the count from roof to roof. Above everything, the sky is a flat milk-grey lid. The lower centre band is the dark mouth of a drain-gutter running down the lane's centre, unlit and calm, for the title.
#### Light and Colour
The danger here is the light itself: even, grey, windless and shadowless, the colour of milk in a bowl. It flattens everything, and it is the colour of sleep. Against it, the Slope's lamps are few and poor, burning bad oil with a smoky orange flame. One lamp is lit inside the doorway behind Mag, and its weak **lamp amber** pools on the faces of the smiling sleepers, and that is the accent: thin and failing. Smoke from a dung-fed Bonfire lies across the upper lane in a brown smear. The palette is otherwise milk-grey, tin-grey and rope-brown.
#### Texture and Flesh
The tin is dented, rust-blooded and dripping. Rope hangs everywhere, hemp and tarred, from lines between the houses, frayed into hairs. The sleepers' faces are the horror: slack, softened, the lines of a lifetime of waking smoothed away, mouths curved into small contented smiles. The grandfather's cheek is flattened against the floorboards and his eyes, peeled open by Mag a moment ago, are rolled back and wet. On the back of the younger man's hand are fresh pinpricks in a line, and one welling bead of red. Mag's own face is the opposite: grey, sweat-shined, the eyes standing out, the lips bitten raw. The pin-glove is cracked leather with nine bent steel points. On the inside of every wrist in the room, lamp-black names. On the table, a cup of rouse gone cold, with chalk-grit settled in the bottom, the cut stuff. Implied smell: wet tin, cheap oil, chalk and ash, and this year a faint smell of the sea.
#### The Story in the Frame
The Slope is forty thousand households in a hundred lanes too steep for the Wakers' carts, a city of rope, wick, laundry and rag under its own dung-fed Bonfires, where the rouse is cut with chalk and ash and the nudgers are mostly children. Slope families nudge in a ring, everyone watched and watching, the youngest set to watch the oldest, and nobody may be unwatched in their own home. The ring holds on most days. It does not hold on a grey afternoon. When the wind drops and the cloud closes and the light goes all one colour, whole families go down one after another, and the last one awake moves between the bodies slapping and screaming until they go quiet too. The Weather-Board at the Ludmere Bonfire has been chalked *GREY AFTERNOON* since the ninth bell. This is that afternoon. Mag Startle keeps the ring for the whole of Cinder Row, not just her own house, and she knows by the sound of the shouting which household is weakest. This one went quiet and she came running. She has got the grandmother back, and the grandmother is upstairs, being slapped by a child. The other three are gone, smiling, breathing in time with sixty thousand others down the hill. Next the cart will reach the door and the Wakers will try the needle at all points, and then they will lift them. Mag will stand in the lane and count them onto the cart, because that is what the Slope does: it comes out to watch, and to count. Then she will go home and look again at two of her grandchildren, who she has begun to think are very careful copies.
#### Hidden Details
- One of the children shouting on the roofs in the right third has the same face, stance and cap as another child two roofs over. These are Mag's grandchildren, or her Copies of them.
- The Waker on the cart's left side has a figure *41* chalked on his coat-sleeve: the afternoon's count so far.
- The man at the table wears a padded hand-ward box strapped to his forearm. It is locked, and the key hangs around the grandmother's neck upstairs.
- In the lane above, a woman stands motionless at an upstairs window pinching her own wrist in a slow rhythm. She is *at the window*, and she will lose this whole long watch.
- Painted over the door is the Slope's skipping-rhyme, half scrubbed out: *six for the door that lets the number in*.
#### Painter's Prompt
Dark painterly wide street scene, 3:1, of a steep slum lane of tin-and-plank houses stacked on each other, ropes strung everywhere, under a flat windless milk-grey sky. At left, through a doorway, a family lies asleep where they fell: an old man on the floor, a woman against a post, a man slumped over a table, all slack and smiling, eyes rolled back. A broad exhausted woman in a cracked leather pin-glove stands in the doorway shouting. Up the lane, two long-coated men push a rattling wooden cart downhill. On the roofs, small figures cup their hands to shout. A single weak lamp inside gives a failing amber glow on the sleepers' faces. Desaturated milk-grey, tin and rope-brown, the only accent thin lamp amber. Oil and ink, scratchy dry-brush texture, a calm dark gutter in the lower centre.

## THE GLASS HOUSE
[plate: glasshouse]
[pull] I'm sorry. I'm sorry. Please don't look at my mouth. I'm sorry. | — Lukas Marre, through the speaking-tube, to every visitor, every time
**Plate:** THE GLASS HOUSE · *Quarantine for those whose yawns are catching* · the Vigil · lamp amber and smoke
#### The Shot
We are on the Glass Spur, the bare finger of rock at the windward edge of the Lid, inside the long greenhouse. The camera is a 50mm at a seated eye-height, looking along the corridor that runs the length of the building between two rows of cells walled in thick green glass and iron frames. In the **right third**, close to the lens, is a cell. Behind its pane sits Lukas Marre, the Yawning Man: thirty-one, thin, bookish, in a patched grey coat, his lower face held in a stiff leather muzzle that clamps his jaw half shut. His eyes are lowered from the glass in apology and a book lies open on his knee. A brass speaking-tube runs from his cell through the glass and ends at a funnel mouth on the corridor side. In the **centre third**, on the corridor side, an old woman visitor sits on a stool with her mouth to the funnel, her eyes turned firmly away from his face as the rules require. Beside her stands Keeper Ottilie Grain: fifty, gruff, wax in her ears, a weighted cosh at her belt and her other hand raised, ready to slap the visitor if she begins to yawn. In the **left third**, the corridor recedes past cell after green-lit cell, each with a hunched masked figure inside, to a far door where the wind beats on the glass. Foreground is the brass funnel and the worn stool-rung. Midground is the visitor and Ottilie. Background is the receding cells. The lower centre band is the dark worn flagstone floor of the corridor, unlit between lamps, held calm for the title.
#### Light and Colour
Each cell is lit from outside by its own lamp, hung on the corridor side, so the light passes through green glass and the prisoners sit in a drowned, bottle-coloured glow. The corridor is lit from these cell-lamps and from the Glass Crew's lamps, burned in sacred quantities, harsh and too many. **Lamp amber** is the accent, in the corridor lamps and in the warm edge they throw on the visitor's cheek. The cell interiors are green-grey, a colour that sits against the amber and must be kept desaturated and murky, like old bottle glass. Outside, beyond the glass roof, is a cold grey gale and the brown smoke lid.
#### Texture and Flesh
The glass is old, thick and wavy, full of seeds and bubbles, with stains of condensation running down in long beads. Through it Lukas is slightly distorted, like something in a jar. The muzzle is saddle-leather, dark and stiff, buckled behind his head with three straps. It has rubbed the skin at the corners of his mouth into raw red sores that have crusted and split many times, and a faint glistening of spit stains the leather below the lips. His jaw muscles are visibly clenched against it. Above the muzzle his face is gentle and hollow, the eyes wet and red. His fingers are inked from writing. Ottilie Grain's face is weathered, wire-lidded, the wax in her ears yellow. Her eyes are dry, very dry, and wide. The brass tube is green with verdigris at its joints and polished bright at the funnel where mouths have pressed to it. The visitor's hand grips the stool, knuckles white.
#### The Story in the Frame
In the Vigil a yawn is contagious, and in a few people it is catching beyond all reason. Lukas Marre is the worst of them. One gape of his jaw and a room goes down, a street sways, and once, at nineteen, before he knew, a whole market square went down. Everything else is linen-white, lavender-grey and the deep brown-black of the old beams.
#### Texture and Flesh
Sabeth's hands are the centre of the texture: the skin of the knuckles split and weeping, a thick callus on the inside of the ring finger where the hook of the knife rests, the nails scrubbed down to pink. The pumice is grey and gritty, the brush bristles splayed, the copper of the basin scoured bright on the inside and green on the outside. The Brink woman's nightgown has been cut open at the breastbone. The crowning's lips are soft, rose-brown and moist, its edges perfectly round, with a fine thread of a failed stitch dissolving at one side. The pale curve within is smooth and wet, like the crown of a skull, and the parted seam shows a grey-blue eye glistening. The answered Opinions' forearms are pale and puckered from water, the fingertips wrinkled white. The wooden door behind Wenna has a faint bulge in its planks, as if it were being pressed from the other side. Implied smell: lavender, lye and iron.
#### The Story in the Frame
The Lying-In is a ring of red walls forty feet high with no gate, entered by ladder and basket hoist. Here the Attendance brings the births nobody speaks about: the ones the Second Opinion spared and should not have, the ones it could not reach, the mothers who will not stop bearing, and the Brink-touched, in whom the flesh has opened into a crowning through which something presses toward the light at every pang. Nothing has ever come all the way out. Its upper wards are clean and quiet and smell of lavender. Its lower wards are bricked, cell by cell, and the bricklayers are paid triple and change every year. The Lying-In keeps its own book, and nobody outside the Attendance has read it. Sabeth Thorne was for eighteen years the Opinion the other Opinions sent for. Last winter, in Tarnbrow, she counted eleven breaths, and the thing lived, and she has not reported it. Tonight she has been called to the bed by the east window, where the night Opinion has entered the patient as *the same as last month* and knows she is not. Sabeth has done nothing yet. She has opened the case and closed it again, and now she is scrubbing her hands, which is what every Opinion does, until the skin breaks. The woman in the bed has turned her face to the wall, which the Book says is to be permitted. Wenna holds the door, as apprentices are taught, because the door opened on its own when they came in and will not stay shut. In a moment the voice in the crowning will ask for water, and Sabeth will have to turn round.
#### Hidden Details
- The Lying-In's own book lies open on a lectern. The last entry reads *the same as last month*, and below it someone has written *it is not*.
- Down the ward, forty-four chairs line the windows, a few of them empty with basins still steaming.
- On the floor by the Brink bed lies a small bone door the size of a hand, hinged and closed.
- One of the answered Opinions is looking not at her basin but at Sabeth's back, and is counting on her wet fingers.
- Through the window, low on the ring-wall, a fresh course of bricks shows paler than the rest.
- The rush-lamp's smoke drifts toward the crowning, not toward the window.
