# Plates of Sallowreach, the Fatlands and Brinehollow

These fifteen plates cover the first three lands of the Long Grace: the grey north, where nothing ends; the warm south, where everything is meat; and the western coast, where the sea has moved into the people and is leaving the land. Each is a place, not a portrait, and that changes the brief. A god-plate is a face. A land-plate is a life, and the painter's job is to make the viewer feel they could step through the gold frame and be handed something to carry.
So every plate here is built around work. Somebody in each image is doing a job they will do again tomorrow: tarring a post, reading to the shelved, lowering a sack, stamping a barrel, bailing an old man over a knee. The horror is never the spectacle. It is the routine, held at ground level, by a named person with tired hands. Keep the figures small against the place but give them faces you could pick out in a crowd, and let the place press in on them.
The three lands must be told apart at thumbnail size. Sallowreach is close, vertical and wet, all pilings, ladders and shelving, in tea-stain sepia with bruise-green rot and fly-black. The Fatlands are low, wide and warm, ramps and mounds and furrows, in tallow yellow with raw meat red. Brinehollow is long and horizontal, piers and flats and cliff, in drowned teal and salt-white, and every one of its plates should lean slightly west.
As always, the title sits in the lower centre band, so keep that band dark and calm: black water, shadowed turf, wet plank. And in every plate there is at least one thing that has been scraped off, chiselled flat or counted wrong. Don't explain it. Just paint it.

## LASTGATE
[plate: lastgate]
[pull] Ninth, Weir Row, the Coombe house, is where you'll find Gran. Sixth, Old Weir Row, is where you'll find her mother. | — directions given to a Seamer's Threader, Needle Street
**Plate:** LASTGATE · *The city on pilings where nothing ends* · Sallowreach · bruise-green and fly-black on tea-stain sepia
#### The Shot
Low and close to the water, from a punt drifting in the canal off the Landing at sixth bell, with a 35mm lens held a yard above the black surface so the city stacks up over us like a cliff. The 3:1 frame is all verticals. In the left third, close enough to touch, stands the Ferrying Post, the oldest piling in Lastgate, black as iron with centuries of tar, and on a plank cradle hung from it Gideon Tarr, the Tarrer, brush in one hand, bucket on a hook, working over a patch of the carved procession where one figure has been gouged away. In the centre third the city goes up in storeys: the Ninth on top with its lit door-candles, the Eighth below with damp rooms rented by the bed, and under the canal-line the Seventh, its windows half full of water, glowing faintly green through the gratings. In the right third the houses lean together over a narrow canal like old men sharing a secret, ropes and plank bridges strung between them, and beyond them the Breath swallows everything into yellow-brown fog, with the long roofs of the Lofts just showing at the far right edge. Foreground: black water, a raft of old leaves, our own prow. Background: fog and a dark fly-front coming in low over the roofs. The lower centre band is canal water, still and nearly black, holding the city's reflection in broken strips. The title sits there, over water, with only the reflected door-candles beneath it.
#### Light and Colour
Two lights only. Hundreds of door-candles, lit by law at sixth bell, glow warm sepia through the fog so the whole city looks like something seen through the skin of a lantern. Below, the Drowned Storeys give off a cold bruise-green from the fen-glow jars Ossie Wade keeps lit, coming up through the gratings and the half-sunk windows. The accent bruise-green appears only below the canal-line and in the lacquered faces of the Kept porters. Fly-black is the incoming swarm over the roofs and the tar on the Post. Everything else is tea-stain brown and fog.
#### Texture and Flesh
The Post is tar laid on tar for six hundred years, in drips, ridges and blisters like the hide of something old, and the fresh coat steams in the damp. Gideon Tarr is so sealed in it himself that his face reads as carved: a Kept man with a glossy black jaw, a crack at the corner of his mouth showing pale grey under it, fingers fused stiff around the brush handle. Pilings below the waterline are furred with green weed and soft as wet bread, leaning but never breaking. On the plank bridges walk the Kept in waxed gauze and cedar lacquer, cheeks drawn up with wire at the corners and shining like sideboards. A red cord shows on every porter's left wrist. The water carries leaves, a bobbing cork, a slick of rainbow tallow. Weep is falling and has left tea-coloured streaks down every wall. You should be able to smell it from the paint: tar, vinegar, cedar smoke, and the sweetness all of those are there to hide.
#### The Story in the Frame
Lastgate is the capital of the grey north, built on ten thousand tarred pilings driven into the fen where the border road ends. The pilings do not rot; nothing here finishes. They soften, lean and sink an inch a decade, so for six centuries the city has built a new storey on top of the old and moved upstairs. Addresses give the storey, the board and the house. The Kept outnumber the breathing nine to one, and most families live with a dozen dead relatives at the table, up in attics, in chairs by the window, kept clean and stitched by people who will one day need keeping themselves.
This is the Landing, the city's door to the south: Company warehouses, punt-stands, foreigners' inns, customs men splashing vinegar on everything off the road. The bell of the Ferrying Post, cast in the year 9 and never finished cooling, has just rung sixth, and all over the city families are lighting their door-candles off tins of smoulder-coal.
Gideon Tarr has tarred the Post at Court wages since 338. He is the man who covers the gouges in the eighth figure of its carved procession. They used to come twice a year. Now they come every month, and tonight's is fresh, cut from inside the wood outward, as if something were trying to get out.
At ground level Lastgate feels like a crowd in a cellar. Planks give a little under each step. Voices across the canal seem to come from just behind you. Underfoot, through every grating, the Seventh Storey looks back at you, green and wet, and somebody down there is still sitting in their parlour with the water at their chin.
#### Hidden Details
- The gouged figure Gideon is tarring over is the eighth in the procession; under the wet tar its outline still shows, a little longer in the arm than the rest.
- In a green-lit window of the Seventh Storey, a small figure in a schoolroom smock is pressed to the glass: one of the Smalls, and next to her the long pole and tin of Ossie Wade.
- A red-corded Kept porter is walking south along the causeway at the far left, and a living hand has caught hold of the cord to turn him round.
- On a doorframe on the Ninth, a chalked fly-count reads *41*, the Mallory tally for Pocock's Almanack.
- One door-candle near the centre is leaning against the draught, toward the viewer: a Wick Tallow candle, trying to be noticed.
- Floating face-up beside the punt is a lacquered Guild mask with a painted smile, and the eyeholes are not empty.
#### Painter's Prompt
Dark painterly wide 3:1 view from a punt at water level of a sinking city on black tarred pilings in a warm yellow-brown fen fog, houses stacked in storeys and leaning over a narrow canal, hundreds of small door-candles glowing sepia through the mist, the lowest storeys half drowned with cold bruise-green light coming up through gratings. Left foreground: a huge ancient tar-black post carved with a procession, one figure gouged away, and a Kept man glossy with tar painting over it from a plank cradle. Lacquered, wire-stitched dead walking plank bridges. A low fly-swarm darkening the roofs. Still black canal water with broken reflections across the lower centre. Oil and ink-wash texture, dry-brush grit, heavy shadow, tea-stain sepia, bruise-green and fly-black accents, intimate decay.

## THE LOFTS
[plate: lofts]
[pull] Visitors remember the Lofts for the whispering. Wardens remember them for the drip. | — Loftside saying
**Plate:** THE LOFTS · *Nine storeys of the Set-Aside* · Sallowreach · bruise-green and fly-black on tea-stain sepia
#### The Shot
Inside Hall Six, at the floor of the aisle, looking up and down its length with a 24mm lens tilted slightly upward from waist height, so the shelving rises nine storeys on both sides and narrows to a vanishing point in brown dark. The 3:1 frame reads as a long tunnel of shelves. Left third: the near shelves close enough to read, bodies wrapped tight in waxed linen from feet to crown, laid side by side like rolled carpets, each with a card tied at the throat. Centre third: the aisle running away, its sawdust floor dark with drip, and halfway up the right-hand stack, on a ladder that runs on a rail, Aldo Penny, the Reader of Hall Six, with a lamp hooked on a rung and a letter held close to his near-sighted eyes, reading aloud to the fourth storey. Right third: the stacks continue into the dark, broken by the rails of other ladders, a Warden's lamp very far off like a single star, and at the extreme right, the edge of the Wrapping Room door, ajar, with a long table and a pale shape on it. Foreground: the lowest shelf, tarred black, and a family's small gifts left on its edge, a book, a dried flower, a child's drawing. Midground: Aldo on the ladder. Background: the vanishing aisle. The lower centre band is the aisle floor, dark sawdust and shadow, calm and nearly black. The title sits there, on the floor of the hall, under the drip.
#### Light and Colour
Low lamplight only, warm sepia, from Aldo's lamp and a few hooked along the rails, each throwing a small cone up the linen. The halls are kept cool, and the air between the lamps is brown dark. Bruise-green appears in the old linen of the upper shelves, where a century of seepage has stained the wrapping, and in the faint mould on the label-cards. Fly-black is the drip-marks on the sawdust and the few flies that got in, crawling on the oldest wraps. Thin threads of drip catch the lamplight as falling gold.
#### Texture and Flesh
Waxed linen, wound tight, creased and shiny at the high points of each body: brow, nose, folded hands, knees. Where the old wraps have slumped, the shape inside has settled downward, and the linen hangs in a soft pouch at the hips and jaw. Brown seeps through the weave in tide-lines like tea on a cloth. Below each upper shelf, slow beads of Loft-weather gather and fall, a sweetish brown rain that has spattered the tarred lower shelves into a crust. The label-cards are stained, rewritten, foxed, their strings sunk into the linen at the throat. On the oldest shelves the *Visited* line is simply ruled through. One wrapped figure close to the viewer has turned its head toward the lamp, and the linen over the mouth is moving. Camphor, wax, lamp-oil and old meat should rise off the paint together.
#### The Story in the Frame
The Lofts are nine long timber halls on the north edge of Lastgate where the Set-Aside are kept: the Kept too far gone in the long rot to stand or sit, wrapped, labelled and shelved. Forty thousand of them, nine storeys to a hall, all still aware. Families pay forty lacks to wrap and shelve a relative and one lack a season for upkeep, and come on feast days in a queue that runs the length of Loftside, carrying letters, books and small gifts the shelved cannot use. The Loft-Wardens move along the aisles on rail ladders with lamps and lists, rewrapping the oldest every decade in the Wrapping Room, where families are not invited, because what is under the linen after a hundred years is still talking.
The street outside is clean and swept and quiet except for the whispering, a soft continuous sound like rain on reeds that comes through the walls into every house on Loftside. Locals stop hearing it in a month. Visitors never do.
Aldo Penny is thirty-one, thin, with ink on his cuffs and a trained soft voice. He has read to the four hundred shelved of Hall Six every night for nine years and knows most of them by voice. Tonight he is reading Teal Mallory's letter to her mother Hanne, fourth storey, shelf nineteen: a day on Weir Row, eels, smudge-pots, nineteen saved breaths in a jar for a Closing license. Hanne listens. Around him, the whole hall listens.
What Aldo will not tell Teal, on Old Bettany's order, is that under the old whispering there is now a single word, and all the shelved are saying it together. He has begun waking at the same hour every night with it in his mouth. In the shot he has paused mid-line, lamp swaying, because the whisper has risen, and for a moment he is not sure whether the next word on the page is Teal's or theirs.
At ground level the Lofts feel like a library where every book is breathing. It is cool, and patient, and the drip ticks like a clock shop.
#### Hidden Details
- The card on shelf nineteen, fourth storey, reads *Hanne Mallory* and has the most feast-day marks on its *Visited* line of any card in the frame.
- On the far wall a Warden's chalked list has nine names struck through at once: shelved who went silent in the same night.
- Aldo's lips are shaped around a word that is not on the page he is holding.
- A candle in a lamp along the rail is a Wick Tallow named candle, leaning toward the shelves, not the aisle.
- In the Wrapping Room doorway, the figure on the table has linen unwound to the chest, and its hand is up, holding a Warden's wrist.
- The child's drawing on the bottom shelf shows a stick-figure family of two breathing and eleven wrapped, all smiling.
#### Painter's Prompt
Dark painterly 3:1 interior of a vast timber warehouse hall, looking down a long aisle between shelving nine storeys high, every shelf packed with human bodies tightly wrapped in waxed linen from feet to crown, label cards tied at their throats, brown seepage staining the cloth, slow drips falling from the upper shelves through warm lamplight. A thin young reader with ink-stained cuffs on a rail ladder halfway up the right-hand stack, lamp hooked beside him, reading a letter aloud to the shelves. Sawdust floor dark with drips; small gifts on the lowest shelf; a distant lamp far down the aisle. Warm sepia lamplight, brown darkness, bruise-green mould and staining, fly-black drip spatter. Oil paint and ink texture, intimate, still, unbearable patience.

## THE SUMP
[plate: sump]
[pull] The Sump is the only place in the north where nobody asks for anything back. | — Lastgate saying
**Plate:** THE SUMP · *Where the unstitchable are sunk* · Sallowreach · bruise-green and fly-black on tea-stain sepia
#### The Shot
At dusk, from the bow of the Sackmen's long punt on the open deep fen west of Lastgate, looking back down its length toward the stern with a 28mm lens at kneeling height, so the black water fills the lower half and the low sky hangs over it like a lid. The 3:1 frame is wide and flat and empty. Left third: three sewn hessian sacks lying in the bottom of the punt, roped, each with a lead name-strip at the neck, and the family who sewed them sitting very upright on the thwart beside them, not speaking. Centre third: the stern, and standing in it the Sackman, Abner Rook, fourth of his line, rope in both hands, a fourth sack just gone over the side, his lips moving on the words. The rope runs from his hands down into the water and has not yet gone slack. Right third: the open Sump, flat black water to a horizon of grey reed, and on its surface a pattern of rising bubbles, rings inside rings, with a line of them running away from the punt like something spelled. Far right, a dredger's stilt-hut with one lamp burning where none should be. Foreground: the bow and black water lapping over it. Midground: the punt, the family, the Sackman. Background: the bubble-rings, the reed horizon, the dredger's light. The lower centre band is the water beneath the stern, black, greasy, calm, where the rope enters it. The title sits on that water.
#### Light and Colour
Late dusk, the Breath gone from yellow to brown, giving a dull tea-stain sepia light from the west with no visible sun. A single horn lantern on a pole at the stern throws a small warm circle on the Sackman's hands and the rope. Bruise-green is the oily sheen on the surface of the Sump and the faint glow inside the bubble-rings as they break. Fly-black is the water itself, and the cloud of flies over the sacks. The dredger's far lamp is a pinprick of warm wrongness.
#### Texture and Flesh
The sacks are coarse hessian, wet through, sewn shut by family hands with uneven stitches, and the shapes inside are knees, a jaw, a shoulder, pressing at the weave. One sack has a darker patch spreading at the bottom, soaking into the punt's planks. The lead name-strips are dull grey, stamped with a name and a date, tied with tarred cord. The rope in the Sackman's hands is greasy and black with six centuries of use, and his palms are callused into hide. The Sump water is thick and slow, with a skin on it like cooling broth, and where the bubbles rise they leave rings of scum and a smell, implied in the paint, of drains. Abner wears a long oilskin gone soft and brown, a hood back, a living face lined and grey with damp. The family's hands are folded and very clean, because they scrubbed after the sewing.
#### The Story in the Frame
The Sump is the deep fen west of Lastgate where those beyond stitching are sunk: the Kept whose bodies have come apart too far for the Seamers' Guild to hold together, burned too badly, torn, or simply gone past wire and lacquer. It is the nearest thing Sallowreach has to a graveyard, and it is not one, because nothing in it is dead. At the bottom, by the account of the Kept who walk the fen-bed and have gone near it, is a mound: six hundred years of sacks heaped one on another, softened together, the ropes rotted into one mat, the whole of it slowly rising and falling.
The Sackmen are a hereditary trade, living and Kept, and they keep a strict and joyless decency. Each sack is sewn by the family, never by the Sackman. Each carries a lead strip with the name and date of sinking, so that what is down there may be found again if anyone needs it. At the drop the Sackman stands in the stern, holds the rope in both hands and recites the name, the dates and the words *Go under and keep*, and pays out until the rope goes slack. In the deep Sump that can take a long time. Nobody else speaks.
Abner Rook has done it since he was fourteen. Tonight he is sinking a grandmother, two uncles and a child cousin of the Lusk family from the Sinks, all of them past what the family could afford to have restitched, and he is on the first. The family watches the rope, not the water.
What they are not watching is the bubbles. Sump-watchers say the patterns are speech. Tonight they are rising in a line away from the punt, toward the dredger's hut at Grimsey Reach, where a lamp is burning after dusk. The dredgers, hated more than the Hookmen, come out with grapnels and rakes after the Sackmen leave.
At water level the Sump feels like being watched from below by something that is being polite about it. When Abner's rope goes slack, he will say the next name.
#### Hidden Details
- The child cousin's sack is the smallest, and the family's mother has stitched a sprig of rosemary into its seam; it is the only sack with no stain.
- In the bubble-rings, if read as a line, there are eight rings and then a gap where a ninth should be.
- On the dredger's platform at the far right, a pile of opened, emptied sacks is stacked to dry like grain-bags.
- Hung from a nail at the dredger's hut door is a single lead name-strip, kept, not cut off: the dredger's memento of someone, and the reason the hut has two cups on its table.
- Under the black surface beside the stern, a pale hand has come up to rest against the punt's planking, not pulling, only holding on.
#### Painter's Prompt
Dark painterly 3:1 view at dusk from the bow of a long flat punt on still black greasy fen water, low brown fog sky, reed horizon. In the stern a weathered man in a soft oilskin holds a black rope in both hands as it runs down into the water, a lantern on a pole lighting his hands. Hessian sacks sewn shut with human shapes pressing inside lie in the bottom of the punt, lead name-strips tied at their necks, a grieving family sitting upright beside them. To the right, bubbles rising in concentric rings and lines across the oily surface with a faint green glow; a distant stilt-hut with one lamp. Tea-stain sepia, bruise-green sheen, fly-black water and flies, ink-wash and dry-brush grit, quiet dread, heavy negative space.

## DUNMERE HUSH
[plate: dunmere]
[pull] We sing it to the end out here. That's the only place in the north a song gets an end. | — Brother Abel Fisk, at the Verge
**Plate:** DUNMERE HUSH · *The first and largest silence* · Sallowreach · bruise-green and fly-black on tea-stain sepia
#### The Shot
Standing at the Verge, the encampment along the edge of the silence, with a 50mm lens at eye height looking straight along the grey line, so it runs diagonally from the lower left corner to the right horizon and divides the frame. The 3:1 composition is two countries. Left third: the living fen, crowded with the Verge camp, tents, lean-tos, punts drawn up, candle-sellers, pilgrims kneeling, a soup kettle steaming, and at the kettle Brother Abel Fisk, a Kept Lamp of the Finishers, ladle in hand, feeding a queue of living children. Centre third: the line itself, as sharp as a cut: on this side green-brown reeds, flies, mud; a step further, reeds the grey of cold ash, standing perfectly still. A Finisher procession of candle-bearing Kept has just crossed it and walks on into the grey toward the village, getting smaller, their song stopped where they stopped. Right third: Dunmere village inside the Hush, four miles off, small and exact: doors open, washing on lines, and the shapes of its people lying where they lay down. Foreground: the Verge mud, wet, trampled, with dropped candles and name-strips. Midground: the line and the walkers. Background: the grey disc and the village under a white sky. The lower centre band is the dark trampled mud of the living side just before the line, where the grey begins. The title sits there, straddling the edge.
#### Light and Colour
On the living side, the usual tea-stain fog light, thick and moving, and the small warm glows of hundreds of Finisher candles. On the Hush side, a flat, even, painted light with no source and no shadow, because nothing there moves the air: everything rendered in bone-grey. The bruise-green accent lives on the living side only, in the reeds and the lacquered faces of the Kept pilgrims, and stops dead at the line. Fly-black is the swarm that hangs over the camp and stops in a straight wall at the edge.
#### Texture and Flesh
On the living side: muck, rope, wet canvas, beeswax dripped on everything, Kept pilgrims in waxed gauze with their seams loosening, faces brown and slipping under lacquer, carrying their own name-strips. The flies are thick, crawling in the corners of eyes. On the far side, nothing has texture except stillness. The grey reeds are dry and crisp as paper. The dead of Dunmere lie unrotted, cheeks whole, clothes clean, hands open, as if asleep in the road, and there are no flies on them at all. The pilgrims who have just crossed walk with their seams already settling, their lacquered faces losing the wet sheen as they go, as if colour were draining off them like water off a plate. Where the procession's candles cross the line, their flames stop flickering and look painted on.
#### The Story in the Frame
Eight years ago Dunmere was a reed-cutting village of three hundred souls, living and Kept. In the Carving of 633 a ferryman poling past at dawn could not hear the village dogs, or his pole, or himself shouting. Everyone inside was simply, properly dead. When the news reached Lastgate, half the country wept with envy. Now the silence is a grey disc four miles across, growing a few yards a month, and it is the first and largest of the Hushes: places where the god of death, eaten and unable to do its work for six centuries, has begun to close things again.
Around it is the Verge: two miles of camp along the line, moving outward with it. Finishers live here, the death cult that walks into the Hushes singing, in long processions mostly of the Kept, carrying candles and the names of the dead. The Lamps and Singers who lead them live here. The families who watched someone walk in and cannot go home live here. Court guards, all Kept, are posted at the main approach, and some of them have walked in themselves. Dredgers wade the edge for dropped rings. Someone sells bottled air from the line, which the Court calls stolen licenses.
Brother Abel Fisk has led forty pilgrimages to the edge and never once gone in. He runs the soup kitchen for the living children left behind. In the shot he has turned from the kettle to watch the procession reach the end of its song, because the custom here is the last verse: those who stay sing it to its end, and the song is finished when the last singer outside stops. A small boy in the queue is still singing. Abel is waiting for him to finish before he fills his bowl.
At ground level the Verge is loud until it is not. Voices drop as you walk toward the line. At the edge, the nothing presses on your ears like deep water, you taste iron, and your own heartbeat seems to come from out in the grey, walking toward you, never arriving.
#### Hidden Details
- Ten feet inside the line, on the grey grass, lie a pair of severed hands, grey, open, nine joints to a finger: Lord Pellam Fallow's, given back in the Restitution of 639.
- One footprint in the grey reeds leads in and then back out again: Edda Thrush's, from 638.
- Among the walkers is a living man in a Fen Guard coat, breathing fog from his mouth; the Finishers cannot always tell in the dark who is breathing.
- A woman on the living side holds the end of a rope that runs over the line and lies slack in the grey.
- The Verge camp's tent-pegs on the line itself have grey ash creeping up them; the edge moved in the night.
- A Coldharrow pair of boots stands by a tent flap, laces tied, toes pointing toward the line.
#### Painter's Prompt
Dark painterly 3:1 landscape at the edge of a perfect silence in a fen: a sharp diagonal line divides wet green-brown reeds, mud and a crowded camp of tents, candles and grieving pilgrims on the left from a vast still disc of ash-grey reeds on the right where nothing moves and no shadows fall. A procession of stitched, lacquered undead pilgrims carrying candles walks into the grey toward a distant silent village where clean unrotted bodies lie in the lanes. A gentle undead monk ladles soup for living children by a steaming kettle. A wall of flies stops at the line. Tea-stain sepia, bruise-green on the living side, bone-grey beyond, fly-black swarm; oil and ink-wash texture, mournful, vast negative space.

## THE JAR ROOM
[plate: jarroom]
[pull] Read them out each morning, love. They like to hear their names. Even the ones facing the wall. | — Merrit Cask's mother, handing her the keys
**Plate:** THE JAR ROOM · *One shelf, very long* · Sallowreach · bruise-green and fly-black on tea-stain sepia
#### The Shot
In the low brick vault under the Hall of Hands, from one end, with a 40mm lens at seated eye height, looking down the full length of the single shelf so it runs from the left edge into a far point near the right third. The 3:1 frame is a long, low tunnel, the ceiling barely above the jars. Left third: the near end of the shelf, close and enormous, three thick green glass jars sealed with lead, each holding a head floating in amber pickle, hair drifting upward; on the shelf below, smaller jars of hands. Centre third: the shelf going away, hundreds of jars catching the lamplight one after another like a row of drowned lanterns, and halfway down, in a plain wooden chair, Merrit Cask, Keeper of the Jar Room, a lamp on the floor beside her, a ledger on her knee, reading the labels aloud. Right third: the far end of the vault, dark, and a stretch of the shelf where nine jars in a row have gone cloudy and still, their heads slumped, the labels unchanged. Foreground: the brick floor and the iron drains set into it beneath the shelf, wet. Midground: Merrit and her chair. Background: the far brick wall and the dark. The lower centre band is the floor, dark brick and the black mouths of the drains, calm. The title sits along the drains.
#### Light and Colour
One lamp, warm sepia, on the floor beside Merrit, throwing her shadow long down the vault and lighting the jars from below so each face glows through its glass. Bruise-green is the glass itself, thick and old, and the skin of moisture sweating on every jar. Fly-black is the vault's dark, the lead seals and the drains. The amber pickle in each jar catches the lamp, but keep it brown-gold and dull, never bright. Where the nine silent jars stand, the green goes flat and grey, as if the light had stopped reaching them.
#### Texture and Flesh
The jars are thick hand-blown glass, bubbled and uneven, so each face behind them is warped and doubled. Every jar sweats, a skin of green beads running down to pool on the shelf as if the vault were breathing on them. Inside, the heads float in thick amber: skin gone smooth and pale and soft as cheese, eyelids open, lips moving, hair rising and turning slowly like weed in a still pool. The severed necks are sealed with old wax and stitched. On the shelf below, the hands are busy, opening, closing, drumming, the nails worn to stubs from scratching at the glass, which is clouded in arcs where they have worked. Labels on parchment, stained, give name, crime, date. A few have a second label in red. Merrit's hands are red from lye; her apron is damp.
#### The Story in the Frame
The Jar Room is the Mortuary Court's prison: one shelf, very long, the full length of a vault under the Hall of Hands on the Upper Boards. In a land where nothing dies, the worst sentence is not death, which is the most precious thing the Court has to sell, but its opposite: to be jarred, Kept forever in pieces, head in one jar, hands in another, the rest rendered or sunk. The heads are aware. The oldest jar is from 304. The jarred for treason are shelved facing the wall. The Salt Assay has proved with buckets and arithmetic that the sea is not evaporating, and has been forbidden to say where it is going instead. Few go to the edge. Fewer return. Dagna Hull is building a sealed iron vessel, the *Follow*, to chase the water down, and has nine hundred volunteers.
Kessa Drowle builds Lowmark's next mile of pier each year. She has calculated the year the timber runs out and the year the sea drops off the shelf entirely, and they are the same year, 650. Beyond the twentieth mile the seabed falls to the Trench and pilings will have nothing to stand on. She has begun ordering pilings longer than any sea could need and does not know why. Her foreman kept from her that at dawn, three times this Carving, new pilings have been found standing west of the pier's end in a straight line, still wet. The Tenanted among the crews get up in the night, quiet as cats, and come back with blistered hands.
Tonight she followed the line herself, thirty miles, by lantern. It ends here. In the shot she has just seen that the last piling is not the last: there is another beyond the lip, standing in the water, driven into nothing, and below it another, going down like a stair.
At ground level the Trench is warm when the whole coast is cold, and the swallowing never stops.
#### Hidden Details
- Beyond the lip, a further row of piling-tops descends into the black water in a straight line, like a stair going down.
- The Salt Assay gauge-post's gauge is snapped off; carved into the wood below it is a long column of tallies and the word *matches*.
- In the deep light, if looked at long enough, there is a shape like a hand, grey and longer than a boat, palm up.
- Kessa's pencil has written, on the piling beside her, a depth figure that is not a number but a name.
- One of the Tenanted crewmen still wears a cut wrist-rope, the knot neatly tied, the cut clean.
- Far down, eight faint points of light, one closer than the rest.
