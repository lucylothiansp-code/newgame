# The Table's Cookbook

> We were hungry. Say it plainly. We were hungry, and they were there.
>> — first line of every history written on the Table, and, in Gristmoot, of every cookery book

> Eat while it's warm. It won't be.
>> — Fatlands proverb, stitched on oven-cloths

[fiction]
*Preface to* The Receipts of the Seven Tables and the Road Between, *gathered by Odo Pellance, under-cook of the Company inn at Saltlick Cross, in the twenty-sixth year of his service, 641 A.G. The manuscript was bought at the Gristmoot rag-stall bound in oilcloth, with a crust of reed-bread pressed between the last two pages like a flower.*
I have cooked for every kind of mouth there is. I have cooked for Fatlanders who ate the plate's glaze off when the stew was gone, and for an Oathen notary who looked at my roast for a quarter of an hour and then signed, slowly, *if able*, and did not touch it. I have cooked for the Kept, who chew for company and spit into a blue dish, and who always, always say it was lovely, because what else is there for them to say. I have set a cold supper on the step for an Unfed boy and watched him walk past it three times before he would take it on the fourth, and then eat it facing the stable wall, with tears on him, as if I had hit him.
A cook on the Rim learns two things early. The first is that every land eats its god, still, every day, a little, and calls it supper. The second is that nobody on the Table has ever once been properly full, and that the ones who say they are are lying or are Seated.
I have written down what they eat and how they eat it and what they say before and after. I have put in the curses where I know them, because a receipt without its curse is half a receipt. Where a dish is wicked I have said so. Where it is only sad I have said that too. Where I have eaten it myself I have marked it with a small cross, and there are more crosses than I would like.
On the Inn at the Crossroads: we burn the table. We do not read the card. I want that written down here, in my own hand, before anything else. We burn the table.
I have been at Saltlick twenty-six years. The chair rents for a platter a night now. I have never once seen it empty at dusk.
[/fiction]

This chapter is a cookbook in two voices. The first is the Table's own: the receipts, rites, curses and manners of seven lands and the Rim Road between them, set out dish by dish as the cooks of the Long Grace would set them out, with what the food does to those who eat it. The second voice is ours. Every dish ends with a box headed **For Your Table**, which is a real recipe, for real and ordinary food, made from ingredients found in any market, to be cooked and eaten on game night. The wake-pie of the Fatlands is, at your table, a beef and ale stew. Lady Aspic's blinking jelly is a red-wine jelly with grapes in it. The ration-bread of the Fast is an oatcake. The horror is all in the fiction; the food is just food, and it is good.

[bigquote] A receipt without its curse is half a receipt. | — Odo Pellance, under-cook of Saltlick Cross

## How to Use This Cookbook

Each land has its own section, opened by its sigil and by a short account of its **table manners**: how its people sit (or stand), what they say, what they will not eat, and what will get a guest thrown out, broken, needled or served. Then come five or six dishes. Each dish has four parts and a box:

- **The receipt**, in an in-world hand, set as fiction. These are not instructions anyone should follow. Several of them are crimes.
- **Lore**, the story, ritual or curse the dish carries: who first made it, who still does, and what happened at the table where it went wrong.
- **Rules**, for the dish in play: its Taint (see the Rim Market chapter), any effect it has on Hunger, Flesh, Fray or the land's own tracks (Weariness, Pangs, Want), and the Dread check, if any, for eating it, watching it eaten, or knowing what it is.
- **Hook**, a scene or adventure seed that begins at the plate.
- **For Your Table**, a real recipe themed to the dish, with quantities and steps, for players to cook and share.

[box: A Note on the Real Recipes]
Every For Your Table recipe is ordinary home cooking: no rare ingredients, nothing dangerous, nothing that is not normal food. Quantities are given in metric and cups; oven temperatures in Celsius with Fahrenheit and gas mark. Recipes serve four to six unless stated, which is a party plus a Carver. Check your guests for allergies before game night: several dishes use nuts, gluten, dairy, eggs, shellfish or fish, and each box says so. Where a recipe uses wine, beer or spirit, an alcohol-free swap is given; the drinks marked "for adults" are for adults. Handle hot sugar, hot oil and sharp knives with the care you would give a Hand-line's ungloved fingers. And do not, whatever the fiction says, eat anything that bites back.
[/box]

### Cooking in Play

Characters cook, and on the Table cooking is a craft with consequences. To cook any dish in this chapter properly, a character rolls **Eye + Craft** against the dish's listed Difficulty (most are Routine 10; wake dishes, jellies and anything of a god are Hard 14 or worse). A character who knows the land's own way of cooking it (a native, or anyone who has eaten it at the source) gains +2.

- **Success.** The dish is made and has its listed effect.
- **Helping.** For each Helping, one diner of the cook's choice gains an extra benefit: −1 Fray, or 1 Flesh healed, or +1 to their next Persuade roll at that table.
- **Grace (doubles on a success).** The dish is remembered. Everyone at the table gains −1 Fray, and the cook gains a reputation in that land: +2 to Persuade anyone who ate it, for a season.
- **Lack (doubles on a failure).** The dish carries its curse. Every dish below says what that is.

**A shared meal.** A party that sits (or stands, in the Fast and the Vigil) to a proper meal together, with the grace said, removes one level of Starving, or one Pang in the Fast, and each character may roll Caul + Resolve (Routine 10) to shed 1 Fray. Only once a day. In the Fatlands, ordinary food never satisfies: the Starving level goes, but Hunger does not, and a Fatlander who eats a full meal and is still hungry is simply a Fatlander.

**Eating the god.** Anything grown in a Godeater soil, coughed from a Godeater lung, or carved from a Godeater body carries Taint, as given in the Rim Market chapter. Foreign gods are worse. An Unfed character who eats Tainted food of a Godeater land risks Seasoning; three marks and they are Returned. Several dishes below are perfectly safe. Several are sold as safe and are not. The receipt usually knows the difference. The cook does not always say.

[pull] Every pot has a granny in it. | — Fatlands proverb, said fondly

## The Grace in Seven Forms

> Lack keep away.
>> — said before eating with any stranger, everywhere on the Table

Nobody on the Table eats with a stranger without first saying *Lack keep away*. Skipping it is the universal insult: worse than spitting, worse in some lands than a blade. But the grace is never only the three words. Every land has added to it, cut from it, signed it, slapped it or refused it, and a traveller who learns the seven forms eats in seven countries as a guest instead of a suspect.

**In play.** Saying a land's own grace correctly at its table gives +1 to every Persuade and Haggle roll with the hosts for the rest of the meal. Saying the common grace only is courteous and gives nothing. Saying no grace at all with strangers is an insult: −2 to all social rolls at that table, and in some lands, worse, as each form below notes.

### The Fen Grace (Sallowreach)

*Lack keep away. May we all finish.* Said by the eldest upright person at the table, who is often dead. If the eldest's jaw no longer holds, the next eldest says it on their behalf, standing beside their chair with one hand on the back of it. The Kept at table do not eat but answer *finish* with a click. To say *long life* after the grace is the worst curse in the north, and a guest who does it by mistake (it is the commonest toast on the Rim) will find every Kept head at the table turned slowly toward them, and the meal going on in silence. Dread 1, the first time.

### The Field Grace (the Fatlands)

*Thank you, Long Acre. Thank you, Hessop's Rise. Thank you, Granny Dunn's Meadow.* Every Fatlands meal begins by thanking the fields by name, the fields the food came from, which are very often the household's own dead, Seated, as hills. Strangers add *Lack keep away* at the end. The grace ends, after the meal, with the plate wiped clean with bread. A guest who leaves a smear is fined; a guest who does not thank the field is thought to be either a Thin or a thief, and a Bailiff may be called. Five times a day.

### The Dry Grace (Brinehollow)

*Lack keep away, and keep you dry.* Said with the bail-bucket at the foot of the chair, after everyone has bailed, because it is unforgivable to cough brine at the table. The answer is *and you*. At the Lantern on Mile Nine and every Mile inn, no table on the west wall is laid after dusk, and nobody says the grace facing west, because a word said toward the sea is said to the sea. In houses that have lost someone to the Calling, a pause is left after *dry*, the length of a breath, for whoever is not there.

### The Waking Grace (the Vigil)

*Lack keep away*, said with the eyes open, wide, every eye at the table on every other eye, and followed at once by a flat-handed slap on the table, loud enough to rattle the sharp-rimmed bowls. In the rich houses of the Height the slap is a ritual of great elegance, done with a jewelled paddle. On the Slope it is done with a fist. A guest who blinks during the grace is pinched by their neighbours, hard, as a kindness. A guest who closes their eyes to pray, as is the custom in some lands of the Rim, is needled.

### The Knocking Grace (Cradlewrack)

The table is knocked three times before anyone sits, to warn whatever is under it. Then: *Lack keep away, and let it stay.* The highland addition is older than the Gorging and its meaning has drifted. Once it meant *let the Lack stay away*. Now every highlander means something else by it, privately, and looks at the door while they say it. A guest who sits before the knocking is regarded with real horror; it is like opening a bricked room without leave. A guest who does not look at the door is thought to be very brave or very foreign.

### The Signed Grace (Oathen)

In Oathen the grace is signed in handtalk, which is held not to bind: the left hand flat, palm down, and drawn away from the mouth. It is said aloud only when foreigners are at table, and then very slowly, with three exits: *Lack keep away, if able; as I now intend; barring weather.* Nothing more is said for the length of the meal. To praise the food aloud is a grave error, because *this is delicious* is a statement, and the god weighs statements; a foreigner who said *best meal of my life* at a Tacit table was found the following morning unable to taste anything, ever again. Weight 1.

### The Decline (the Fast)

The Unfed do not say grace. They refuse it. Before eating, standing, facing a wall, an Unfed says *No, thank you* three times, quietly, to nobody, and then eats what they carried themselves. The Decline is the whole of their table-rite and the whole of their last rites, and to hear it said in the dusk of a Wanting yard by a hundred people at once, all facing outward, is a thing travellers do not forget. An Unfed who hears a Godeater say *Lack keep away* will sometimes add, under their breath, *it didn't*.

### The Grace Bell (the Rim)

On the Rim Road the common grace is said at noon at the Grace Bell, when three miles of Gristmoot stalls stop at once and every person turns to the nearest stranger and says it, face to face. It is the one moment of the day on the Rim when nobody is selling anything. Company inns ring a smaller bell at supper. The Company's own *Book of Usages* adds one instruction to the grace, and it is followed by every inn-keeper from Fogmouth to Wanting Post: *Count the chairs before the grace. Count them again after.*

[box: The Line That Is Not Said]
The oldest grace-stones on the Table, at the corners of Fatlands fields and in the walls of the Witnessing Vaults and on the lintel of the Empty Chair, carry the grace carved in Table-speech, and every one of them is longer than the grace anyone says. After *Lack keep away* there is a further line, and in every case it has been chiselled out: by different tools, in different centuries, leaving a rough pale scar the length of perhaps five words. Grandfather Peat, the bog body of the Sump, will say the common grace if asked, in his dead dialect, and then stops, with his mouth open, as if waiting for someone else to say the rest. A character who studies the scars (Eye + Lore, Dire 22) can make out the shape of one word only, which in the old script might be *guest* or might be *chair*. Carvers should not say which. A character who works out that it is the same word in all seven lands makes a Dread 2 check, and from then on, at every meal, notices how many chairs there are.
[/box]

## Sallowreach: The Kept Table

[sigil: sallowreach]

> The Kept to be helped to spit into the blue dish, not the good one.
>> — Tablenight bill of fare, Needle Street, 640 A.G.

Food in the north is sour, smoked and quick. The living eat eel that will not stop moving, reed-bread from the fen-margins, mushroom from the peat, and pickled everything, because vinegar is the one thing in Sallowreach that makes rot stop and think. Imported Fatlands meat is feast food. Stilled meat, closed by a licensed butcher's touch, is a luxury, the only properly dead flesh in the country, and tastes, northerners say, of quiet. The Kept do not need to eat, and most of them still come to table, and chew, and spit, and say that it is lovely.

### Table Manners in the North

- **The eldest upright says grace.** Even if the eldest upright cannot keep their jaw on. The family waits.
- **Set a place for every Kept in the house,** including the ones in the attic. Plates for the shelved are carried to the Lofts in the morning.
- **The blue dish** stands in the middle of every northern table, for the Kept to spit into. Never put food on it. Never use it for bones. Visitors from the Fatlands have eaten from it and the story is still told on the Landing.
- **Eat fast, with the back teeth,** and do not talk while chewing eel. It gives them a chance.
- **Never close the lid on someone else's pot.** Closing is the Hand-lines' business, and a guest who puts a lid on the host's dish has insulted the host's blood. A guest who shuts a Kept diner's eyes for them, even kindly, has committed a mortal offence.
- **Do not wish anyone long life.** Do not say *I could eat that forever*. Do not say *it's to die for*; say *it would finish me*, which is the highest praise there is.
- **Bring vinegar or perfume.** A guest who smells of nothing is either a Closer or rude.

[pull] Mam says don't give it to visitors. I say let them learn. | — the latest hand in the Mallory receipt-book, Weir Row

### Lively Eel in Sour

[fiction]
*From a fishwife's slate on the Landing, Lastgate, chalked fresh each morning and wiped each night, Carving 641.*
EEL. LIVELY. DON'T ASK HOW LONG.
Pot boiling hard: sour, vinegar, dill. Eel behind the head with a rag. Four pieces, quick, on the block, before they find each other. Lid on, stone on lid. Boil the length of the Closing Hymn. Pieces that climb the side go back with the spoon. Reed-bread, a pickle, eat it standing if you like. Don't lie down after.
TWO CRUMBS THE PLATE. FOUR IF IT'S STILL GOING.
[/fiction]

**Lore.** No fen-eel has died in six hundred and forty-one years, and the Sump is full of them, the same eels, pulled up and boiled and eaten and passed and returned to the water, round and round, which the fishwives of the Landing say is why the eels of the north know the way home. Cut, they try to rejoin; boiled, they knock on the lid. Eaten, they keep moving in the belly for an hour or so, which northerners regard as a pleasant warmth and foreigners regard as the worst hour of their lives. The cure is to drink a cup of *sour*, the peat-smoked beer, and walk about. The worst thing a northern mother can say to a lazy child is *you'll lie down after eel, you will*.

**Rules.** Difficulty Routine 10 to cook. No Taint for a Sallowreacher. For anyone else: Taint 1 Dread, the first time (the knocking). A diner who lies down within an hour of eating it must make a Gut + Endure roll (Hard 14) or lose 1 Flesh and the rest of the night, as the pieces argue. On a cook's **Lack**, two pieces found each other in the pot, and one bowl has a whole eel in it, quite unhurt, and very interested in the diner.

**Hook.** A fishwife on the Landing will swear on her mother's jaw that the eel she sold the party this morning has come back. She knows it by a nick in its fin. It has come back eleven times this year, and each time it is a little longer, and this morning it had a ring on, a gold ring, Lastgate-made, engraved inside with the name of a woman sunk in the Sump in a weighted sack in 588.

[box: For Your Table — Fish in Dark Beer with Dill and Mustard]
Serves 4. Time: 35 minutes. Contains fish, gluten (beer), mustard. The eel's knock is replaced by the crackle of the beer; nothing in the pot will move.
- 4 fillets of firm white fish (cod, haddock or hake), about 150 g / 5 oz each
- 1 tbsp butter and 1 tbsp oil
- 1 onion, finely sliced
- 250 ml / 1 cup dark beer or stout (or alcohol-free stout, or chicken stock)
- 2 tbsp cider vinegar
- 1 tbsp wholegrain mustard
- 1 tsp brown sugar
- 4 tbsp crème fraîche or sour cream
- a large handful of fresh dill, chopped
- salt and black pepper; rye bread and gherkins to serve
Melt the butter with the oil in a wide frying pan with a lid. Soften the onion over a medium heat for 8–10 minutes until golden. Pour in the beer and vinegar, stir in the mustard and sugar, and let it bubble for 5 minutes until reduced by about a third.
Season the fish and lay it in the sauce. Cover with the lid (a stone on top is optional) and simmer gently for 6–8 minutes until the fish flakes when pressed. Lift the fish onto warm plates. Stir the crème fraîche and most of the dill into the pan, warm through without boiling, and taste for salt.
Spoon the sauce over the fish, scatter the rest of the dill, and serve with dark rye bread and a gherkin on the side. Eat it quickly, with the back teeth, and do not lie down after.
[/box]

### Reed-Bread for the Dead

[fiction]
*From the receipt-book of the Mallory house, Weir Row, in Gran Mallory's hand, which is very old, and in places sewn back into the page where the paper tore.*
For the Dish for the Dead you want the black reed-flour from the far margin and none of your Rim white. Rye if you must. A cup of old sour to wake it. Caraway, a palmful, and the treacle, and a spoon of ash from the peat so it bakes dark as a shut door. Knead it with the heel of the hand and not the fingers, the fingers being for other things. It rises slow. Everything here does. Bake it till it knocks back.
One loaf at every Kept place on Tablenight. The youngest breaks it. Nobody eats it. It goes up to the Lofts at dawn, in the basket, for the shelved, who cannot eat it either, but who can smell it, and who say thank you, all of them, at once, which is a sound you do not want to hear twice.
[/fiction]

**Lore.** Reed-bread is the north's daily loaf, but the Dish for the Dead is its sacrament: the same black bread, made a little richer, broken by the youngest child of the house and laid at every Kept place at the Tablenight banquet. The custom is older than Keeping. Bram Hollin, the Last Mourner, says that before the Gorging the loaf was laid on the closed eyes of the dead so that Ossel would know the household had fed them to the end. Now the dead do not close, and the loaf goes up to the Lofts, and the Wardens stack the Tablenight bread along the ends of the shelves, where it does not go stale, because nothing in Sallowreach finishes, so that some of the loaves in the oldest Lofts are three hundred years old and still smell faintly of caraway. The Loft-Wardens eat them when they are hungry. Old Bettany Shroud says they taste of being remembered.

**Rules.** Routine 10. No Taint, unless the loaf has been on a Loft shelf: a Loft-loaf carries Taint 1 Regrowth (Ossel) and, to the eater, the whispering of the shelved for one night, which counts as a Dread 1 check and gives one true answer to any question asked of the dead in the Lofts. On a cook's **Lack**, the loaf will not finish baking. It stays warm and damp in the middle for ever.

**Hook.** Old Bettany Shroud wants the party to carry the Tablenight basket to the ninth storey of the Lofts, where the shelved are oldest. The basket holds forty loaves. The ninth storey has forty-one shelves. On the forty-first is someone who has no family, who was shelved without a label, and who has been asking, for two hundred years, in a voice like paper, whether anyone has brought the bread yet.

[box: For Your Table — Black Rye Bread]
Makes 1 large loaf. Time: 30 minutes active, 3–4 hours rising, 50 minutes baking. Contains gluten.
- 300 g / 2½ cups dark rye flour
- 200 g / 1⅔ cups strong white bread flour
- 7 g / 1 sachet (2¼ tsp) fast-action dried yeast
- 1½ tsp salt
- 1 tbsp caraway seeds, plus extra for the top
- 1 tbsp cocoa powder (for colour; it will not taste of chocolate)
- 2 tbsp black treacle or molasses
- 350 ml / 1½ cups warm water (or half water, half dark beer)
- 1 tbsp vegetable oil
Mix the flours, yeast, salt, caraway and cocoa in a large bowl. Stir the treacle into the warm water until dissolved, then pour it in with the oil and mix to a sticky dough. Rye dough is heavy and tacky; do not add much extra flour. Knead on a lightly floured surface with the heel of your hand for 8 minutes. It will not become smooth and springy like white bread. That is correct.
Shape into a round, set it on a baking tray lined with baking paper, cover loosely with an oiled sheet of cling film, and leave somewhere warm for 2–3 hours until noticeably puffed. Rye rises slowly. Everything here does.
Heat the oven to 200°C / 400°F / gas 6. Brush the loaf with water, scatter caraway, and slash a cross in the top. Bake 45–50 minutes until dark and hollow-sounding when knocked underneath. Cool completely on a rack before slicing, at least an hour. Serve thin, with butter, gherkins and cheese, and lay one slice at the empty chair.
[/box]

### Tablenight Pie with a Closed Eye

[fiction]
*From the Needle Street bill of fare, Tablenight 640, in the kitchen chalk: the second course, with a note.*
SECOND — Pie of Fatlands mutton and fen-mushroom, with a crust made to look like a closed eye.
*(Note on the slate beneath, in the mother's hand:)* The eye is SHUT. Lashes down. Do the lid with the back of the knife, not the point. Last year Teal did it open, for a joke, and Great-Grandfather would not come down from the attic for a week, and the flies were dreadful, and we lost a Plate's rent in vinegar. SHUT, Teal.
[/fiction]

**Lore.** The Tablenight pie is the centrepiece of the northern banquet for the dead: imported mutton, the fattest that can be afforded, with fen-mushroom and pickled onion under a lid decorated with a pastry eye, closed. The closed eye is Ossel's, the Closing Hand's, as it was carved on the old fen-shrines: the god that shut everything that finished. Every house in Lastgate makes the eye a little differently, and Seamers say they can tell a family by the way it draws the lashes. In the houses of the Hand-lines the pie is never cut by a living hand. A Kept servant cuts it, slowly, because the pastry eye must not be opened by the knife, and the slice must be taken from the side; to cut through the eye is to wish the god awake, and in 633, the year of Dunmere, the cook of House Vane cut through it by accident, and that winter, people say, was when Osric Vane's fingers grew their sixth joints.

**Rules.** Hard 14 to cook well (the pastry eye). No Taint to a northerner; Fatlands mutton carries Taint 1 Hunger to everyone else, which most people do not notice. A pie served with the eye properly shut at a Tablenight banquet gives every living diner −1 Fray. On a cook's **Lack** the eye bakes open, and every Hand-line at the table must make a Caul + Resolve roll (Hard 14) or feel their Want to close it, now, with their fingers.

**Hook.** The pie-shop of Widow Alder on Tanner's Boards has made the House Vane Tablenight pie for a hundred and ten years. This year the order came with a pastry-stamp for the eye, cut in ivory, very old, sent down from the Vane wing in a lead box. The eye on the stamp is open. The Widow wants to know whether to use it, and who in the locked wing could possibly have carved it with fingers like that.

[box: For Your Table — Lamb and Mushroom Pie]
Serves 6. Time: 2½ hours (most of it simmering). Contains gluten, dairy, egg, celery (in stock).
- 900 g / 2 lb lamb shoulder or neck, diced
- 2 tbsp plain flour, seasoned with salt and pepper
- 2 tbsp oil
- 1 large onion, chopped, and 12 small pickled or fresh silverskin onions
- 250 g / 9 oz chestnut mushrooms, quartered
- 2 garlic cloves, crushed
- 1 tbsp tomato purée and 1 tbsp Worcestershire sauce
- 500 ml / 2 cups lamb or beef stock
- 2 sprigs thyme and 1 bay leaf
- 1 tbsp malt or red wine vinegar
- 320 g / 11 oz ready-rolled puff pastry
- 1 egg, beaten
Toss the lamb in the seasoned flour. Brown it in batches in the oil in a heavy casserole and set aside. Soften the chopped onion in the same pan for 5 minutes, add the mushrooms and garlic and cook until the mushrooms colour. Stir in the purée and Worcestershire, return the lamb, pour in the stock, add the herbs and small onions, cover and simmer very gently for 1½ hours until the lamb is tender. Stir in the vinegar, taste, and cool for at least 20 minutes.
Heat the oven to 200°C / 400°F / gas 6. Spoon the filling into a pie dish, lay the pastry over, trim, and crimp the edges. With leftover pastry, cut an almond-shaped eyelid and a fan of thin strips for lashes, laid downward. Press them on top, so the eye is shut. Brush everything with egg, cut a small steam hole somewhere that is not the eye, and bake 30–35 minutes until deep gold. Serve the first slice from the side.
[/box]

### Undying Cheese

[fiction]
*A cheese-seller's label, pasted to the rind of a decade-wheel from the Brinemoss dairies, still legible after forty years.*
BRINEMOSS DECADE CHEESE. Laid down: Lack 601. To be opened: at a Setting Aside, a marriage, or a Closing, and not before, and not for less. The cheese will continue to ripen once cut. This is not a fault. Keep it in a cool place, in a lidded crock, with the lid weighted, away from the Kept, who like it, and away from children, who should not hear it.
[/fiction]

**Lore.** Nothing in Sallowreach finishes ripening. A cheese made in the north goes on maturing for as long as anyone keeps it, deeper and sharper and softer every year, until the oldest wheels in the cellars of the Upper Boards are not so much food as weather, and have to be cut with a wire and eaten with a spoon. The dairies of Brinemoss sell them by the decade. Families lay a wheel down at a birth and open it at a Setting Aside, so that the old one's shelving is marked by a cheese exactly as old as they are, and the family eats it at the wrapping, crumbled on reed-bread, in silence, while the linen goes up over the face. The rind is dusted with peat-ash to keep it black. The oldest wheels hum faintly. The Brinemoss dairy-wives say it is the cultures. The cultures, in Sallowreach, do not die either.

**Rules.** Routine 10 to serve; a decade-wheel costs 1 L, sold by the decade. No Taint to northerners. To others, a wheel more than fifty years old carries Taint 1 Regrowth (Ossel) and a Dread 1 check, because of the hum. A wedge of decade-cheese eaten at a Setting Aside lets each diner recover 1 Fray: grief, here, is supposed to be shared, and to taste of something. On a **Lack**, the wheel is opened too young, and the family will not forgive the party for it.

**Hook.** A cheese-merchant of the Upper Boards has the oldest wheel in the north in his cellar: laid down in 2 A.G., in the Glad Century, when the first generation had not yet begun to come apart. He wants to sell it to a Second Table buyer for an extraordinary sum. The wheel is not humming any more. It is speaking, in a dialect only Grandfather Peat understands, and what it says is the guest list.

[box: For Your Table — Ash-Crusted Labneh Cheese]
Makes about 12 small balls. Time: 15 minutes, plus 24–36 hours straining. Contains dairy, sesame. A soft fresh cheese made from yogurt, rolled in black seeds so it looks as if it has been dusted with peat-ash.
- 1 kg / 2 lb full-fat plain yogurt (or Greek yogurt)
- 1 tsp salt
- 3 tbsp black sesame seeds, or a mix of black sesame and nigella (black onion) seeds
- 1 tbsp dried thyme or za'atar
- olive oil, to cover
Stir the salt into the yogurt. Line a sieve with a double layer of muslin or a clean thin tea towel and set it over a bowl. Spoon in the yogurt, gather the cloth over the top, and set it in the fridge for 24 hours (36 for a firmer cheese). Whey will drain out into the bowl; this is the cheese ripening, slowly, as northern cheese does.
Unwrap the thick labneh. With lightly oiled hands, roll it into walnut-sized balls and set them on a tray in the fridge for an hour to firm. Mix the black seeds and thyme on a plate and roll each ball until it is coated dark all over.
Pack the balls into a clean jar and cover with olive oil. Keep in the fridge and eat within a week. Serve on black rye bread with honey or a pickle, and do not listen at the jar.
[/box]

### Burnt Honey Cakes for the Kept

## The Fatlands: The Table That Eats Back

[sigil: fatlands]

> Nobody's full, but some are fuller.
>> — Fatlands proverb

Fatland cooking is lavish, rich and brown: pies and puddings, roasts and dripping, sausages fat as a forearm, gravy on everything. All of it is grown in Ummer's flesh, all of it is delicious, and none of it satisfies. Five meals a day are law. The dead are served at the wake. The wheat has teeth.

### Table Manners on the Plains

- **Five meals:** Firstbite, Elevenses, Midday, Fourth Plate, Supper. Travellers must comply. Bailiffs inspect inns with a ladle.
- **Thank the field by name** before every meal, and wipe the plate clean with bread after. A smear is a fine. Leaving food is a fine. Refusing a meal is sedition.
- **Feed your neighbour.** It is courteous to put food on the plate of the person beside you, by hand, before your own. Lovers do it with their fingers. Bailiffs do it with a funnel, gently.
- **Compliment the body.** *You look heavy* is the kindest thing there is. *You look well* means nothing. *You look thin* is said only to start a fight.
- **Never ask what it was.** At a wake, never ask which part. At an inn, never ask whose. The question is answered, sooner or later, by the taste.
- **Do not thank the cook.** Thank the field. The cook is only the one who brought it to the table.

## Brinehollow: The Cold Table

[sigil: brinehollow]

> Hot soup (by request only, and we'll look at you).

Brinehollow eats what the Uncovered gives and what the Rim Road sells. Fish, once daily bread, is now a luxury hauled nineteen miles down the Mile in wet barrels. Heat the oven to 220°C / 425°F / gas 7. Rinse and pat the fish dry and stuff the cavity with lemon slices and herbs. Mix the salt with the egg whites and a splash of water until it feels like damp sand.
Spread a third of the salt on a large baking tray in a layer a little longer than the fish. Lay the fish on it and pack the rest of the salt over and around it, pressing firmly, so the fish is entirely buried (the tail may peek out). Bake 30 minutes for a 1 kg fish.
Bring it to the table in its crust. Crack the crust with the back of a heavy spoon and lift it away in pieces. Peel back the skin and lift the white flesh off the bone. Dress with olive oil and lemon. Turn the head inland.
[/box]

### Kelp-Bread from the Cellar Tide

[fiction]
*A note pinned inside a cellar door on the Middle Mile, Lowmark, by a landlady, for her lodgers.*
The tide comes up the cellar stair twice a day. It leaves kelp on the steps. You may take the kelp for bread, it is free, it is clean, it is the same kelp the whole Mile eats. You may NOT go down past the fourth step while the water is up, whatever you hear. Mr. Ambry went down past the fourth step in Lack and the tide went out and he did not. If you hear him, he is not asking for bread.
[/fiction]

**Lore.** In every cellar in Brinehollow, however far inland, water rises twice a day and drains away, leaving kelp on the stairs. The poor of the Mile gather it, dry it on the roof-lines, grind it, and bake it into a grey, close-crumbed, salty bread that keeps for a week and tastes of low tide. Kelp-bread is the commonest loaf on the coast. It is also, the Salt Assay's leaked reports suggest, made of a sea that is not there: the indoor tide is fed from somewhere, and the kelp it leaves grows nowhere in the shallows. Brenna Kelp, the Salt Assayer, took her surname from her grandmother's bakery, and has not eaten the bread since she counted the buckets.

**Rules.** Routine 10; 3 c a loaf. No Taint to Brinehollowers; Taint 1 Regrowth (Dromm) to others, who start, after a week of it, to taste salt on waking. On a cook's **Lack**, the cellar tide was gathered from below the fourth step, and the bread is still wet in the middle, and moves a little, and the person who bakes it hears their own name called from the cellar that night.

**Hook.** A bakery on the Raw Mile has begun to sell kelp-bread that never goes stale. The baker's cellar has no tide any more: her cellar water stopped coming up in Plenty. But she bakes every morning, and her kelp is fresh, and she will not say where she gets it, and her son, who was Called last year, has not been seen to come back, but someone has been leaving the kelp on her step.

Makes 1 loaf. Time: 15 minutes, 40 minutes baking. Contains gluten, dairy. Soda bread needs no yeast and no rising: mix, shape, bake.
- 450 g / 3¾ cups plain flour (or half plain, half wholemeal)
- 1 tsp bicarbonate of soda and 1 tsp salt
- 2 sheets of dried nori (sushi seaweed), snipped into small pieces with scissors, or 2 tbsp dried seaweed flakes
- 100 g / 1 cup mature cheddar, grated
- 400 ml / 1⅔ cups buttermilk (or milk with 1 tbsp lemon juice stirred in and left 10 minutes)
Heat the oven to 200°C / 400°F / gas 6 and dust a baking tray with flour. Mix the flour, soda, salt, seaweed and three-quarters of the cheese in a bowl. Make a well, pour in most of the buttermilk, and mix quickly with a fork to a soft, slightly sticky dough, adding the rest of the buttermilk if dry.
Tip onto a floured surface and shape into a round with floured hands; do not knead. Set it on the tray, cut a deep cross in the top, and scatter the rest of the cheese. Bake 35–40 minutes until it sounds hollow when tapped underneath. Eat the same day, with salted butter, cold, as is proper.

### Cold Gull with Kelp Relish

[fiction]
*The Lashings' way with gull, told to a Rim cook by Tolly Brack, the Bailer Boy, eleven, while he bailed the cook's landlady.*
You salt it a night. Then you roast it and then you let it go cold, cold like the water, and you eat it with the green stuff. My gran says gulls used to follow the boats. Now they follow the bodies. The ones on the flats. That's why they're so fat. She says don't think about that, Tolly, just eat it. I don't. I just eat it. Cough now, missus. Bit more. There. That's blue. You've got a while yet.
[/fiction]

**Lore.** Gulls are the meat of the Lashings, where the pier-poor live roped to the pilings. The dead of Brinehollow, those who die of ordinary causes, are laid face-down on the Uncovered, weighted with stones, for a tide that no longer comes; the bodies lie in long rows on the salt flats, and the gulls are fat. Everyone on the Mile knows what the gulls have been eating. Gull is salted overnight in bail-water, roasted, and eaten cold with a sharp green relish of kelp, sorrel and vinegar. It is the cheapest meat on the coast and the Lashings eat it every day, and the custom, when a gull is served, is to say the name of someone laid out on the flats, so that it is at least a kind of visit.

**Rules.** Routine 10; 3 c. Taint 1 Dread to a diner who knows what the gulls eat (Dread 1 check, once). A Brinehollower who says a dead name over the gull and eats it may, that night, dream one memory of that person. On a **Lack**, the gull was taken from the flats near the Longslab, where gulls will not land, and so it was not a gull.

**Hook.** The rows of the dead on the Uncovered flats have been counted by the Lashings' gull-catchers for a century, because the catchers set their snares among them. This season there are more bodies than burials. The extras are face-up. They are Tenanted, and they are not dead, and they are lying very still among the dead, waiting for the tide.

Serves 4–6. Time: 30 minutes active, overnight brining, 1½ hours roasting, plus cooling. Contains nothing common beyond the chicken; check stock for allergens if using.
- 1 whole chicken, about 1.6 kg / 3½ lb
- for the brine: 2 litres / 8 cups cold water, 120 g / ½ cup salt, 2 tbsp sugar, 2 bay leaves, 1 tsp peppercorns
- 1 tbsp olive oil

### Uncovered Cockles

[fiction]
*From the Lantern board, Mile Nine, Carving 641.*
Uncovered cockles, a bowl, raw (bailers only; dry folk ask for the pot) — 1 c.
*(Under it, a note in a Rim Road auditor's hand, from the margin of Reeve Calder Stokes's expense-book:)* Ordered the pot, as advised. Very good. Cider, onion, a little cream. The bailers at the next bench ate theirs raw from the shell and watched me eat mine cooked, and one of them said to the others, quite kindly, "He'll be on raw by Lack." This morning I tasted salt.
[/fiction]

**Lore.** The cockles dug from the drying Uncovered are safe raw for anyone who bails, and poison to the dry: so the inns of the Mile serve two bowls, raw for the coast and stewed in cider for foreigners, and the change from the pot to the raw is how Mile folk know a stranger has turned. Cockle-diggers say the shells are older than the Gorging and should be dead. They are not. They have been waiting under the mud for six centuries for the sea to come back, and when they are opened they are always, always facing west.

**Rules.** Routine 10; 1 c. Raw: Taint 1 Regrowth (Dromm) to any non-Brinehollower, who must also make a Gut + Feast roll (Hard 14) or be sick for a day. Cooked: safe, no Taint. A character who orders raw without being a bailer is assumed by everyone on the Mile to be Called already. On a **Lack**, one shell has a pearl, and the pearl is an eye.

**Hook.** Reeve Calder Stokes, the Company auditor with dry lungs, has been in Lowmark three months and this morning tasted salt. Tonight he has ordered the cockles raw. He asks the party to sit with him while he eats them and to tell him, honestly, whether he is still himself afterward. He has written his own name on a slip of paper to compare the hand.

### Knot-Rolls for the Knotting

[fiction]
*From a Lashings household book, the receipt for the Knotting, in early Grace, when every rope in the house is replaced.*
Make the dough the night before. On the morning, every rope in the house comes down, and the new ones go up, and while the men splice, the children tie the bread: one roll for every knot in the house, tied in the same knot as the rope it stands for, bowline for the bed, clove hitch for the cistern-ring, hangman's for nothing, never, not in this house. Salt on top. Bake. Eat them all by dusk, every knot, so the new ropes hold. If a roll comes out of the oven untied, someone sleeps in the middle of the bed that year, between two others.
[/fiction]

**Lore.** Brinehollowers sleep roped, chained or weighted to the bed, because on certain nights a person will stand up, walk to the shore, and keep walking. Every household replaces every rope at the Knotting, in early Grace, and the feast-bread of the day is a salted roll tied in the same knot as each rope in the house. Children learn their knots on bread before they learn them on hemp. A roll that unties in the oven is the worst of omens; families who see it tie the person the rope belongs to with a second rope that year, and some of them still lose that person, and keep the untied roll, dried hard as wood, in the rope-box by the bed.

**Rules.** Routine 10 (Hard 14 for the full set of a large household's knots). No Taint. A character who eats the knot-roll for their own rope at the Knotting gains +1 to resist the Calling for the season. On a **Lack**, one roll unties: the Carver notes whose, and the next Calling of the campaign will come for that character first.

**Hook.** At the Knotting on the Middle Mile, a widow has baked a knot-roll for her Called husband's rope, though his rope was cut and shared out at his rope funeral two years ago. The roll came out of the oven tied in a knot nobody on the Mile can name. It is the knot on the anchor-cable of a ship that has stood on props for forty years, and the knot is new.

Makes 12 rolls. Time: 30 minutes active, 1½ hours rising, 20 minutes baking. Contains gluten, dairy, egg.
- 500 g / 4 cups strong white bread flour
- 7 g / 1 sachet fast-action dried yeast, 1 tsp salt, 1 tbsp sugar
- 300 ml / 1¼ cups warm milk (or half milk, half water)
