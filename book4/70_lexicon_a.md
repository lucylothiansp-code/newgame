# The Lexicon of the Table: A–K

> A word is a portion. Before you use it, ask which part of the meal it came from, and who was left with the bones.
>> — Oswy Larkspur, Cutwright, Keeper of the Word-Stacks at Quire, preface to the Ninth Recension of the *Dictionary of Table-speech*, 641 A.G.

[sigil: rim]

## On Table-speech

Every people on the Table speaks one tongue and swears it speaks seven. Both claims are true. Table-speech is older than the Gorging, older than the Lack, older than the carvings with the eighth place chiselled out; the tally-priests of the Bone Dole wrote their lists in it, and so, six centuries later, does the factor who chalks the price of rope at Gristmoot. A fen porter and a canyon notary can understand one another perfectly. It is only that one of them will take an hour to say what the other refuses to say at all.

The College has kept this dictionary since 140 A.G., when the first Copyists found that they could not trace a bloodline without first knowing what its grandmothers had meant by *portion*, *board* and *company*. It has been revised nine times. Each revision is longer, because the Regrowth makes words faster than the College can file them, and each is sadder, because a language is a record of what its speakers needed to say, and lately they have needed to say some terrible things. What follows is the first half of the Ninth Recension, abridged for the road. It gives Common usage first, then the dialects, then the cant of trades, thieves, children and the dying. Where a word is not printed, that is not an oversight.

### Of the Root

Every student begins with *orr-*. From it come *orren*, to eat; *orrel*, to worship and also to give back; *orrish*, a shared meal; and *Orrum*, the Host, *the one at whose board*. In the oldest stratum of Table-speech there is no way to say *I worship* that does not also say *I eat*, nor any way to say *I eat* that does not admit you are at somebody's table. Scholars insist the words always shared a root. The Purgation insists this is the whole problem. The College insists on nothing, in print, and notes only that the root turns up in places it has no business being, as the reader will find under *abhorr*, *corrish* and *horror*, and that it is pronounced at the back of the throat, rolled, the way a person clears the throat before saying grace.

### How Oatheners Conjugate

The canyon dialect has the richest verb in the language and the fewest occasions to use it. Where Common has one present tense, Oathen has five moods, each a door out of the sentence. The **Bare** is the plain statement (*I come*), used aloud only by Sayers, by the dead in Testimony, and by fools once. The **Exit** adds *-ra*, rendered in Common as *if able* (*I come-ra*). The **Intended** adds *-ent*, rendered *as I now intend*. The **Hearsay** adds *-sed*, rendered *I am told*, and is the mood of all gossip and most prices. The **Weathered** adds *-wed*, rendered *barring weather*, and covers death, illness, flood and the late Rain. They stack, outermost exit first: a canyon child says *cumerentwed*, *I come, if able, as I now intend, barring weather*, before she can say *mother*. A sentence with no exit at all is called *naked* and is treated as Common treats an obscenity. Oathen has, in addition, a **Niche** tense for speaking of the dead, who Testify; it is the past tense with every verb turned into a question (*Did she keep it? Did she not?*), so that nothing said over a body can be held to be a claim. Handtalk has no tense and no mood whatever. That is one reason it was held not to bind.

### How the Unfed Decline

The Fast dialect declines its nouns by distance from the mouth. A thing you carried yourself takes the **Carried** case, marked *-own* (*breadown*, my-own-bread, safe to eat). A thing held out to you takes the **Offered** case, which can only be spoken negated (*nay-bread*, the bread I am not taking). A thing laid by the Host takes no case at all: the Unfed drop the noun and point with the chin (*there is a — on the sill*), and children are slapped for supplying the missing word. The verb *to accept* has no first-person present. *Come in* cannot be conjugated, only uttered, and is classed by Unfed grammarians as a curse and not a phrase. And the Decline, the threefold *No, thank you*, works as grammar: the first *no* is a particle of politeness, the second an emphatic, the third a closing; an offer made a fourth time is answered in what the Fast calls, without smiling, the *coat mood*, after the proverb that the fourth no is a yes in a nicer coat.

### The Seven Tells

A trained ear can place a speaker within a sentence. The fen drawl of Sallowreach never finishes a word: final consonants are left hanging, so that *May you finish* comes out *May you finis'*, a habit the College calls the *open end*. The Fatlander talks with a full mouth, literally and otherwise: vowels rounded by chewing, everything a diminutive (*a little drop of gravy-kin*), and praise always measured in weight. The Brinehollower's voice is wet before the dawn bail and hoarse after it, and has a gargle in the back vowels called the *salt-burr*; the coast counts in fathoms even on dry land. The Vigilant speaks in long slurred runs between rouse-stammers and marks time in bells; to a Vigilant, a pause is a medical event. The highlander of Cradlewrack places a small catch, the *knock*, before any word beginning with a vowel, so as not to enter it unannounced. The Oathener is clipped, verbless where possible, and ends every sentence in an exit. The Unfed puts the negative first (*No bread, thank you, none*) and has no polite word for *please*, since asking is a form of accepting. And the Rim speaks Rimmish, a pidgin of all seven, which a factor can use to sell you rope in four dialects at once.

### How to Read an Entry

Each entry gives the headword, its part of speech and where it is used (*Common* means everywhere on the Table), and then its sense, its story where it has one, and its use in a sentence, set in italics. Cross-references point to other headwords, some of which will be found in the second half of this dictionary, L to Z. The College has added a few notes for those who must use these words in company; where a word can get you killed, the entry says so. Where a word has a dangerous older form, the entry gives the newer. Use the new one.

[pull] There are two words for *guest*. Use the new one. | — schoolroom correction, everywhere

### The Shibboleths

The College keeps a short list of words that betray a speaker's land at once, used by wardens, poachers, Reckoners and the Netwatch. A character passing as native of another land makes **Tongue + Lore** against Hard 14 whenever one comes up; a Lack means the slip is noticed by exactly the wrong person.

## A

[entry: A.G. | abbr., Common | *After Gorging*: the count of years since the Night of Seven Tables, now standing at 641. The fen says it as one sound, *ay-gee*, and the canyons will not say it at all, since a date is a kind of claim. Before 0 A.G. there are no numbered years, only the nine of the Lack, which are counted backward and named for what failed in them. *Born in four hundred A.G., dead in four hundred A.G., and still on the stoop.* See *Lack, the Long* and *Gorging*.]

[entry: abhorr | v., Common (old), Fast | To refuse a meal that has been offered to you; to turn from the board. From *ab-*, away, and *orr-*, the board. In the Fast it is a verb of praise (*she abhorred three times, and on the fourth they wed*), and in every other land it has slid into meaning *to loathe*, which the Unfed take as proof that the Godeaters cannot tell disgust from good manners. The College's etymology is disputed by the College. See *Decline, the*.]

[entry: Abide | n., Fast | An Unfed given name, from the Refusal, meaning *to wait without eating*. Like all such names it is given on the day the infant first refuses something, usually the breast, which is why many Abides are three months old before they are anybody. Diminutive *Abby*, considered a little soft. See *Abstain* and *Forbear*.]

[entry: Abstain | n., Fast | The commonest girl's name in the Fast and the commonest insult in the Fatlands, where *a right Abstain* is any woman who leaves food on her plate. In the Fast the name is held by roughly one girl in nine, so that at any meeting of the Abstinent someone must specify *Abstain-of-the-Lean-End* or *Abstain-who-wept*. *Get your elbows off the table, Abstain, there is no table.*]

[entry: Abstinent, the | n., Fast | The council of elders at Wanting who hold the Pantry and decide, by the simple act of leaving a name off a list, who will eat this winter. Members are addressed as *Elder* and never thanked, since thanks imply a gift. To be *abstinented* is Rim slang for being quietly starved by a committee, and is applied with relish to anyone denied a Company pension. See *omission* and *Pantry*.]

[entry: Admiralty | n., Brinehollow | The Tidal Admiralty: the navy that became a government, commanding four hundred warships standing on props in the mud. Hence *admiralty* (adj., Rim), describing any institution that drills daily for a world that has left it: *the Court is very admiralty about the Hushes*. A Brinehollow joke asks how many Admiralty captains it takes to sail a ship; the answer is *all of them, and they are waiting*.]

[entry: afters | n., Brinehollow, rude | The Tenanted; what comes back after the sea has had its meal. Said in the Chapel Reach rouse-houses and never on the Mile in daylight, because the afters hear very well and are perfectly courteous about it, which is worse than if they were not. *Her husband? He's afters now. Brings her tea.* See *Tenanted*, *Passenger* and *Wreckwife*.]

[entry: aftertaste | n., Common | The memory a family keeps of the Kitchen Year; the thing in the house nobody mentions. Every old family has an aftertaste, and the Cutwrights can usually tell you what it is. *They're good people, the Strakes, but there's an aftertaste.* A family that has *lost its aftertaste* has forgotten what it did, and is held to be in danger of doing it again. See *trading tables* and *Kitchen Year*.]

[entry: aftward | adv., Sallowreach | In the fen, *after death*, used of the Kept as other dialects use *later in life*: *He took up the fiddle aftward and has been learning it two hundred years*. A fen child asked what she wants to be *aftward* is being asked what trade she will follow once she has stopped breathing, which is the longer career by some centuries.]

[entry: aleuse | n., Sallowreach | (Pronounced *ALE-yoos*.) The interval in a fen tavern when the Kept patrons, who cannot drink, are brought a cup to hold for the sake of company and the living drink it for them. The custom is fond. The cups are refilled. The aleuse can last until morning, and a Kept regular in Weir Row has been holding the same pewter mug since 377.]

[entry: Already Counted | adj. phr., Vigil | Written off; as good as asleep. Originally said of a Vigilant who has been found lying down and carried to the Dormitory before their bed number is assigned; now said of any cause, love affair or ward of the Slope that has gone beyond help. *Don't bother nudging that one, he's Already Counted.* Said to a person's face, it is the cruellest thing on the Lid short of *sleep well*. See *Counted, the*.]

[entry: anchor | v. and n., Brinehollow | To sleep tied, chained or weighted to the bed so that the Calling cannot walk you to the sea; the rope, chain or weight itself. Children are anchored to a parent's wrist until seven. The great domestic tragedy of the coast is a rope cut in the night, and the great domestic comedy is the husband who comes home late and has to be anchored by a wife who is still angry. *Anchor tight, love, and I'll see you at the count.* See *Anchorite* and *rope, to take the*.]

[entry: Anchorite | n., Brinehollow | One of the order who chain themselves permanently to the cliff of the Hanging Abbey, so that the Calling can never take them. The Deepening calls them *nails*. Fishwives call any immovable old man *an anchorite*, especially at a dinner he will not leave. *Father Ebb has held fifty-one years on the same rock, and says it is not the chain that holds him.*]

[entry: and let it stay | phr., Cradlewrack | The highland half of the common grace, said after *Lack keep away* over every meal, and by the crowd at the Tally-steps when a birth is read out as *stays*. It is the most hopeful phrase in the red hills and the most frequently disappointed. See *Lack keep away* and *Questions, the Two*.]

[entry: ankle-biter | n., Cradlewrack, children's | A Part, especially a hand, that has got loose and crawls along the floor of a house at night, gripping whatever it finds. Highland children say it as other children say *bogey*; highland parents do not correct them, because it is accurate. *Feet up on the bench, there's an ankle-biter in the Gorses' scullery and it hasn't been found.* See *Part* and *Gathered*.]

[entry: Annalow | v., Rim, Fatlands | To stop a thing with one word, completely, and in a way that suggests you know what was inside it. From Mistress Brisket Annalow of the Renderers' Union, who can halt every barrel on the Rim Road by saying so. *The factor tried to raise the toll and the drovers annalowed him.* A barrel that has been *annalowed* is one nobody will now open, at any price. See *Chute*.]

[entry: annex | v., Oathen | To put a terrible fact where nobody will be required to read it aloud. From the Clausewrights' practice of placing awkward provisions in an annexed opinion, page thirty-eight or so, which the parties swear they have read and need not recite. *The bride's mother wanted the word "living" in the bed clause; the drafter annexed his reasons.* In the Galleries, to say a family *keeps an annex* is to say it has a cellar.]

[entry: Appetite, the | n., Common | The second stage of the Regrowth, between the Taste and the Course: the stage at which the body begins to want what the god wants and to do it without asking. In the fen it is the closing of lids; in the south, the eating of soil and hair; on the coast, the walking west; in the Vigil, the blink; in the highlands, the false labour; in the canyons, the compulsion to swear. *She's into her Appetite* is said of anyone with a new and embarrassing habit, and *a bit of Appetite* of anyone in love. See *Taste, the*, *Course, the* and *Want*.]

[entry: appleskin | n., Fatlands | The fine outer skin of an orchard fruit from the Bleeding Orchards, beneath which lies a layer of fat and, at the centre, a pit very like bone. As an endearment, a lover's skin: *my little appleskin*. As an insult, someone who is thin on the outside and fat within, that is, a hypocrite of the Thin. Orchard-keepers peel appleskins with their teeth and do not, as a rule, look at the pit.]

[entry: apron | n., Cradlewrack | A midwife of the Attendance, by the white apron of her office; also the Attendance as a whole. *Send for the apron* means that a birth has begun, or that someone is about to be judged, which in the highlands is the same summons. *An apron without a knife* is a midwife of the Late-faction. Children who see an apron in the lane go quiet and stand against the wall until it has passed. See *Opinion, Second* and *Attendance*.]

[entry: arithmetic, the | n., Brinehollow | The Salt Assay's forbidden finding that the volume of sea lost each year matches the number of the Tenanted. *Doing the arithmetic* on the Mile means drawing a conclusion the Admiralty has made illegal, and the phrase is said with a glance at the door. *I'm not saying, I'm just doing the arithmetic.* A clerk heard doing it aloud in an Admiralty office is reassigned to the far end of the pier.]

[entry: arrears | n. pl., Sallowreach | The parts of a Kept relative that have come off and have not yet been restitched, pickled or sunk: a finger in the button-box, an ear in the vinegar crock, a jaw on the mantel awaiting the Seamer's spring visit. Every fen house has its arrears and every fen landlord asks about them before letting a room. *Gran's arrears are in the hatbox, mind you don't sit on them.* See *Keeping* and *Seamer*.]

[entry: Ardent | n., Fast, shameful | An old Unfed surname, once one of Orrum's names, and now borne by families that would rather it were not. Welcome Ardent, who walks a mile ahead of the Host laying its tables, is the reason. To call an Unfed *ardent* in the adjectival sense, eager, is a deadly insult. Compare *Blythe* and *Welcome*.]

[entry: as I now intend | phr., Oathen | The second of the canyon exits, rendered in grammar as the Intended mood: a promise that binds only what the speaker means at the moment of speaking, so that a change of heart is not a Breaking. It does not protect against the god's reading of what one *now* intended, which is always more than one admits. *I will love you, as I now intend* is the canyon marriage vow in short, and the long form runs to forty pages. See *exit* and *If able*.]

[entry: Ashby | n., Fast | An old Unfed surname from before the Gorging, meaning *the farm by the ash trees*, and now, after Lent Ashby rose from the Host's table, a verb in the Hostwatch: *to ashby*, to get up from somewhere nobody gets up from. It is said of the impossible and never of anything good. *He ashbied out of the Lean House, and now there's a table laid wherever he sleeps.*]

[entry: ashmouth | n., Fatlands, Common | A member of the Thin, the outlawed fasters of the south, from their diet of water and ash. Fatland children are told that an ashmouth will kiss them and take their appetite away, and some of them wish one would. *Lock the larder, there's ashmouths in the hedge.* The Thin, who have no fondness for jokes, use the word of themselves with a certain bony pride.]

[entry: Ask me again | phr., Fast | The whole of an Unfed proposal of marriage, repeated over months by the proposer and answered by the beloved's threefold *No, thank you*, until the fourth asking is made and taken. Children play at it in the Cold Yards with pebbles. Used elsewhere on the Rim for any courtship that goes on too long: *Still at Ask-me-again with the cooper's widow, are you?* See *Decline, the* and *coat mood*.]

[entry: Assay, the Salt | n., Brinehollow | The Admiralty office that measures the falling sea, whose figures are a state secret. *To take the assay* of something, on the coast, is to measure exactly how bad it is and then not say. A man who visits a doctor and comes home silent has *taken his assay*. See *arithmetic, the*.]

[entry: Assembler | n., Cradlewrack | A member of the sect that collects Parts from every family in the highlands and joins them into one figure, eleven feet long, lying in a barn near Rudge. Their slogan, chalked on walls, is *Every hand in the crock is somebody's baby*. To *assemble* a thing, in highland slang, is to make a whole of what should have been left in pieces: a marriage, an argument, a story. *Don't assemble it, Nan, let it lie.* See *Barn, the* and *Gathered*.]

[entry: Attend | v., Cradlewrack | To be present at a birth as a midwife of the Attendance, with the right and duty of asking the Two Questions. The Attendance's single vow is *I will go into the room*; nobody has been known to break it, though many have died keeping it. *To attend* a matter is to settle it finally and without appeal. *I'll attend to your brother* is a threat in the red hills, and is meant as one. See *apron*.]

[entry: Attendance, the | n., Cradlewrack | The order of midwives who rule the highlands: judges, physicians, priests and executioners in one apron. The Attendance has never called itself a government, which is why it has never fallen. See *Attend*, *Opinion, Second* and *Lying*.]

[entry: at the window | phr., Vigil | Gone somewhere that is not the room and not the street, for a whole watch, upright, eyes open, pinching one's own wrist in a steady rhythm; returned with nothing but the bruise and a smell of clean linen. It is not sleep. The Wakers call it malingering; the Hourkeeper believes it is where the lengthening hours are going. *Where's Mam? At the window again.* See *blink* and *Seeming*.]

[entry: auditor | n., Rim | A Company assessor sent to price a loss. *To be audited*, in Bonded Hands' slang, is to be costed by the Company and found more valuable as something else: a Bonded Hand who stole a platter and was *audited* is now on display at Treaty Stair. Reeve Calder Stokes, sent to price Brinehollow, is the Rim's best-known auditor; the coast has begun to say he is *being audited himself*, since he tasted salt.]

[entry: aye-cough | n., Brinehollow | A short dry cough given in place of an answer at the morning count, when a neighbour calls a name through the wall and the one called has not yet bailed. It means *still here, can't speak*. Silence means someone was Called. Two coughs means *still here, but come round*. A widow on the Mile can tell her whole street's health by its aye-coughs. See *count, the*.]

[entry: Aspic, in | phr., Fatlands, Rim | Displayed, preserved, humiliated and still blinking. From Lady Aspic Dellamore's gallery of rivals, set in clear jelly, garnished, and awake. *The Board has the old Chairman's son in aspic: a title, a window seat, and nobody listening.* A Sated hostess who seats you facing the room where everyone can see you eat has *put you in aspic*.]

[entry: awake-and-sworn | adj. phr., Vigil | The formula with which every Vigil contract opens, certifying that the party was not in a blink when they signed. Contracts lacking it may be voided by anyone who can produce a blink-book entry for that bell. Hence, in common speech, *genuinely meant*: *I love you, awake-and-sworn*. See *blink-book*.]

[entry: axe-morning | n., Fast | The dawn after Tablenight, when every Unfed household turns from the long cold standing, goes in, and breaks the table that has been laid in the house overnight. Every Unfed house owns an axe for no other purpose; it hangs by the door. Axe-morning is a day of quiet exhaustion and sweeping, of carrying out the splinters and the untouched food, and of counting who is not standing in the yard. Those not standing are usually found indoors, at the table, very happy.]

[entry: abed | adv., Vigil, obscene | Lying down. In every other dialect *abed* means asleep or ill; on the Lid it is too filthy for the bell-sheets, and the Ordinances spell it *a—d*. Lovers whisper it. Wakers write it on charge sheets in red. *Caught abed* is the Vigil's equivalent of *caught in the act*, and covers the same territory, and worse.]

[entry: Ambrose's beard | n., Cradlewrack | Anything that should get smaller and will not, or anything that is getting smaller and should not; from Ambrose, the Eldest Infant, born twelve years ago with a white beard and growing younger. *The interval is Ambrose's beard* means that the pangs are shortening. Highland mothers say it, gently, of a child who is learning too fast.]

[entry: Archprovost | n., College | The head of the Cutwrights' College, keeper of the Master Concordance at Quire; there is only one, and she bears no mark of office because she is known. The present Archprovost has had the extra chair removed from every room in Quire, which junior Copyists are forbidden to remark upon and remark upon constantly.]

[entry: ague-salt | n., Brinehollow | Salt boiled from the bail-water of the Called on their last night ashore, believed to carry the pull and sold to the lovesick to make a sweetheart follow them. It does not make a sweetheart follow. It makes them stand at a west window. Illegal, cheap, and found in most wedding chests on the Mile.]

[entry: aleway | n., Rim | The gutter that runs down the middle of the floor of the Groaning Board and every Fatlands-style common room, carrying grease, spilt gravy and worse to the drain. *Gone down the aleway* means wasted, ruined, or rolled out of the inn at closing by six men. See *Groaning Board*.]

[entry: Aubrey's kick | n., Cradlewrack | A decision made by someone who cannot be seen, interpreted by someone who is tired. From Queen Dowager Sibyl Aubrey, who has carried the King Within for forty-four years and reports his kicks to the court: one for yes. Any order from on high whose source is doubtful: *Is that the Attendance's ruling, or is it Aubrey's kick?*]

[entry: abundance | n., Fatlands | A word the south uses for a field in which somebody's grandmother has been Seated, since the crop is always heavy there. *We've an abundance on the north slope* means the family has lost its matriarch to the ground and gained a very good acre. The proper response is congratulation, and a small cake.]

[entry: assent-bone | n., Oathen | A knucklebone carried by the canyon poor and laid on a table in place of saying *yes* to a bargain, since a bone set down is held not to bind. Merchants of the Felt Market accept it. The Bench has never ruled on it, and since handtalk began to break, the price of a good assent-bone has doubled.]

## B

[entry: bad day's wage | n. phr., Rim | A human thigh-bone, among the coin-cutters of the Cure-House at Saltbridge, who get nineteen lacks from a good one. The phrase has crept out of the sheds into Rim speech for any profit that came out of someone: *He inherited the farm, poor lad, a bad day's wage.* See *bone-grain* and *shut-mouth*.]

[entry: bail | v. and n., Brinehollow | To cough up, at dawn and dusk, the salt water that fills a Brinehollower's lungs from nowhere; the act and the water both. Children are taught to bail before they read. A good bail is a long wet heave over the bucket followed by a ragged first breath that tastes of the open sea. *Bail first, talk after* is the coast's answer to every question asked before sunrise. Figuratively, to confess: *Go on, bail it, what did you do?* See *bail-bucket*, *dry man* and *land-drowned*.]

[entry: bail-bucket | n., Brinehollow | The small tin or tarred-leather pail every Brinehollower carries at the waist-rope for bailing. Losing one is like losing your shoes. Sweethearts exchange them; to give a girl your bucket is to propose, and to take it back is the end. *Bucketing*, the confiscation of an offender's bucket for a day and a night, is the coast's punishment for slander, and a bucketed woman crawling door to door at dusk, gargling, with her hands out, is a sight every child on the Mile has seen.]

[entry: bail-token | n., Brinehollow | A lead disc stamped with a bucket, given by the parishes to those who bail the infirm, good for a cup of sweet water at an Admiralty cistern. Small change on the coast. *Not worth a bail-token* is said of a man who will not get out of bed to help his neighbour cough.]

[entry: Bait, the | n., Common | The third and illegal doctrine: that the gods wanted to be eaten, that the Lack was theirs, and that this is how gods breed, by being swallowed by a people and growing back through ten thousand bodies instead of one. Outlawed in four lands and gaining ground in all seven. On the coast it is called *the Spawning*. Its believers are *Baiters* to their enemies and nothing at all to themselves. See *Theft, the* and *Gift, the*.]

[entry: Baiter | n., Common, rude | A believer in the Bait. Also, in Fatlands kitchens, a cook who seasons too well, as if to make the eater want more than is good for them. The College, which is dominated by quiet Baiters, does not include itself in the definition. *Don't let the Baiters at the Weeping Post hear you say "seed."*]

[entry: bark soup | n., archaic | The food of the fifth and sixth years of the Lack, made of boiled inner bark, leather and belt. In the oldest songs it is the dish that grandmothers pushed across the table to the youngest child before they died. *To push the bark soup* means to give up one's share so that someone younger may live, and is said in four lands with a particular grief. See *Kitchen Year* and *eldest, to walk the*.]

[entry: Barn, the | n., Cradlewrack | The Assemblers' Barn near Rudge: guarded, lamplit, warm, and long, where four hundred Parts from four hundred families have been fitted into one figure that breathes. Visitors are not shown the far end. *The far end of the barn* is highland slang for the place in any story that the teller will not go. *What happened to your first husband, Marta? Far end of the barn.* See *Assembler*.]

[entry: Barren | n., Cradlewrack | A member of the Barren Order, who have had themselves cut so they can bear nothing, and preach that the kindest thing a person can do is start nothing. Their numbers grow with every pang. *To go Barren* is to give up on a project, a love affair or a grudge before it can begin. Highland mothers say a quiet child *has a Barren look*, and mean it fondly, and are frightened.]

[entry: Barrows, the | n., Fast | The long chalk mounds on the hill at Hallowboard where those who starved at the Refusal lie, nine in ten of a whole people. The Unfed swear on them: *By the ones who stood*. A serious oath is sworn barefoot, standing in one's own ancestor's footprint in the trench. See *ones who stood, by the*.]

[entry: Bed One | n., Vigil | The first bed in the Dormitory, in which Aveline Morne, a baker's daughter, lay down on the First Night of 341 and did not rise, and whose lips have lately begun to shape words. *Since Bed One* means *forever* on the Lid, and *to be Bed One* is to be the first to fall in any disaster. *Who'll be Bed One?* is the question asked at the start of a hard night shift, half joke. See *Counted, the* and *Dormitory*.]

[entry: Bedded, the | n., Vigil | The heretics who preach that sleep is a homecoming, that the eye in the long room is kind, and that the city is three hundred years late. Their prophet looks rested, which on the Lid is the most suspicious thing a face can do. *Don't talk Bedded at me* means *don't tell me it'll be all right*. See *Lullaby, the*.]

[entry: Belch, the | n., Fatlands | The hot wind of Plenty that comes up from the south smelling of rendering. Belch days are good for curing and bad for marriages. A Fatland child born on a Belch day is said to be *Belch-born*: loud, hungry and impossible to embarrass. See *Marbling* and *drip*.]

[entry: bell | n., Vigil | The Vigil's unit of time, a quarter-hour struck by the city's bells to keep it awake, and by extension any measure of a life on the Lid. Slope children are named for the bell they were born at: Pip Eleven-Bell, Rook Quarter-Bell. *Which bell were you born at?* is the first question at a Vigil hiring, since the Ward-Sergeants hold that it tells you how tired a person will be. *What bell?* is the Vigil's *what time is it*, and the shibboleth that betrays a Vigilant abroad. See *bell-sheet* and *long bell*.]

[entry: bell-sheet | n., Vigil | The printed news of the Vigil, run off at every first bell and posted at the Bonfires: entered Seemings, Dormitory admissions, rouse prices, Breakings of the Waking Laws. A Vigilant who says *it was in the bell-sheet* means that it is officially true, whether or not it happened. See *Seeming* and *struck book*.]

[entry: bellyful | n., Fatlands | A unit of time, the interval between two of the five lawful meals, roughly a fifth of the waking day. *I'll be a bellyful* means *I'll be back before the next meal*, and *two bellyfuls late* is very late indeed. A Fatland prisoner's sentence is reckoned in bellyfuls. Abroad, Fatlanders get hopelessly lost in other lands' clocks, since nowhere else is anyone hungry on schedule.]

[entry: Bench | n., Oathen, Brinehollow | In the canyons, the Bench of Clauses, the council of Clausewrights that rules in practice under the Mute King, and whose members are *Benchers*. On the coast, the Drowned Bench, the judges of the Tenanted, whose verdicts are final and whose loyalty nobody questions aloud. *To go before the Bench* means a reckoning in either land, and in the Rim's mouth it means a lawsuit you will lose to someone who is not entirely human. See *Clausewright* and *Tenanted*.]

[entry: bench, honest | n., Rim | The long bench nearest the fire in every Company common room, where strangers of different lands are seated together by the factor's choice and not their own. It is a Rim saying that more marriages, partnerships and murders have begun on the honest bench than anywhere else on the Table. *We met on the honest bench* is how half the Rim's mixed couples explain themselves, and how most of its poisonings are explained in court.]

[entry: Bill, the | n., Common | What comes after the Long Grace: the reckoning, the gods grown back, the meal paid for. Priests of every doctrine use it; the poor use it as a shrug. *We'll all be at the Bill* means *we'll all die*, and *put it on the Bill* means *don't worry about it, it hardly matters now*. The Company, which owns all the other bills on the Table, does not care for the expression. See *Grace, the Long*.]

[entry: bit | n., Oathen, Cradlewrack | (1) In Oathen, the soft leather muzzle worn by every child from the first tooth until twelve, decorated with beads, ribbons and family marks; also the iron bit of the Appetite-struck and the gold bit of a Sayer. Old family bits, worn by six generations, taste of all of them. *Better bitted than broken.* (2) In Cradlewrack, a pet word for an unnamed infant, along with *small* and *keep*, used through the naming-year so as not to love the thing too much before it is known what it is. See *Unbitting* and *naming-year*.]

[entry: bit back | v. phr., Common | Of the god in the blood: to cost more than was meant. Said of Partaking when the Tooth comes up a single pip and the hunger rises twice. *I called on her and she bit back.* At the table, a Tooth showing 1 adds a further point of Hunger. Among the poor it has become a phrase for any help that costs more than it saves: *The moneylender bit back.* See *Tooth, the* and *Partake*.]

[entry: bite of the house | n. phr., Rim | What a guest at a Company inn takes when they leave without returning the crumb they were given at the gate with their pinch of salt. A guest who has taken a bite of the house is not let in again, at that house or, by gossip, at any other on the Rim. Figuratively, any small ingratitude that ruins a reputation for good.]

[entry: Bitterhouse | n., Vigil | The Apothecary-General's works where rouse is compounded; hence *Bitterhouse black*, the sealed state cake, and *bitterhouse* as an adjective for anything harsh and necessary. *She's very bitterhouse with the children, but they're all still up.*]

[entry: Black Draught | n., Common | The emetic of the Purgation, administered at their altars to Penitents and retched up before the congregation; whatever comes up is held to the lamps and judged. It purges nothing of the god and everything else. *To take the Draught* is Rim slang for a public humiliation endured on purpose, such as a confession in a tavern or a marriage to a Fatlands heiress.]

[entry: Black Noon | n., Sallowreach | A front of flies thick enough to put out lamps, darkening the fen in the middle of the day. Fen almanacs predict them by counting flies on the inside of a windowpane at dawn. *Black Noon on Needle Street* means a crowd so dense you cannot see your hand. Kept elders are brought in off the stoops before a Black Noon, since the green-bottles favour the newly lacquered. See *Breath, the* and *weep*.]

[entry: blade-man | n., Rim | A thief of Company platters, so called from the shoulder blade from which each platter is cut. Blade-men are hanged at the post like murderers, since the Company regards a stolen platter as a stolen oath. *Watch him, he has a blade-man's hands*: long fingers, a scarred thumb, and an eye always on the wax.]

[entry: Blank | n., Common | A person born with no godsign at all: Cut 0, a mouthful of nothing. Hunted in every land as saints, specimens or spare parts. A Blank's organs fetch a noble's ransom and their blood is said to quiet any Regrowth for a season; neither claim has been shown, and neither has slowed the trade. *Blank* is also a child's taunt for a slow learner, and a poacher's word for a good night's work. See *blank bone*, *Unfed* and *Seasoning*.]

[entry: blank bone | n., Rim | Bone coin cut from the skeletons of Blanks or the Unfed: the only lacks with no god in them at all. Collectors pay twenty times face value. They are almost always proof that someone was killed for their bones. *A blank-bone price* is an obscene price for something nobody should have sold.]

[entry: blessing | n., archaic | Godsign, in the first generations after the Gorging, when it still looked like a gift: the fen elder who could not die, the Fatland field that rose in a week. The word survives in the Upper Boards and the Gut-lines as a polite term for the Course. *His lordship's blessing has reached his knees.* The poor have stopped using it. See *godsign*.]

[entry: blink | n. and v., Vigil | A sleep of a second or two, on one's feet, mid-sentence, the mark of the Appetite in the Vigil; during a blink the sleeper is in the long room with the eye for exactly as long as they are out, and comes back having moved. *I blinked* is the Vigil's most frightening confession and its most common lie. *In a blink* means not quickly but dangerously. See *blink-book*, *blink-murder* and *room, the*.]

[entry: blink-book | n., Vigil | The small book in which a nudger records every blink of their charge, by bell, in their best hand; required by law in contracts of nudging and by the Registrar of the Seeming for every witness. A clean blink-book is the Vigil's certificate of good character. Forged ones are sold on the Slope at a crumb a page. *His blink-book's longer than his arm* is said of a man who is going down.]

[entry: blink-murder | n., Vigil | A killing done by an ordinary person during a two-second sleep, with no memory after. The Wakers have forty such cases on file and the method is the same in each. The word is never used in front of the families. Ordinary Vigilants say instead that the killer *went to the window with a knife*. See *at the window*.]

[entry: Blue in the bucket | phr., Brinehollow | Half a proverb, *blue in the bucket, rope on the bed*: when a person's bail-water comes up tinged with blue, tie them tight tonight, because the Calling is near. Bailer-boys read the colour of the water at every door they bail. A man told his water is blue is being told, kindly, to say his goodbyes. See *Called, the*.]

[entry: Blythe | n., Fast | An old surname of the heartland, once one of Orrum's names, now a family shame like *Ardent*, *Welcome* and *Mercy*. Osanna Blythe, who leads three hundred starving Fatlanders into the Fast to find the Host and sit down, has made the shame worse. To be *blythe*, in the Fast, is not to be cheerful; it is to be ready to say yes.]

[entry: Board | n., Common, Fatlands | (1) In Old Table-speech, a table, especially the table of a Provider; cognate with *orr-*, the root of eating and worship. The phrase *at whose board* gives the Host his name. (2) In the Fatlands, the Board of Plenty, the government at Sated, whose members' votes are weighed. *The Board's appetite* is the south's phrase for taxation. Compare *Hallowboard* and *Groaning Board*.]

[entry: Boarding | n., Vigil | The Vigil's death rite: the one day a corpse is permitted to lie down, flat on a board in the public way, under a Boarding Licence, so that the neighbours may come and touch its closed eyes and envy it. *Don't board me yet* is said by any Vigilant accused of looking tired. To *board-envy* is to look too long at someone else's ease. See *licence* and *lie down, to*.]

[entry: boiling | n., Cradlewrack | One of the born-grown who walks out of the steam of the Coppers, the great laundries of Kest, naked and new, having been born of a vat in the night. The laundresses' rule: give it an apron and walk it to the Crèche. *A boiling* is also highland slang for any grown man who behaves like he arrived last week, and for any idea nobody remembers having. See *born-grown* and *without bearer*.]

[entry: Bone Dole | n., Common | The seventh year of the Long Lack, when the governments of every land collected the dead and rendered them in public kitchens and gave out the meat by ration, and the first bone ration-discs were cut. It was the most orderly year of the famine. The College was born at a vat-side with a pen. *As fair as the Bone Dole* means scrupulously fair and utterly monstrous. See *lack* and *tally-priest*.]

[entry: bone-cough | n., Rim | The dry, gritty cough of the old coin-cutters of Saltbridge, whose lungs are full of the fine grey flour of sawn bone. The cutters say you can tell what a man cut by what he coughs. *Grandad's got the bone-cough* explains a family's comfortable circumstances and its silence on certain subjects.]

[entry: bone-grain | n., Rim | The texture of a lack's face under a lamp, from which an expert can tell a cattle coin from a person coin. Learning to read it is a Grim 18 matter of Eye and Lore, and afterward the reader knows which coins in their purse were people, and spends them anyway. *I know the bone-grain on that one* means *I know where that money came from*.]

[entry: bone-lifting | n., Oathen | The theft of a witness from its niche in the Witnessing Vaults, by an oathbreaker who hopes a dead one removed can no longer Testify. It does not work. The bones Testify from wherever they are taken. The Keeners hate bone-lifters above all criminals. See *eating the witness* and *Keener*.]

[entry: boardless | adj., Fast, Common | Without a table. In the Fast, the proper condition of every decent home, where the centre of the floor is left bare and worn smooth in a ring by people walking round it. On the Rim, *boardless* means homeless and is said with pity, which the Unfed find hilarious in their own unsmiling way. *A good boardless house, swept every morning.*]

[entry: Bonfire | n., Vigil | One of the great fires burning in the squares of the Vigil, around which the city gathers to hear the bell-sheets read, to marry, and to stand together through the long watches. *Bonfire weddings* are loud, bright and short. *To have it read at the Bonfire* is to make something public beyond retraction. See *Pinching, the*.]

[entry: born-grown | adj. and n., Cradlewrack | Born as an adult, sometimes mid-stride, with no childhood and nobody to claim them; the stuff of the Foundling Companies, the state's only army. General Tibb was born six years ago as a man of thirty and has a private terror of the day he turns seven. *Born-grown* is said of any foreigner who seems to have come out of nowhere, fully opinionated. Abbreviated *g.* in the Tally. See *boiling* and *Foundling*.]

[entry: bottle-sea | n., Rim, Vigil | Brinehollow bail-water bottled and sold inland as a tonic, chiefly by Dry Jonah Skerry, and cut into Vigil rouse on the Slope as *sea-cut*, because it holds longest. Its customers stay awake. They have also started walking west, four hundred miles from any coast. *Bottle-sea in the blood* is the Vigil's newest phrase for a neighbour who stands at the window facing the wrong way.]

[entry: bowshot | n., Rim | The width of the Company's jurisdiction on either side of the Rim Road, as granted by the Ring Charter of 71: within a bowshot of the road, no land's law runs, only the Company's three articles. *Within a bowshot* means *on neutral ground*; *a bowshot off* means *anything goes*. The Ditch Toll is charged on anyone sleeping within a bowshot, two crumbs a night, collected at dawn by whoever finds you.]

[entry: bread without salt | n. phr., Common | A fool; someone with nothing in them to give a taste. The commonest insult on the Table after the omission of the grace. In Brinehollow it is softened to *dry bread*, since everything there is salted whether it likes it or not.]

[entry: bread-cousin | n., archaic, Fatlands | A child one was traded with in the Kitchen Year: the cousin who walked across the village one way while you walked the other, holding a stranger's hand, and passed you in the lane, and did not look up. The word survives only in the oldest Fatlands families, where it means a relative one is bound to by a debt that cannot be named. *The Strakes and the Hesks are bread-cousins* explains why two great houses never quarrel and never dine together. See *trading tables*.]

[entry: Breakdowns, the | n., Oathen | The low canyon slum on the floor of the gorge at Tacit where the Forsworn live, organized, angry and with very little left to lose. *Breakdown talk* is plain speech, without exits, of the kind only those with nothing left to break can afford. *He talks like the Breakdowns* is said of an honest man by people who mean it as a warning. See *Forsworn* and *Unread*.]

[entry: Breaking | n., Oathen | The physical result of a broken promise in Oathen: the body breaks to match the oath, at once, with a sound, aptly and never fatally. A man who swore to stand by his brother and ran had both legs fold under him; a faithless wife's chest opened like a cupboard. *The Breakings* are the cases tried before Judge Thane Urrow, where the accused simply repeats the promise aloud and the court watches. See *Forsworn* and *Weight*.]

[entry: Breath, the | n., Sallowreach | The fog over the fens, which has not lifted in living or unliving memory; warm, wet and sweet in the wrong way. A long dry wind off the southern road, a *sigh*, clears it for an hour and shows a white sky. *Lost in the Breath* means forgotten, or gone into the deep fen and still moving there a little. See *weep* and *dew-rot*.]

[entry: breath | n., Sallowreach | The smallest money of the north: a twelfth of a minute of Closing, about five lacks, issued as a stamped lead slip by the Mortuary Court. Families save breaths in a slotted crock under the hearth-board for the day they can afford to finish a grandmother. *Not worth a breath* means worthless in the strictest sense. See *licence, death-* and *minute*.]

[entry: Breath Toll | n., Rim | Five lacks a post, charged on any traveller showing godsign at the Course, since a Gut-line rooted in the road near the Chute in 604 and had to be dug around. Waived for Company officers. *Paying breath* is Rim slang for being visibly far gone, and is said of nobles behind their backs at every inn.]

[entry: brick | n. and v., Cradlewrack | The brick that seals a dead kinsman's niche in a Stillyard wall, stamped with the family mark; also, to mortar a person into a room for the night, or into the Debt Wall for life. Oaths are sworn with a hand on a kinsman's brick. *Bricked is the only shut.* A bricked couple on their wedding night are broken out at dawn, which is lucky, since nothing came in. *Who's in the Brick?* is a lane game. See *Debt Wall*, *shut door, by the* and *Unbricking*.]

[entry: Brink, the | n., Common | The penultimate stage of Regrowth, between the Course and the Seating, when the god is close enough to touch and the person is a thin skin over it. *On the Brink* is said of anyone at the end of their tether, and *Brink-mad* of those whose godsign has driven them past help. Canyon mothers of Brink-mad sons keep a stone by the door of the room, not for the door. See *Seating, the*.]

[entry: Brisket's word | n. phr., Fatlands | A word that stops a trade. See *Annalow*. In the Renderers' yards it also means a secret everybody in the Union knows and nobody repeats: *What's in the notched barrels? Brisket's word.*]

[entry: bucket-kiss | n., Brinehollow | The exchange of bail-buckets between sweethearts; a betrothal. *They've bucket-kissed* means *they're engaged*. A bucket-kiss refused is a public humiliation, and a girl who keeps two buckets on her rope is talked about on the Mile.]

[entry: burr-cloth | n., Vigil | Coarse wool woven with dried thistle-heads, worn on the Lid so that every movement pricks. The rich wear *galled silk*, laced with fine wire. *Soft as burr-cloth* is a Vigil joke, and *a burr-cloth welcome* is a hospitable one, with a pin offered at the door.]

[entry: butcher's grace | n., Fatlands | The apology a butcher says to each joint as it comes off the carcass, after the habit of Hob Gristle, the Honest Butcher, who can taste what a cut used to be, down to the name. Most butchers say it as a joke. In Sated, lately, fewer of them are laughing. *Say the butcher's grace over that, will you* is said of any meal of uncertain origin.]

[entry: by my mother's meat | oath, Fatlands | The binding oath of the south, sworn with a mouthful of a dead relative's flesh held on the tongue: *By my mother's meat, which is in me.* To break it is to have eaten your mother for nothing. Families keep a little dried meat of their elders in a salt-box under the marriage bed against the day they must swear. An oath-breaker is set an empty plate at every meal for life. See *salt-box*.]

[entry: by the line that did not hold | oath, Brinehollow | The coast's binding oath, sworn with a piece of a Called person's rope held in the fist. To break it is to invite the Called one to come back for you, which people believe literally, and sometimes they are right: the Saltwick salt-merchant found drowned in two inches of cellar water, his brother's rope round his wrist, wet footprints going down the drain. See *name-rope*.]

[entry: Bucketing | n., Brinehollow | See *bail-bucket*. The punishment of the bucketless: a day and a night begging every neighbour to bail you, door to door, gargling. Used figuratively of any public begging: *The Admiralty went bucketing to the Company for timber.*]

[entry: Burying, the | n., Fast | The first of Grace in the Fast, when families walk to the Barrows and cut the grass on them once, with knives, kneeling. It is the nearest thing the Unfed have to a festival of the dead, and nothing is eaten on the hill. See *Barrows, the*.]
