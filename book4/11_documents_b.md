# Found Pages II: More Documents of the Table

> Everyone on the Table keeps a book. Even the flies have a book now.
>> — Teal Mallory of Weir Row, in a letter to her mother on the shelf

The Table writes everything down. It writes because the gods cannot be argued with, only recorded; because a Matron must count, a Weigher must weigh, a Clausewright must quote the exact words; and because in a world where your grandmother may be a hill and your neighbour may be a candle, the only way to keep hold of anybody is on paper. This chapter collects six more of those papers, each reproduced at length as it might fall into the hands of the Guests: in a stolen bundle, at the bottom of a Company strongbox, on a stall at Gristmoot between the sausages and the rope, or folded small in a dead man's shoe.

Each document is set out the same way. A short account says what it is, who made it, where it is kept and what it costs to own. The long excerpt follows in the document's own voice. Then the Carver's notes say what the document is for at the table: the hook it hangs, the rule it carries, and the secret folded inside it that the Guests may or may not find. Every one of these pages can be handed to players as a prop. Several of them should not be, until the right moment, and one of them should be read aloud only by someone who is willing to be heard.

## Bed One's Words

[sigil: vigil]

> Two of flour to one of water, salt, the old starter, knead till it's warm as a cheek.
>> — the lips of Aveline Morne, Bed One, as transcribed by Matron Cecily Dorm, Carving 641

**What it is.** A Dormitory register-book of the ordinary pattern, octavo, bound in grey buckram gone soft at the corners, its pages starched as stiff as the Dormitory's sheets so that they crackle when turned. It is the private notebook of Matron Cecily Dorm, Keeper of the Count, and it contains everything the lips of Aveline Morne, the Sleeper in Bed One, have ever been seen to shape: not only in the month since her lips began to move in earnest, but across the whole of the three hundred years she has lain in the Dormitory, recovered by Cecily from the margins of every Matron's register since 342. It smells of starch, lye and pencil-lead, and under all three of warm bread. There is flour in the gutter of the binding. Nobody has ever put any there.

**Where it is.** At Cecily's belt, beside the bed-keys, or under her hand on the rail of Bed One. Since Carving 641 pages have been cut from it with a razor and sold in the Pinchmarket on the Slope, four or five lacks a leaf, by a thief who has learned what the names on the last pages mean and charges accordingly. Unique; not for sale; **Taint Dread 4** to read through (Dread 2 thereafter, as one gets used to things), and a reader whose name is on the last pages takes no further Dread from it, because they are past that.

**How it is set.** Cecily wrote out the old marginal shapes and the new lip-words together, as one piece, in the order and with the spacing in which they were made, and she broke it into lines the way she says she heard it: as a poem. She knew it was a recipe by the end. She did not know it at the beginning, and neither, she says, would anyone. The excerpt below is her fair copy of the whole, with her own notes in the margin.

[fiction]
BED ONE'S WORDS. A fair copy, made by me, Cecily Dorm, Matron, in the Lack of 641, from my own notes and from the registers of the Matrons before me, beginning with Matron Ysolde Harrow, who received Bed One in the Corn Granary in 342.
To whoever has this book after me. Every Matron since Harrow has kept a margin for Bed One. We were taught to: draw what you see, do not write what you think. So for three hundred years the Matrons of the Dormitory drew a little mouth in the margin whenever Aveline's lips were seen to move, and wrote the date, and the bell, and nothing else. Two hundred and twelve little mouths. Nobody ever thought they were words. They were years apart. They were breathing. They were the face settling, the way a sleeper's face will.
Thaddeus Knell reads lips. He has had to, since the bells took his ears. In Plenty of this year, before she began in earnest, I brought him the registers one at a time and we sat in the Matron's office with a lamp and he looked at the little mouths in order, and he said what each one was. He did not know why I wanted it. I did not tell him. When we had done all two hundred and twelve he would not take his fee. He said, "Matron, that is somebody talking very slowly," and went home and did not ring the next quarter, and was fined.
I have set them out here as she said them. Where a line stands alone with a date, that is one word, or two, in a whole lifetime of nights. You will see the spaces close up. I have not made them close. She has.

THE NAMES (from the tenth day of the Carving Plate)
On the tenth day, at the second bell, she said the whole of the bread again, quickly, under her breath, the way you say a thing you know by heart while you are thinking of something else. And then she said a name.

Since then there has been a name most days, at the end of the bread, where the knocking is. I have written all of them. I have put a line through each when they come in through the Gate. I have not yet had to wait more than a day to draw the line.

*Set another place.*

The lips come together at the front as if for a kiss, or for the start of the word *bread*, and then they open wide, and stay open, as if for someone to put something in.

I am going to sit with her now. I will keep counting. Fifty-eight thousand and twenty-six. Fifty-eight thousand and twenty-seven. Good night, love.

[box: Carver's Notes: Bed One's Words]
**The hook.** The stolen leaves are on sale in the Pinchmarket. Each leaf sold carries the names written on it, and the people on those leaves are still going to the room one a day, whether or not the page has been crossed through. Somebody has bought the leaf with a Guest's name on it, and is trying to sell it back. Or: the Matron hires the Guests to find the razor-thief, and the trail runs through the Slope to an orderly of Bed One's own hall, who sells the pages because he believes that a name sold is a name the room will not take. He is wrong, and he knows it now, and he has kept selling.
**The rule: reading it aloud.** Reading the Old Margins aloud, in order, inside the Vigil, takes about ten minutes. Everyone in earshot who has gone more than a day without sleep makes a blink check at Hard (14), and anyone who blinks during the reading meets the kind lady in the baker's apron at the soft end of the long room, who finishes the line they were on. Reading the bread section aloud is harmless anywhere but the Dormitory. Reading the last page aloud at the foot of Bed One adds the reader's voice to the row: for a heartbeat, every one of the Counted in the hall breathes in with them.
**The rule: the bread.** The recipe works. A Guest who bakes it, with ordinary flour, makes an ordinary, excellent loaf and can treat the bread as **a feast's worth of comfort**: everyone who shares the loaf removes 1 Fray, once per Plate. A Guest who bakes it with a pinch of the flour from Aveline's knuckles makes a loaf that never stales (Taint 2 Regrowth, Iss) and that, laid on the table at night, fills the house with the smell of a bakery at dawn; anyone who sleeps in that house sleeps very well, and anyone with the Vigil in their blood does not wake.
**The secret the Carver can reveal.** The spaces between the words are not random. Set the dates of the Old Margins against the Dormitory's count of filled beds, and they line up: each word came when the Dormitory filled by roughly the same number of sleepers, and as the beds filled faster, the words came faster. The bread is a count. Cecily has worked this out. She has also worked out that the bread has a fixed number of words, that she has now heard all of them but the last, and that the last word comes after the cooling, the hardest part of all, when the loaf is cut and shared. She has not written down what she thinks the last word is, and she will not say it aloud.
**The secret nobody knows.** The old shape Cecily could not read on the twenty-sixth day is a word older than Table-speech. A Cutwright, or Grandfather Peat in his tank at Quire, could read it. The Carver should decide what it would cost them to say it, and should not let it be said lightly.
**Real recipe.** The bread in the excerpt is a plain country sourdough and will work in any kitchen: 100 g of active starter, 500 g strong white flour, about 360 ml of warm water, 10 g of salt; rest flour and water an hour, add starter and salt, four sets of folds half an hour apart, bulk rise until half again in size, shape, proof overnight in the refrigerator, bake in a preheated lidded pot at 250°C for 30 minutes, then lid off at 220°C for 20 to 25 more. Let it cool before cutting. It really is the hardest part.
[/box]

#### What the Names Have in Common

The Matron has not seen it. Those who read the stolen leaves together, with access to the Dormitory's visiting-books, will. Every name Bed One has spoken belongs to someone who once stood at the foot of a Counted bed for an hour or more a day, for a year or more: mothers, sons, nudgers who lost their charge, a Waker captain whose nudger went down. The room is calling those who love the Counted most. A Guest who has visited a sleeper faithfully is a Guest who should be afraid of this book.

## The Rim Road Company Employee Handbook

[sigil: rim]

> Pay the toll. Keep the peace. Say the grace.
>> — the Three Articles, printed on the inside cover of every copy

**What it is.** *The Book of the Wheel: Being the Instructions of the Rim Road Company to Its Bonded Hands, Waymasters and Factors, in the Ninth Revision (619 A.G.), With the Amendments Since.* A small fat book, palm-sized, bound in oiled black calf with the seven-spoked wheel blind-stamped on the front and a brass clasp that a Hand can open one-handed while holding a lantern. Every Bonded Hand is issued one at Treaty Stair with the iron wrist-ring, and is expected to carry it on the road, to know it, and to lose it never. The paper is good Oathen rag; the print is small; the margins of most copies are full of pencilled amendments, drovers' jokes and the names of inns that water their beer.

**Where it is found.** In the coat of any Bonded Hand, living or otherwise. Secondhand copies sell at Gristmoot for 2 lacks, because travellers who know what is in it get better service at Company houses; a copy carries no Taint. Every copy issued since 619 has had its Section 14 removed, neatly, with a blade along the gutter, at Treaty Stair, before issue. The stubs remain. The table of contents still lists it.

[fiction]
THE BOOK OF THE WHEEL. NINTH REVISION.
FOREWORD, BY THE FIRST SPOKE.
You have sworn the Bond. You walked down the stair and you looked in at every window, and you saw what the Company does with a Hand who steals from it, and you walked up again with the ring on your wrist. Good. Now you belong to the road.
The road is two thousand four hundred miles of fitted stone, and you will walk most of it. You will be cold on it and wet on it and you will be offered, on it, everything that every land on the Table has to sell, and some of it will be people, and some of it will be gods. This book will not tell you what to think about any of it. The Company does not think. The Company carries. This book will tell you how to carry it, how to be paid for carrying it, and how to come back to the next post with the same number of fingers as you left the last.
Read it in order. Read it again in Lack, when the road is slow. If anything in it seems strange to you, it is because somebody died to have it written down.
O. Penhallow, First Spoke, from the carriage-house, on the road between Gristmoot and Toll Nine.
SECTION 1. THE THREE ARTICLES.
1.1. The Company has three laws, and they are recited at every post at dawn by the senior Hand present. Pay the toll. Keep the peace. Say the grace.
1.2. These three are sufficient for all cases. Where a case appears to be not covered by them, the Hand will find on reflection that it is covered by the first.
1.3. The grace is "Lack keep away." It is said at the gate and at the table. A Hand who omits it is fined one lack. A Hand who omits it at a table where there are travellers present who have also omitted it will be asked, by the Factor, at the next post, why nobody at that table said it, and is advised to have an answer.
SECTION 3. TOLLS, AND THE TAKING OF THEM.
3.1. The schedule of tolls is posted on the bone slate at every gate. The Hand will know it by heart. The Hand will not alter it, discount it, or explain it.
3.2. Tolls are taken in lacks, crumbs or Company platters. Sworn notes of Oathen are accepted at the Factor's discretion and at a discount the Factor will set. Fatlands meat-scrip is not accepted. Vigil rouse-tabs are not accepted, however many are offered, however tired the Hand.
3.3. Every lack is to be weighed in the palm and turned once before it is taken. A lack that is warm on one side is to be accepted and not remarked upon. A lack that turns in the palm, by itself, toward a land, is to be accepted, and passed to the Factor at day's end in the separate pouch.
3.4. The Foot Toll is waived for children under seven. The Hand will not ask a child's age. The Hand will look at the child and decide. In Cradlewrack country the Hand will be wrong in both directions and is not to be disciplined for it.
3.5. The Hush Toll is charged on corpses, by the body. The Kept of Sallowreach are not corpses and are charged as Foot, once, at Fogmouth, after which they are not charged again, because they are no longer travelling. See Section 9.2.
3.6. The Breath Toll is charged on any traveller showing godsign at the Course. The Hand is not required to determine what counts as the Course. If the Hand had to look twice, it counts.
3.7. Of the Cage Toll. Live cargo in a closed cart is charged three lacks a post and is entered as livestock. The Hand does not open the cart. The Hand does not knock on the cart. If something in the cart knocks, the Hand does not knock back, and enters it as livestock.
3.8. The Seal is charged on nothing. A courier under an Oathen oath is to be passed through at once, by night or day, and fed standing, and not delayed for any cause, including the Hand's own death; see Section 13, Item 11.
3.9. The Ditch Toll is collected at dawn from travellers found sleeping within a bowshot of the road. If the traveller cannot be woken, the Hand will collect it from the traveller's purse, count it aloud, and leave a chit. If the traveller has been sleeping on the inner side of the Hem, the Hand will not step over the Hem to collect it. The Company will absorb the loss.
SECTION 5. DRESS AND BEARING.
5.1. On duty the Hand wears the road-coat, grey, to the knee, with the wheel in white on the left breast and the post number in white on the right. The coat is to be clean at the gate. It is not expected to be clean anywhere else.
5.2. The iron wrist-ring is worn on the left wrist and shown when asked. It is not to be covered, polished, engraved, wrapped in cloth against the cold, or removed. It cannot be removed. Hands who have attempted it are in the window at Treaty Stair, second gallery, fourth from the top.
5.3. Waymasters carry the brass whistle on a cord and the caravan's slate on a strap. Factors wear the gate-keys at the belt and the platter-seal on a chain. Toll-Lords carry the bone-shod staff. No Hand shall carry another rank's mark for any purpose, including a joke.
5.4. Land dress. When working a post within a land, the Hand will observe the following, in addition to the coat:
5.4.1. In Sallowreach, a phial of vinegar or cedar oil at the collar. The Hand who smells of nothing will be taken for a Closer and will not be served.
5.4.2. In the Fatlands, no belt. The coat is to be worn open or tied with a sash. A belt on a thin Hand will be taken for a mark of the Thin, and the Bailiffs will take the Hand to the Gavage, and the Company's protest will arrive afterward.
5.4.3. In Brinehollow, a bail-bucket, carried on the hip, whether or not the Hand needs it. A Hand without a bucket will be thought to have been Called and come back, and will be asked where he went, which in Brinehollow is a grave rudeness, and a fight will follow.
5.4.4. In the Vigil, a pin in the lapel, offered point-out to any guest on arrival, and the Hand's own nudger-cord, worn at the wrist, so that a Waker who finds the Hand drowsy knows whom to fine.
5.4.5. In Cradlewrack, the knuckles of the right hand to be kept bare, for knocking.
5.4.6. In Oathen, the card of safe phrases, and the slate. A Hand who speaks aloud in Oathen does so on his own account. The Company will not stand surety for any Hand's mouth.
5.4.7. In the Fast, nothing that smells of food. The Hand will eat before he arrives, and will wash his hands and his beard.
5.5. A Hand will not wear a glove at the gate. Travellers must see the Hand's palms when he takes their coin, so that they know the Hand is not a Closer, nor anything else with hands that should not be touched.
SECTION 7. CONDUCT IN THE COMMON ROOM.
7.1. The common room belongs to the Company. The guests are permitted in it.
7.2. A guest arriving after dark is met at the gate with salt in the palm and one crumb. The salt is licked. The crumb is kept and returned on leaving. A guest who leaves without returning the crumb has taken a bite of the house and is not to be let in again, at any post, for any price. Their name is posted on the slate. Do not argue about this. The crumbs are counted.
7.3. The honest bench is nearest the fire and is filled by the Factor's choice. The Hand will seat strangers of different lands side by side. The Hand will not seat a Reckoner beside a Hand-line, nor an Unfed beside a Fatlander who is eating, nor anyone at all beside a Tenanted who has asked to sit with his back to the west, unless the Factor directs it, in which case the Factor has a reason and the Hand does not need it.
7.4. The Night Count. At dusk and at dawn the senior Hand walks the house with a lamp and counts heads aloud, every room, every bed, the stables, the privy and the roof, and enters both numbers in the ledger. If the dawn count is lower than the dusk count, the wardens are called. If it is higher, see Section 14.
7.5. The head of the table is the Company's chair. Nobody sits in it. Not a Spoke. Not the Factor. Not a guest of any rank who asks. It is to be dusted daily, and laid at every meal with a clean plate and a cup, which are to be cleared unused and washed with the others.
7.6. No Hand is to be drunk on duty. No Hand is to be sober at a Fatlands wake to which he has been invited. No Hand is to accept a meal from a Fast traveller, because none will be offered; if one is offered, see Section 11.
7.7. Fights. The Hand will separate fighters with the pole and not the hands. A guest who draws a blade in the common room is to be disarmed, held, and given to the wardens, who will hang him at the post before the blood is dry, as the Charter allows. The Hand will then mop the floor. The Hand will say the grace over the mop-bucket, as over any meal, because it is the Company's custom, and because nobody now remembers why, and because the one Factor who stopped doing it is not with us.
SECTION 9. THE DEAD, THE DROWNED, AND OTHER TRAVELLERS.
9.1. The Company carries everyone who pays.
9.2. The Kept of Sallowreach may travel the northern stretch as far as the Drop at Fogmouth and no further. A Kept traveller who insists on crossing the line is to be warned three times, in front of a witness, and then permitted. The Hand will then collect the body, charge the Hush Toll to the estate, and enter the departure in the ledger as *finished, by choice*. The Hand is reminded that the family will often be watching from the other side of the line.
9.3. A Brinehollow guest who rises in the night and walks west is to be stopped at the gate by any means short of a blade, roped, and returned to his bed. The Company keeps rope rooms at every western post at a crumb a knot. A guest who has paid for knots and been let walk is entitled to a refund, payable to the next of kin.
9.4. A Vigil guest who falls asleep in a Company house is not to be woken by force. He will not wake. He is to be carried, gently, to the Sleeping Sheds or the nearest Vigil-bound cart, with his papers on his chest, and his room charged to the Watch of Watches, which pays these bills promptly and without comment.
9.5. A guest who goes into labour at a Company house is to be given a room, a midwife if one can be had, and a Hand with a cleaver at the door, facing in. The Hand is not to use the cleaver unless the midwife tells him to. The Hand is not to argue with the midwife.
9.6. An Oathen guest who has promised something in the common room is to be helped to keep it.
SECTION 11. IF A TABLE APPEARS IN THE COMMON ROOM.
11.1. It will be found laid. It will be warm. There will be a smell of bread, and of roasting, and of something sweet and brown that the Hand will remember from a kitchen he was in as a child. It will not have been there at the last count. The Hand will know it is not one of the Company's tables, because it will be better.
11.2. The Hand will not sit.
11.3. The Hand will not thank it. If a chair has been pulled out, the Hand will not thank the chair, nor push it in. The Hand will not say *come in*, or *after you*, or *please*, or *what a spread*, or any other kindness. The Hand is advised to say nothing at all, and to walk backward from the table to the door without turning round.
11.4. The senior Hand will ring the gate-bell three times, slowly, and then three times quickly. This is the table-call. On hearing it, every Hand in the house goes to the guest-rooms and keeps the guests in them. Guests who wish to come down and look are to be told that there has been a fire. In a sense there has.
11.5. The Factor, or the senior Hand if the Factor is absent, will take the axe from the hooks behind the bar, which every Company house keeps for this purpose and for no other, and will break the table. Four blows: a leg, a leg, the board across the middle, the head. He will not taste anything that falls from it. He will not look at the place cards.
11.6. If there are place cards, the Hand will not read them. If the Hand has read them, the Hand is to report it to the Factor, who will relieve him of duty at that post and move him on by the next cart, in the opposite direction from the one he was travelling. The Company does not inquire whose name was on the card. The Company has found that it does not need to.
11.7. The broken table is carried out to the yard and burned the same night, with salt, to ash. The ash is swept into the ditch on the outer side of the road. Not the inner side. Never the Hem side.
11.8. If a guest has already sat, the guest is not to be pulled from the chair. The Hand will not try. Those who have tried are entered in Section 13, Item 9. The table is not to be broken while a guest is at it. The guest is to be screened from the room with blankets, and fed nothing, and the room closed. The guest's account is to be settled from the guest's purse to the end of that day, and closed, and the guest entered in the ledger as *departed*. The guest will thank you. The Hand will not reply.
11.9. The Hand is reminded that the guest is happy, and that this is not the Hand's fault.
11.10. A Fast traveller in the house at the time of the appearance will know what to do better than any Hand, and the Hand should defer to them, and if necessary, to their axe.
SECTION 12. NIGHTS OF THE LICKED-CLEAN PLATE.
12.1. On the night of the new moon no guest leaves the common room after the last bell. Chamber-pots will be provided. The Hand will empty them in the morning without complaint.
12.2. A guest who insists on going out is to be let out, and the door barred behind him. He will be let in at dawn if he knocks in the right voice. The Hand on the door is the judge of whether the voice is right. The Company will support the Hand's judgement in every case, both ways.
12.3. The Hand on the door will not answer a knock twice. The first knock may be the guest. The second knock is not.
12.4. On Tablenight the gates close at dusk, the extra chair is set at every table, turned to face the wall, and nobody in the house says *come in* to anyone, for any reason, until dawn. This includes Hands to one another, sweethearts to sweethearts, and mothers to children. A child who must be brought in from the yard after dark on Tablenight is to be carried in, without a word, and the door shut behind with the heel.

**The hook.** A Guest who is a Bonded Hand already has a copy; a Guest who is not can buy one at Gristmoot and use it, with a Hard (14) Eye + Lore roll, to pass for a Hand at a Company post for a night. Copies older than 619 still have Section 14 in them, and are therefore worth a great deal to several people: the Wheel, which has been buying them up quietly for twenty years at 300 lacks each and burning them at Toll Nine; the Auditor-General, Lysander Coyle, who wants one for reasons of his own; and the Second Table, whose agents on the Rim have been told that Section 14 contains "the Company's terms of service to the thing that pays it." A drover's widow at Thimble Cross has an Eighth Revision copy in her late husband's sea-chest, and does not know what it is worth.
**The rule.** Following the Book exactly is good protection. A Guest working a Company house who has read the Book and follows Section 11 to the letter when a table appears gains +2 on every Caul + Resolve roll made against the table's pull for that night, and nobody in the house is lost to it unless they choose to sit. A Hand who breaks Section 11.6 (reads a place card) finds the name on it is their own, and from that night is followed down the Rim by tables, one every Plate, each a little closer to the road.
**The secret: what Section 14 said.** The full text, as printed in every revision up to the Eighth, is reproduced below for the Carver. It does not name anyone. SECTION 14. OF THE HEAD OF THE TABLE, AND OF THE COMPANY'S PRINCIPAL. (Eighth Revision, 588 A.G.; text as sworn at Tacit, 61 A.G., under the first bond of the seven houses.)
14.1. The Chair at the head of every table in every house of the Company is kept for the Company's Principal, who is the party to whom the Company is bound before all others, and on whose account the road was built.
14.2. Whether the Principal has come is known by the Night Count. When the count at dawn is higher than the count at dusk, the Principal has been in the house.
14.3. The Principal is not to be asked a name, nor charged, nor counted on the ledger, nor turned away. The Principal is to be served from the Company's chair as from any other, with the best the house has. The Principal's account has been paid in advance, and the Company is in the Principal's debt, and not the other way round.
14.4. The ledger of the house is to be presented to the Principal, open, if the Principal asks for it, and every question the Principal puts is to be answered truly, by the Factor, in writing, in the ledger.
14.5. The page of the ledger on which the higher count is written is to be burned at noon. This is not to hide it from the Principal. It is to hide it from everyone else.
14.6. If the dawn count is higher by more than one, the Factor is to set as many more places as the count requires, and is not to ask how many more will be needed, nor for how long.
14.7. Bonded Hands are reminded that the Bond is sworn *as long as the wheel turns*. When the wheel stops turning, the road is the Principal's, and every house on it, and everything carried on it, and every Hand.
14.8. The Company will know that the wheel has stopped turning when the Principal sits in the Chair.
14.9. Until then, keep the place laid.

## Wick Tallow's Catalogue

[sigil: sallowreach]

> Steady light, sixth bell light, light that will not leave you!
>> — the chandlers' cry on Wick Lane, Lastgate

**What it is.** *Tallow's of Wick Lane: A Catalogue of Lights, With Prices, For the Carving and Lack of 641.* Sixteen pages of coarse reed-paper, stitched with waxed thread, printed by an Heirs' press in the drying-loft over Saul Brindle's tannery (Wick pays in candles), and handed out free at the shop door and at the Rim Market at Gristmoot, where Wick's man keeps a stall each season for the Vigil trade. The cover is a woodcut of the shop sign, a candle with a face. The paper is greasy at the edges, because every copy has been handled by someone who works with fat, and every copy smells, faintly and permanently, of the Lane: hot tallow, lye, singed hair.

**Where it is found.** Everywhere in Lastgate; in the Lofts, where the Wardens order by the gross; in the Vigil, where Lamplighters' agents mark it up and pass it round; folded in Rim Road saddlebags as far as Mumchance. Free. A copy carries no Taint. The candles do: a common Tallow candle is **Taint Dread 1**; a named candle is **Dread 2**; a few of the stock below are worse, and the Carver's notes say which.

TALLOW'S OF WICK LANE. ESTABLISHED 551. CHANDLERS TO THE LOFTS, THE HALL OF HANDS, THE SINKS AND THE VIGIL TRADE. LIGHTS THAT DO NOT GUTTER.
TO OUR CUSTOMERS.
Friends, here is the new list. The prices are up a crumb or two on last Lack, because the Court has started charging me for the Jar Room ends, which it used to give away, and because the Vigil cannot get enough of us, and when the Vigil cannot get enough of a thing the Lamplighters' men turn up on the Lane with a cart and buy the shop bare by noon. I will not have my neighbours going dark for the sake of a city that will not shut its eyes. So the Vigil price is the Vigil price, and the Lane price is the Lane price, and if a Vigil man tries to buy at the Lane price, my boy will know him by his eyes.
Everything on this list is made in my own vats from my own stock and lit from my own coal, which has been burning since the year 551 and has lit every candle I ever sold. Every candle is rendered three times, strained through linen, poured round a wick of Brinemoss reed-pith, and cooled slow in the cellar so it will not crack. They give a steady yellow light that does not gutter, smoke or spit, and they lean, a little, toward whoever lit them. That is not a draught. It is a selling point.
You will hear them. I will not pretend you won't. Most say nothing anyone could make out. Some say one thing, over and over, the way the Lofts do. I have written down, beside each one on the list, what it says, as best I and my journeymen can make out, so that you can choose. A customer who knows what a candle says will not bring it back.
— W. Tallow, Wick Lane, at the sign of the face.
I. COMMON LIGHTS. UNCLAIMED STOCK, BY THE CANDLE OR THE BOX.
From the Sump-sacks, the Court's unclaimed, and the Guild's leavings. I do not know their names. Nobody does. That is what unclaimed means.
**No. 1. Plain Sixth-Bell.** A hand-length taper, yellow. Burns three nights. Says: nothing you could swear to; a sort of humming. 6 crumbs; 5 lacks the box of a hundred.
**No. 2. Long Reader.** Thick, for students and clerks of the Upper Boards; doubled wick. Burns six nights. Says: *one more page*. Very popular with the Court's copyists. 1 lack.
**No. 3. Sinks Stub.** Offcuts and the bottoms of vats, pressed together. Burns two nights, a little blue at the edge. Says: several things at once, none of them to you. Not for nurseries. 3 crumbs.
**No. 4. Loft Box.** As supplied to the Wardens of the Lofts, a gross at a time; squat, so they will not tip on the ladders. Burns four nights. Says: *read to me*. The shelved like them; they say they keep each other company. 7 lacks the gross.
**No. 5. Weir Lantern Candle.** Short and fat, for a shuttered tin on the eel-traps. Burns a week in the wet. Says: *is it cold out there*. The weir-men say it is kind company at the fourth bell. 9 crumbs.
**No. 6. The Black Noon.** Wick dipped in fen-pitch; a smoky flame that flies will not cross. For fly-fronts. Burns one night. Says: a sound like someone swatting, and then sorry. 1 lack.
**No. 7. Door-Candle.** For the window by the door, at the Court's door-duty. Burns a night exactly; out at first bell. Says: *who's there*. Every house on the Boards burns one. Half the city goes to bed to the sound of its own windows asking who is there. 4 crumbs (and a crumb to the Court, which is not my doing).
II. JAR ROOM ENDS. COURT STOCK.
The rendered remains of the Court's long-sentenced, from the Jar Room, when a jar has been sat on the shelf so long there is nothing left in it that will answer to a name. The Court sold me these free until this year. They are hard candles, white, very bright. Some customers prefer them. They are bright because they are angry.
**No. 8. Penitent.** Tall, white, dead straight. Burns five nights. Says: *I didn't, I didn't, I didn't*. They all say that, the Court's clerk tells me. 2 lacks.
**No. 9. Fen Murderer.** From a man jarred in 402 for killing his brother in a punt with a pole. Burns nine nights and is the brightest thing I sell. Says: the brother's name, and *get up, get up*. Leans hard. Reading by it you will feel you are being read. 4 lacks.
**No. 10. Heir of 288.** One of a batch from the War of the Heirs, a young man who killed his father nineteen times; the father kept coming back to his desk; the son was jarred. Burns four nights. Says: *stay down*. Bought mostly by the Heirs, who light it at their meetings for luck. 3 lacks.
**No. 11. The Forger.** A clerk jarred for forging Closing licenses. Burns three nights. Says: a very soft scratching, like a nib. Two customers have told me it writes. I have not seen it. 2 lacks.
III. NAMED LIGHTS. FAMILY STOCK, ALREADY POURED.
Families who commission a named candle sometimes find, afterward, that they cannot bear to burn it, or that they owe me for the pouring and cannot pay, or that there are a great many more candles in a grandmother than anyone expects. These are from such families, sold on with their permission or their debt. I give the first name only. A name is a thing you should have to come in and ask for.
**No. 12. Ide.** A grandmother of Weir Row, a hundred and eighty years Kept, sold by the family to pay for a great-uncle's restitching. Burns a week. Says: *you have cold hands, girl*. Very fond of children; leans toward them across a room. 5 lacks.
**No. 13. Fenwick.** A carpenter of the Landing. Burns five nights. Says: *measure twice*. A customer of the Boards reads her household accounts by him and says she has never made a mistake since. 5 lacks.
**No. 14. Aldous and Merrit.** Husband and wife, poured into one candle at their request, two wicks. Burns a week; the flames lean toward each other and will not stand up straight. Says: both talking at once, quarrelling, it sounds like, for six nights, and on the seventh night, before the end, silent. 9 lacks.
**No. 15. The Small.** A child of the undercity, two hundred years seven, from one of the Drowned Storeys; given to me by Queen Nell's own runners, who said she asked to be light. One candle only. Burns three nights. Says: a counting game. *One for the lamp and two for the wick.* I will not sell her to anyone who laughs at the price. 5 lacks.
**No. 16. Josse.** An uncle, gone Ripe, beyond stitching. Burns four nights, smokes a little, cannot be helped. Says: *is it a feast day yet*. 4 lacks, reduced.
**No. 17. Mother.** That is all the family would give me. Thirty candles, one Mother. Burns three nights each. Says: *where are you going*. Sold singly or the lot. The family asks that the lot not go to one house. 5 lacks each; 120 the lot.
IV. SPECIALTIES.
**No. 18. Wedding Pair.** Two thin white tapers from one source, twisted together. Lit at the betrothal supper and pinched out at the vow, then kept. Burn: one hour, every anniversary, for as long as the marriage lasts. In Sallowreach that is long. Say: *I do, I do, I do*, softer each year. 3 lacks the pair.
**No. 19. Quease Taper.** Made to the order of Dr. Halloran Quease, of a stock he selects, for his studies. Burns two nights. Says: precisely where it hurts, in detail, and the doctor writes it down. Not for general sale; listed because people ask. Price on application to the doctor.
**No. 20. The Long Watch.** For the Vigil trade. Rendered four times, the hardest tallow I can make, a wrist thick. Burns a month and does not shorten, which I cannot explain and do not try to. Says: *don't close your eyes, don't close your eyes*. The Lamplighters buy every one. Lane price 2 lacks; Vigil price 9.
**No. 21. Nudger's Candle.** For the Vigil trade. Small, with a pin pressed into the side at the half-burn. Says: *pinch me*. When the flame reaches the pin, the pin falls, hot, wherever the candle has been set, which is meant to be the back of your hand. 6 crumbs Lane, 2 lacks Vigil.
**No. 22. Hush-Edge Candle.** For the Finishers, who walk into the silences with lights. Plain, steady. Burns until it crosses the edge of a Hush, and then it is not burning, and has never been burning, and is not even warm. Says: *thank you* on the way in. Sister Lorn's people buy them by the box and I let them have the loft free. 1 lack.
**No. 23. Seamer's Lamp-Wick.** Tallow-dipped wick by the yard, for the Guild's lamps. Says: little things, threadlike, *in and out, in and out*. The Guild buys it because a seamer working by it never misses a stitch. 2 crumbs the yard.
**No. 24. Ferry Candle.** For Tam Ruddock's clients waiting in the back room at the Long Farewell. Made from the unclaimed foreign dead, the ones who came across the Drop to not die and then did, eventually, of what they had. Burns two nights. Says: things in other dialects. Tam asks for them. I think it helps his clients to hear someone from home. 8 crumbs.
V. COMMISSIONS.
**Your own.** A named candle of your own dead, from your own family, rendered in my own vat, alone, never mixed. You bring them to the back door on Tanner's Boards before the first bell, wrapped, labelled, with the release signed by the eldest Kept of the house. My journeymen do the rest. They are gentle. You may wait in the front shop. You may not wait in the back. You collect in nine days: forty to sixty candles from an adult, depending, and a cedar box. The candle-blessing is yours to arrange. 50 lacks.
**Yourself.** A customer of the Upper Boards asked me last Plenty whether I would take her before she was finished, while she was still Kept, so that she could be the light her grandchildren read by, rather than a woman on a shelf they visit on feast days. I said I would think about it. I have thought about it. I will, for those who ask in writing, before witnesses, with the eldest of the house consenting. The vat is hot. It is very quick, and very slow, and then it is over, and then it is not over. My wife Bess asked me the same thing, in 618, and I did it myself, and I burn one of her each Tablenight, and she is the steadiest light I have ever made. 50 lacks, the same. I do not charge more for a willing customer.
VI. TERMS.
No returns on any candle that has been lit.
No returns on any candle that has not been lit but has been heard, because once you have heard it you will always hear it, and there is nothing I can do about that.
Candles that run in the heat and stand up are not to be brought back to the shop. Send a boy for me. Do not let it out of the room, and do not let it near the stove, and do not let anyone in the house call it by any name. I will come with the cold-barrel and the hook.
Candles are not to be lit in a Hush, on Tablenight by anyone not of the family, or within a bowshot of the Ferrying Post at the midnight bell, for a reason I cannot give, as I do not know it, but my journeymen will not go near the Post at midnight with a lit candle and I have stopped asking them to.
Snapped candles burn as two. Both halves lean the same way. Both halves say the same thing, out of time with each other, which is worse. Do not snap your candles.
Steady light. Sixth bell light. Light that will not leave you.

**The hooks.** The catalogue is a shopping list for the Guests and a map of Lastgate's quieter griefs. *No. 17, Mother*: thirty candles, sold singly to thirty houses, each of which now hears *where are you going* from its window, and the family that sold her would like her collected again, every candle, before the last one burns down; three are already in the Vigil. *No. 9, the Fen Murderer*: a buyer on the Upper Boards has died, properly died, in a house where no one is supposed to be able to, with his chair pulled up close to the candle and his book open; the candle was saying *get up* and he did not. *Commissions, Yourself*: a Kept Guest, or a Kept companion, may find a relative has written to Wick Lane on their behalf.
**The rules.** A Tallow light used to read, work or watch by gives +1 to Eye rolls made by its light for long tasks, because it does not gutter and it does not let you stop. *No. 2, Long Reader* raises that to +2 and its user cannot willingly end the task before the candle is out (Caul + Resolve, Hard 14, to put it down). *No. 21, Nudger's Candle* lets a Vigil Guest pass one Weariness check automatically per candle. *No. 22, Hush-Edge* is the only light that will tell you, by going out and never having been lit, exactly where a Hush begins; it is Taint Dread 2. *No. 9* and *No. 19* are Taint Dread 3. A candle that runs and stands is a **Tallow-Saint**: treat it as a man-sized Kept thing of soft warm fat (Threat 3 · Flesh 14 · Guard 9 · Attack +4, smothering 2, and the victim catches light · Armor 0 · Dread 3) that wants only to be lit, and to be called by its name, and does not know it no longer has one.
**The secret.** Wick has been honest in every line of this catalogue but one. The Sinks Stub, No. 3, is made of offcuts and vat-bottoms, and the vat-bottoms have not been strained since Lack 640, because what is in the bottom of the vats has started to move toward the north side of the copper at midnight and Wick's journeymen no longer like to put their arms in. Every Sinks Stub sold since then has a little of everything in it, and every one of them, at the midnight bell, leans for one minute toward the Ferrying Post. The Sinks are full of them. On the night a Guest happens to be on the Post at midnight, they will see a thousand little lights in the low windows of the undercity all leaning at them at once, and hear, on the fen wind, a thousand very small voices saying, together, the same short word Bed One said in the Vigil, which none of them can pronounce.
**The worthless gift.** *No. 12, Ide*, was sold by her great-granddaughter to pay for an uncle's restitching. The uncle is Josse, *No. 16*: he went Ripe anyway, the restitching failed in a month, and he was sold to Wick too, at a reduced price. The family has two candles in the window now and a debt to the Guild still owing. They light both on feast days. The two candles lean toward each other and argue.
