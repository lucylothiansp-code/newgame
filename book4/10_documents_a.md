# Found Pages I: Documents of the Table

> Paper is the only thing on the Table that keeps its word without being made to.
>> — inscription over the door of the Cutwrights' College reading-room, Thimble Cross

> Read it to me again. Slower. I want to hear where it goes wrong.
>> — a Kept grandmother of Lastgate, to the grandson reading her the Seamers' bill

The Table writes itself down constantly, because writing is the only way a frightened people has of pretending a thing is finished. Every land keeps books, and every book is a confession of what that land cannot stop. This chapter reproduces six of them at length, as they might be found by the Guests: on a stall at Gristmoot, in a dead man's satchel, nailed to a barn, pressed into a child's hands at a border post, left open on a Netwatch bench at dawn with the ink not yet dry. Each is given as a long excerpt, mostly set as in-world text, and each is followed by **Carver's notes**: the hooks that bring the document into play, the rules for using it, and the secrets it holds that its writers did not know they were keeping.

The documents are meant to be handled. Print them, read them aloud, cut them into slips and hand them across the table one page at a time. A Guest who finds a page should be able to read it the way their character would, which means slowly, by bad light, with something important smudged.

[box: Using Found Pages]
- **As handouts.** Every excerpt here can be given to the players in its own words. The Carver's notes cannot.
- **As clocks.** Several documents (the Tally, the Long Count, the Register) are records that go on being kept. If the Guests return to the source later, add entries. The numbers should always be worse.
- **As evidence.** Each document contains at least one thing that, put beside another document in this chapter, says something nobody on the Table wants said. The notes point these out. Let the players find them first if they can.
- **As objects.** A found book has weight, smell and a price. A Guest carrying the Register of the Called on the Mile at night is carrying something the Netwatch will want back.
[/box]

## The Book of Exits

[sigil: oathen]

Every foreigner who comes up Treaty Stair into Tacit is sold a copy before they reach the top. It is a small, square, felt-bound book, the size of a palm, printed on the grey canyon paper that does not crack in the heat, and it is the most widely owned book in Oathen after the Book of Questions in Cradlewrack and the Pantry Lists in the Fast. Its proper title is longer than most of its entries. Its common name is the Book of Exits, and the Bench of Clauses has issued it, revised it and quietly withdrawn pages from it thirty-one times since 412 A.G.

The Book is a phrasebook of safe speech: sentences for the ordinary business of life, each one tested by the Under-Drafters of the Hall of Conditions (the Bench's own testing, which is done on volunteers, prisoners and Forsworn who have broken ground to spare) and found, in its printed form and in its stated situation, not to bind beyond its meaning. Merchants carry it. Rim Road factors carry two, in case one is stolen. Oathen mothers give it to their children at the Unbitting, with the dangerous pages pinned shut. The copy reproduced here is the thirty-first printing, of Grace 640, bought at Quillhouse by a Fatlands barrel-factor named Abel Strake, whose marginal notes in a broad, greasy hand are given in brackets. He was carrying it when he came apart on the Wending road in Carving 641. The Book was found in his coat. It was open at the chapter on haggling.

[fiction]
THE BOOK OF EXITS, or, Safe Speech for the Traveller, the Factor, the Bride, and the Child Lately Unbitted. Being Phrases Proved Before the Hall of Conditions and Found, as Printed and in Their Places, Not to Bind Beyond Their Meaning. Compiled by the Under-Drafters of the Bench of Clauses. The Thirty-First Printing, Grace 640 A.G. Sold at Quillhouse, at Treaty Stair, and at the Felt Market, three lacks, with errata slip.
THE PUBLISHERS DO NOT WARRANT, AND THIS IS NOT A PROMISE, THAT ANY PHRASE IN THIS BOOK WILL PROTECT ANY SPEAKER IN ANY SITUATION. THE PUBLISHERS STATE ONLY THAT EACH PHRASE HAS BEEN SPOKEN, IN THE SITUATION GIVEN, BY A PERSON WHO WAS AFTERWARD FOUND WHOLE, AS FAR AS THE HALL COULD SEE.
TO THE READER. This book does not promise you anything. Read that sentence twice; it is the safest in the book, and the drafters spent eleven days on it. You have come into a country where your words are physical, where what you say is weighed by a god that is no longer outside you, and where a careless sentence will open you along a seam you did not know you had. We cannot teach you to speak as we speak. That takes a childhood in the bit. We can give you sentences that other people have survived. Use them as written. Do not improve them. Do not add "truly" or "really" or "I swear" for warmth. Warmth is how foreigners die here. If no phrase in this book fits your situation, the phrase that fits is silence, and silence is always available, and is never rude.
[/fiction]

### Of Greeting

[fiction]
OF GREETING, AND OF THE FIRST WORDS SAID TO A STRANGER.
1. "I see you." For good morning, good evening, and well met. It is a true statement about the speaker, and it cannot be broken while your eyes are open. Do not say it to the blind. (They are not offended; the Hall simply has no ruling.)
2. "You are here, and so am I." The greeting between equals in the Galleries. Answer with the same words. It is a pleasantry, and it is also a statement of fact; if either party is not, in some sense, here, it is better that the matter be raised early.
3. "Well met, I think." Never say "well met" alone. A meeting that turns out ill makes the speaker a liar after the fact, and the Book of Ruptures records forty cases of split lips at the end of bad bargains begun with those two words.
4. When asked "How are you?", answer "As you see me." Never answer "Fine." Never answer "Never better." (The second has been heard as a vow of decline.)
5. When introducing yourself: "I am called Abel Strake." Not "I am Abel Strake." The Hall has twice ruled that a person may not, in the god's hearing, be the same thing as their name, and has twice been unable to say why.
6. When entering a house: "Am I let in?" When letting someone into yours: "The door is open, for the present." Never "make yourself at home." That is a promise of a home, and it has been kept, in at least one case, by a guest who would not leave for nineteen years and could not be removed without breaking the host.
7. When parting: "If able." The answer is "Go on." This exchange binds neither party, and it is how every Oathener says goodbye to every other, including at deathbeds. Do not say "see you soon." Do not say "take care," which is an instruction, and is not rude, but may be heard as the beginning of a bargain.
8. Before eating with strangers, the common grace is signed in Oathen. If you must say it aloud, say: "Lack keep away, so far as I may ask it, for the length of this meal." It is slow. You will be thanked for the slowness.

### Of Refusing Marriage, and of Accepting It

The Book's longest chapter is the one foreigners read least. It is written for the Unbitted, who at twelve are thrown into the canyons' furious courtship economy without a single safe sentence of their own, and it is the chapter Oathen parents read aloud at the Unbitting supper while the child, newly mouth-free and terrified, sits with lips pressed together.

[fiction]
OF REFUSING MARRIAGE, AND OF ACCEPTING IT, AND OF THE MANY THINGS BETWEEN.
Marriage in Oathen is a Solemn oath and is drafted, not spoken. Nothing said at a proposal is a marriage. But much said at a proposal is binding, and more harm is done in the canyons by refusals than by vows. A refusal is a promise about the future. Draft it as one.
11. "I do not, at this hour, intend it." The plain refusal. It describes your intention, which is a fact about you, and it leaves the future to the future. It is cold. It is meant to be.
12. "Not in this draft." The kind refusal. It is understood by everyone in Tacit to mean no, and it binds the speaker to nothing at all, since there will always be another draft.
13. "My kin have not drafted it." The delaying refusal. True of every unmarried person in Oathen. Use it to buy a season.
14. "I am honoured, I believe." Use before any of the above. Do not omit "I believe." A girl of the Shadeward said "I am honoured" to a suitor she despised, in 588, and her cheeks went red and stayed red, in two round blotches like a doll's paint, for the rest of her life. The Hall calls it the mildest rupture on record. She called it something else.
15. NEVER SAY: "I will never marry you." It is a Plain oath at the least, and the canyons are small, and the years are long, and people change, and families need water-shares. The Hall has records of seven women and four men who said it at sixteen and married the same person at thirty. The Breaking for a broken refusal is the heart. You will know the shape of it from the street.
16. NEVER SAY: "I could never love you." "Could" is a boast of incapacity and is heard as a vow. See 15.
17. NEVER SAY: "Over my dead body," to a parent, of a match. See the chapter of Phrases That Have Killed People.
18. To accept, say: "Let the drafters begin." Do not say "yes." A yes at a proposal is an unbounded promise, and the drafters have nothing to draft it down to. A boy of the Cisterns who said yes in 604 was held by the Hall to be married to his suitor in every respect any reading could find, including some the suitor had not intended, and the families settled the matter in a way the Hall does not print.
19. To one who asks again after refusal, say: "You have my answer, as given." Then go indoors. The second asking is a pressure; the third is a weapon; Oathen courtship has killed more young people by asking than by answering.
20. To a suitor who threatens to harm himself: say nothing at all. Go to his kin, and sign it to them. Anything you say to him will be weighed against what he does next, and you will carry it.
[In margin, Strake: thank the god I'm married already & in the Fatlands, where a wedding is two words and a ham.]
[/fiction]

### Of Haggling

[fiction]
OF HAGGLING, AND OF THE SPEECH OF THE MARKET.
The Felt Market of Tacit is the quietest market on the Table, and the most dangerous for a foreigner, because every price is a statement and every statement is weighed. Haggle in handtalk if you can. Since the Lack of 640 the Hall can no longer say that handtalk is entirely safe, but it is safer.
21. "It appears dear to me." Never "It is too dear." The first is your perception, which is your own; the second is an assertion about value, and the Hall has no settled reading of value.
22. "I have seen it cheaper, I am told." A foreigner's favourite. It is safe only because it is absurd: you are saying that you have been told about your own seeing. The Hall permits it on the grounds that no god could be bothered.
23. "I would pay four, today." The "today" is the exit. If you come back tomorrow and pay five, you have broken nothing.
24. "That is my offer, for this hour." NEVER say "That is my last offer." See the chapter of Phrases That Have Killed People.
25. "Done, by these terms." Then sign the bargain with the closed right hand on the open left. Do not shake on it. A handshake is a Plain oath in its own right, independent of the words, and both of you will carry it.
26. The seller's phrases. You will hear these; they are not lies, and you should not be insulted. "Fresh this morning, I am told." "It weighs what the scale says." "My mother bought from this stall." "I have no cheaper, in this basket." Each tells you exactly how far the seller will stand behind what they say. Learn to hear the distance.
27. NEVER SAY to a seller: "You'd sell your own mother for that." It is an accusation, not a promise, and so it binds the seller to nothing. But the Hall has three cases since 630 in which the seller later did exactly that, at a fair price, to the Vaults, and in each case the seller swore that before the remark they had never once thought of it.
28. NEVER SAY: "I'll take the lot." You will. You will take it whether you have the money or not, and whether you want it or not, and the Bench will not intervene, because the seller heard you.

His back went like a bow at the Rim boundary and he walked the last nine miles bent with a wheel of cheese on it. God help me I laughed.]
[In margin, a second note, smaller, Strake: 24. Do not forget 24.]
[/fiction]

### Of Condolence

[fiction]
OF CONDOLENCE, AND OF SPEECH IN THE PRESENCE OF THE DEAD.
The dead in Oathen stand in the Witnessing Vaults with their faces outward, and they hear. Speak at a wake as you would speak before the Bench, because you are.
31. "I grieve with you, as I am able." The common condolence. Do not say "I share your grief." You do not, and the Hall has held that a grief claimed and not felt is a lie of Weight 3, and the mouth has gone in the middle of a eulogy.
32. "She was known to me." For speaking of the dead to the bereaved. It is true, it is modest, and it promises nothing about how she was known.
33. "There may be things I can do. Name one, and I will consider it." NEVER say "If there is anything I can do." It is an open promise to a grieving person, and grief asks for impossible things. The Hall's file on this phrase is the thickest in the Archive of Readings after the file on "always." It contains the case of the cooper who said it to a widow at her husband's niche, and was asked, in handtalk, very calmly, to bring him back; and what the cooper then attempted to do in the Vaults, for nine nights, alone, with his tools; and what the Keeners found.
34. NEVER SAY: "I will never forget her." Memory is weather. When you forget, and you will, you will break at the place where she touched you last, and you will not remember why it hurts.
35. NEVER SAY: "She is at peace." It is a statement about the condition of the dead, made in their hearing. The dead have Testified, on four occasions recorded in the Vaults, that they are not.
36. To the dead themselves, in the niche: "Stand well." It is a wish. Wishes do not bind, so far as the Hall has ever found, unless a Sayer makes them.
37. At the wake meal, if asked whether you will come to the anniversary: "That is my wish." Never "I'll be there."
[In margin, Strake: said "she was known to me" at old Dellow's wake in Sated last spring out of habit and his daughter slapped me. Fatlands people want you to say you loved them. Wrong book for home.]
[/fiction]

### Of War, Parley and the Holding of Passes

[fiction]
OF WAR, OF PARLEY, AND OF THE HOLDING OF PASSES. Revised after Wending Pass. Read with the annexed Ruling of the Bench on Military Speech, which is not reprinted here, and which every officer of the Canyon Watch is required to have by heart.
41. "We are minded to hold." For the defence of any place. Never "We will hold." Never "to the last." Never, under any circumstances, "to the death." See Wending Pass, which every child of Oathen knows, and which this book will not rehearse.
42. "Advance, if able." The order of attack. Every soldier who hears it may stop when no longer able, and many have, and lived, and been flogged, and lived.
43. "You may come to harm here." The only threat this book will print. It is an observation. It is also, in the experience of the Watch, more frightening to an enemy than any vow of slaughter, because the enemy understands that the speaker is under no obligation to stop.
44. NEVER SAY: "I'll kill you." To a foreign soldier it is a boast. To you it is a Plain oath, and if you do not kill him, by your hand, you will break, and the Breaking for an unkept threat is the hand that did not do it.
45. At parley: "We come to talk, and intend no harm while talking." Draft the length of the talk before you begin.
46. In surrender: "We lay these arms down, as of this hour." Do not say "We will not take them up again." Your captors will want you to say it. Do not. Make them draft it.
47. To the wounded on the field: "Help is sought." Never "Help is coming." Help is not always coming. A sergeant of the Watch said "Help is coming" to a boy at the Gullet ford in 612 and held his hand until he died, and held it for an hour after, and when the relief came he could not open his fingers, and they are still closed, and he carries the boy's last grip in a hand that no longer opens.
[In margin, Strake: all very well for soldiers. When the Reckoners come at the wagons on the Wending road what am I meant to shout, "you may come to harm here"? Yes, says the clerk. Shout that.]
[/fiction]

### Phrases That Have Killed People

The last chapter of the Book is printed on red paper, and since the twentieth printing it has been sewn in so that it can be cut out by parents. Most foreign copies keep it. Most Oathen copies do not. It is the only part of the Book that the canyon-born read for pleasure, in the way other peoples read ghost stories, and it is the only part that grows: each printing has added one or two. The Breaking in Oathen is never fatal by itself, and the chapter says so in its first line. Then it explains, case by case, the ways in which that is no comfort.

[fiction]
PHRASES THAT HAVE KILLED PEOPLE. The Breaking does not kill. The Hall has never recorded a Breaking that killed by itself. What kills is the sentence: what it binds the speaker to do, or to be, or to suffer, after. These are the cases the Hall has found most instructive. Each phrase was spoken by an ordinary person in an ordinary hour. Read them to your children.
I. "I'M STARVING." Hodd Varrow, Slate-Clerk, Tongue-line, at the Course, 611. He said it at the fourth hour, coming in from the archive to the felted kitchen of his house on the Bench Terrace, as men have said it at the fourth hour since there were kitchens. His wife laid the table. He sat. He ate bread, figs, the green cheese, a bowl of goat stew, a second bowl. He said, puzzled, that he could not taste it, and that he was still starving. He ate everything in the pantry. By the ninth hour his cheeks had fallen in like a tent with the pole pulled out, and his wrists were as thin as a child's, and his belly was full and round and hard under his shirt, and he died at the table at the eleventh hour, weighing, the Bench's physicians found, five stone and two pounds, with the whole of his pantry in him undigested. At the Course, a figure of speech is no longer a figure.
II. "THAT IS MY LAST OFFER." Mattick Sone, wool-factor, of the Felt Market, 597. He said it, and was refused, and went home, and the next day offered again, higher. His tongue split at the first word, lengthwise, in two neat halves like a snake's. It healed so. He went on trading by handtalk. Six years later, at a sale, he offered a bid on a bale of felt with his fingers, and his fingers broke at the first sign, all ten, outward. The god had held that he had made his last offer, and every offer after it was a lie. He could not buy bread. He could not offer to pay for it. He could not offer to work for it. He sat in the Felt Market with a bowl, and did not hold it out, because holding it out was an offer, and was fed by people who were sorry for him until people stopped being sorry, in his eleventh winter, which was very cold.
III. "OVER MY DEAD BODY." Orlen Tace, felt-dyer of the Galleries, 623, of his daughter's marriage to a Forsworn of the Breakdowns. The daughter married. On the morning of the marrying, when she came to the felted door of the marrying-room in the Hall with her drafts under her arm, she found her father lying across the threshold, dead, warm, his eyes open, his arms at his sides, in his good coat. No mark was ever found on him. The Hall ruled that the marriage could not go forward unless she stepped over the body, as the oath required. She stood for an hour. Then she stepped over him. The Hall's clerk records that she did not look down. The Hall's clerk records also that her husband had to lift her the last part, because her legs would not do it.
IV. "MARK MY WORDS." Fennick Ash, an apprentice of the Ledger, 631, said in jest to the chisel-masters at the cliff face, of a wager about the Kept Rain. The words of the wager were found the next morning carved into his back and chest and arms, in the Ledger's own letter, a finger deep, in red, the edges of each cut as clean as stone-work, and the wager itself, which he lost, carved a second time across his face. He lived. The Ledger now has a rule that apprentices may not speak at the cliff at all, and a slate-boy who forgets is struck, hard, across the mouth, by whoever is nearest, out of love.
V. "NEVER AGAIN." Pask Dorrow, lift-hauler of the Shadeward Wall, 629, after the cable of the ninth-tier lift slipped and dropped him forty feet before it caught. He crawled out onto the ninth tier and said it. He never rode the lift again. He could not. When he went to the stair, his legs would not climb it, for the stair was a way of going between tiers and the god held that it counted. He lived on the ninth tier for eleven years, on what his neighbours brought him. In the dry summer of 640 the ninth tier's cistern failed, and the tier was cleared, and Pask Dorrow sat at the lip of the lift-shaft and watched it go up and down full of water-jars for six days, and died of thirst on the seventh in sight of it, with his neighbours shouting up at him from the eighth, weeping, telling him to come down.
VI. "TAKE MY WORD FOR IT." Ysolt Varne, a Surety, to a principal who doubted her, 633. The principal did. In the morning Ysolt Varne woke with her mouth full of blood and no tongue, the root smooth and sealed as if it had never grown, and her principal woke three streets away choking on a second tongue behind his own, warm and heavy and moving. The surgeons of the Murmuring House cut it out of him. It went on moving in the basin for a day. Ysolt Varne asked, on a slate, to have it back, and was refused, on the ground that it was no longer hers.
VII. "I'M DYING TO KNOW." Wenna Calloway, Tongue-line, nineteen, at the Course, at the reading of her grandmother's will in 638, which ran to ninety pages, and which the Clausewright read aloud in its entirety while the family sat in silence, and in which the matter Wenna was dying to know, which was whether the old woman had left her the house in the Cisterns, was the last clause. The Clausewright read it. The house was hers. She smiled, the family say, and then her face changed, and she said, quite quietly, "Oh. Oh, I see," and laid her head on the table, and was gone.
VIII. "I SWEAR ON MY MOTHER." Callum Dree, water-thief, 627, before the Bench, falsely, on the Grave Weight, with his hand on the cold face of his mother in her niche in the Vaults. He was released for want of a Breaking, because he did not break: he was not Tongue-line, and he was lucky, and the god is not always quick. He went home. In the morning his wife came down to find the old woman standing in their kitchen, dry and dark and brittle as a fig, her lips drawn back to the gums in the Vaults' look of having just heard something, her hand still raised where it had been laid on his. The Keeners say the dead Testify when an oath on them is broken. They do not usually walk three miles to do it. She stood in that kitchen for nineteen days. She said nothing. She did not need to. On the twentieth day he went to the Bench and confessed, and was sentenced, and swore the sentence, and the old woman was found that night back in her niche, and has not moved since. Callum Dree's sentence was to carry water for the household he robbed, for life. He does. He is very careful with it.
IX. "I'D DIE FOR YOU." Tamsin Ure, of the Canyon Watch, to his betrothed, Halla Ure, the night before their drafting, in 635. He was Tongue-line, at the Appetite, and it was a plain promise between people who loved each other. In the Lack of 637 she took the canyon fever, which kills one in three in the Galleries, and on the ninth night it broke, and she sat up and asked for water, and he was found in the next room, at the moment of the fever's breaking, dead in his chair, with a cup of water in his hand, unspilled. The Hall records that the oath was kept. We print it because people still say it at weddings. We would ask you not to. We would ask you not to love anyone so much that you will say it. We know you will not listen.
[In margin, beside IX, Strake, the ink very dark: Mags. If you are reading this I did not say it to you because I am a coward & not because I would not.]
[/fiction]

[box: Carver's Notes: The Book of Exits]
**Getting a copy.** Any Guest entering Oathen can buy the Book for 3 L at Treaty Stair, Quillhouse or the Felt Market, and every Oathen-born Guest already owns one with the red chapter cut out, or a parent's copy with it still in. Abel Strake's annotated copy is a better find: it can be taken from his body on the Wending road, or bought from the Breakdowns' book-sellers, who strip the dead.
**Speaking from the Book.** When a printed phrase fits the situation the Book names (greeting, refusing a suitor, haggling, condolence, war), a Guest may say it word for word instead of rolling Conditional Speech. The phrase carries the Exits written into it and is treated as **Clean**: the Carver must honour any reasonable reading. It cannot be improved, shortened or decorated; a single added word ("truly", "I promise you") makes it an ordinary sentence and the roll must be made. Using a phrase outside its situation (saying "If able" to a dying man asking whether you will avenge him) gets no benefit, and the Carver may call the roll at +4 Difficulty, because the god heard you reach for a formula.
**Being heard to use it.** An Oathener hears Book-speech at once. It marks the speaker as foreign or newly Unbitted: −2 on Persuade and Haggle with the canyon-born, who think it a little comic and a little sad. A Guest who uses it in the Breakdowns is mocked loudly; the Forsworn speak as they like.
**The widening.** The Book was tested in an Oathen that no longer quite exists. Each time a Guest speaks from the Book after the first in a scene, the Carver rolls a d10 in secret. On a 1, the phrase is one the Hall has since found unsafe (as the errata slip suggests), and it carries no Exits at all. The Guest does not learn this until the oath is tested.
**Hooks.**
- *The Thirty-Second Printing.* The Hall of Conditions is drafting a new edition and needs the phrases tested again, on volunteers, under the widening. It pays 20 L a day and swears its testers to secrecy at Weight 3. One of the testers before them has not come back from the testing room on the Bench Terrace, and the door is felted from the inside.
- *Mags.* Abel Strake's widow in the Leanings of Sated has never been told what her husband wrote beside the ninth phrase. Delivering the book is a kindness. Mags Strake will read the margin and then ask, very calmly, how he died, and whether he said anything; and the Guests who were there will have to answer her.
- *Number 24.* Strake's second note tells the Guests he was frightened of §24. His fellow factors say he made his last offer for a load of Fatlands barrels on the Wending road and was refused. What he did next, and who was waiting for him, is the reason the wagons were found standing empty with the oxen still yoked.
**Secret.** The preface was written in 412 by an Under-Drafter named Ysolde Marrick, and its first sentence, "This book does not promise you anything," has been reprinted unchanged in every edition. Since the Lack of 640 writing has begun to bind, and the Bench's sealed ruling (which the errata slip does not mention) holds that every printing of the Book is, in part, its compiler's word, sworn before the god, that the phrases are safe. Ysolde Marrick has been in her niche in the Vaults since 451. Since the thirtieth printing she has been heard in the Vaults at night, by the Keeners, reciting the phrases, one by one, in a dry whisper, and stopping at the ones that have since failed, and making a small sound at each, like a woman who has pricked her finger. The Hall has told no one. If the Book is ever wrong in a way that kills a reader, the dead woman will Testify, and nobody knows what a Testimony looks like from a person who has been dead for two hundred years and who is, by the Book's own preface, not promising anything.
[/box]

## The Tally of Cradlewrack, Volume 641

[sigil: cradlewrack]

The great ledger of the Tally-house on Tally Hill in Kest has been kept without a missed day since 131 A.G., and each year is bound as one volume: a slab of oiled paper between boards of red-stained ash, chained to a reading-desk in the long cold gallery under the clock-room where Lark Ninedays keeps the interval. Anyone may ask to see any page. The scriveners will bring the volume, open it on the desk, and stand at the end of the gallery while you read, because people who find their own lines struck through have done things to the pages, and to themselves, in that gallery, and the scriveners are tired of cleaning it.

What follows is a copy of selected pages from Volume 641, as it stood in late Carving, made by a Cutwright surveyor of Thimble Cross who paid the Tally-house a platter for nine days at the desk and was asked, on the tenth, to leave. Her copy keeps the five columns of the Tally (the number, the household, the bearer, *what*, and *stays*) and the house shorthand: *sp.* for speaking-born, *o.b.* for old-born, *g.* for born-grown, *pt.* for a Part, *w.b.* for *without bearer*. [box: Carver's Notes: The Tally]
**Reading the Tally.** Any Guest may climb Tally Hill and ask for a volume. Finding a particular line (your own birth, a missing child, the bearer of a Part) is an extended task: Eye + Reckoning, Hard 14, one hour a roll, 2 Helpings for a known name and year, 5 for anything vaguer. A Lack means the Guest finds a line they were not looking for: a ruled line in their own household, a sibling nobody mentioned, a birth to their own mother in a year they were not born. A Cradlewrack-born Guest who reads their own line makes a Dread 1 check; one who finds it ruled, a Dread 3.
**Entries as hooks.** Every line above is a person, a Part or a door the Guests can visit. The two Gage girls (4,301 and 4,302) are one asleep and one awake, and the awake one says "let her sleep" every time anyone goes near her sister. Tam Corse's old woman (4,303) remembers being Ada Corse's mother in a life before the Gorging, and wants to be taken to a house in Kest that has been bricked since 310. The prints on the moor road (4,309) belong to whatever Josiah Fenn bore and has been following for eight years. The hand at 1,519 was not burned; the Opinion who reported it is lying, and the Assemblers know it, and so does Hester Lowe, who is now not the only person in Cradlewrack hiding a left hand.
**The empty column.** The unnumbered line has appeared once a season since 501 A.G. It is not borne by anyone, and it is not answered, because no Attendant has ever been present when it is entered. A Guest who waits all night at the Tally-house desk during a pang (Caul + Resolve, Grim 18, to stay awake and at the desk through the pang's crescendo; a Vigil-born Guest rolls at Hard) will see the page fill with the narrow black hand on the eighth line, with nobody holding the pen. Dread 3. On a Grace, the Guest notices that the ink is still wet, and smells of bread.
**What the Tally is for.** In Cradlewrack the Tally is the only proof of personhood. A Guest whose line is ruled may be taken for a late Opinion (*returning*) by any Attendant who checks. A Guest whose line is empty, as entry 4,600's, is something the Attendance has no law for, and the Lying-In wants very much to ask them the Second Question.
**Secret.** Rufus Ochre's eleven hundred: the Tally of 640 shows 1,105 more births than were carried by anyone or anything, and Ochre has been following the unaccounted at night and found three, born-grown, walking north in a line. The Carver should compare this number with the Long Count's total for Carving 640 (below) and decide whether it is a coincidence. Nobody on the Table has put the two side by side. Nobody has both documents. The Guests may.
[/box]

## The Long Count

[sigil: fatlands]

Hogget's Barn stands at the end of Kettle Furrow, in Low Tilth, a plain timber barn with its doors propped open and the smell of black soil and chalk-dust coming out of it. Its east wall is the village's own tally, scratched and painted course by course since 629; the core book gives the lower course of it. Its north wall is something else. It is where Pell Hogget enters the numbers that the other villages send him, folded in shoes and hat-bands and the hollow handles of drovers' sticks, every Carving, and it is the only place on the Table where the Reaping is written down in full for anyone to see. Pilgrims come to touch it. The Board has twice tried to burn it. The copy below was made on oiled slate by a Long Count runner named Hennet Callock, who carried it to forty parishes in her boot before the Bailiffs took her at the Tilth causeway in Lack 640; the slate was recovered from the ditch and is now kept, cracked across, in a fold of sacking under Old Mother Rind's pear trees.

The marks on the wall are simple, and every child in Low Tilth can read them: a stroke for each Reaped; a stroke with a cross-bar for each dusk-shifted who did not walk back; a ring for each Seated; and, since 638, a stroke with a small open curve at the top like a hand held out, for *taken by a hill*. Callock's slate turns the strokes into figures. A half is a half.

### The Arithmetic

Pell Hogget does sums the way other men pray, and his sums are on the barn door, in chalk, rubbed half out and done again. Callock copied them onto the back of her slate. Most of them are the arithmetic his neighbours already know and say aloud in the inns of Low Tilth: the Reaping in any village doubles every eight or nine years; Kettle Furrow had two hundred and six souls in 629, and at this rate the year's Reaping will equal the village in 660; Low Tilth will be gone into the wheat before 680. Some of the sums are other things, the arithmetic that people in the Fatlands have begun to call, in a low voice, *the sum you don't do*. Callock copied those too.

[fiction]
ON THE BACK OF THE SLATE, in H. Callock's hand, copied from the barn door. "Pell's door sums. He says copy them but say they're only sums."
ONE. The doubling. 4 in 629. 27 in 640. Doubles in 8 yrs and a bit. 2nd doubling 648, 54. 3rd 656, 108. 4th 664, 216. Kettle Furrow is 194 souls. "Before 664 the field takes the village in a year. Before then it takes more than half. The year it takes more than half, nobody will be left to bring the tally. So the last number on the wall will be wrong. I want somebody to know it was wrong."
TWO. All villages. 330 in 634, 1,104 in 640. Some of that is more villages sending. Take it out, take only the villages that sent every year since 634: still doubling, every 9 yrs. The Board says there are four hundred thousand in the Fatlands. "I worked it the long way and the short way. The short way, the field takes the whole country in one Carving in 712. The long way, 709. It doesn't matter which. Barnaby will be 87, 84. If he's lucky."
THREE. The eating. Pell took the villages that eat most (by the Bailiff's own Feasting lists, which the Bailiff's clerk copied for us for a ham) and the villages that eat least. "The ones who eat most lose most. Every year. Every village. The fat villages lose two to the thin villages' one. This is a sum you can be hanged for, because it means the Thin are right, and fasting is treason, so I have not written what it means, only what it is." FOUR. The house. "Barnaby did the sum for our house. Three of us: me, Barnaby, and [a word rubbed out]. Two of us. ## A Child's Book of Refusals

[sigil: fast]

There are few books in the Fast, because paper must be carried in from the Rim like bread, and anything carried in is counted. This one is in every household. It is printed in Wanting from woodblocks cut in 412, on coarse grey Rim paper, folded and stitched with ration-thread, one copy to each family on the Pantry roll, and it is how every Unfed child has learned to read for two hundred years. It is read standing. It has no pictures of food. It has one picture of a table, and the child is meant to find it. The copy here belonged to Abstain Rooke, called Abby, eleven, of Bell Ward, and it carries her additions in pencil; the Hostwatch took it from her house in Carving 641, after her mother brought it to them with her hands shaking and asked them to burn it, and they did not.

[fiction]
A CHILD'S BOOK OF REFUSALS. Set for the Cold Yard schools of Wanting and the Wards, under the Abstinent, by Mistress Meagre Holt, in the year 412. Reprinted from the same blocks. To be read standing, facing the wall or the sky. A child who cannot say the Rules does not eat today. A child who can say the Rules may eat, and must not enjoy it.
[In pencil, on the inside cover, Abby: THIS BOOK BELONGS TO ABSTAIN ROOKE, BELL WARD, WANTING, THE FAST, THE TABLE. IF FOUND, NO THANK YOU.]
[/fiction]

### Lesson the First: The Rules

The Five Rules are the oldest thing in the Fast after the Refusal, and the book sets each as a skipping rhyme, to be chanted in the Cold Yard while the rope turns. The rhymes are coarse, quick and easy, and every Unfed adult can still skip to them, and some do, alone, at night, when the smell of baking comes up the road.

[fiction]
THE FIRST RULE. Never enter a house where the hearth is already lit.
Smoke in the chimney, nobody home,
Warm on the doorstep, warm on the stone,
Who lit the fire? Not your Gran.
Turn on your heel and run while you can.
THE SECOND RULE. Never answer "Come in."
Knock at the door and the voice says come,
It sounds like your sister, it sounds like your Mum,
Bite on your tongue till the tongue goes numb.
Come in, come in. No, thank you. Mum.
THE THIRD RULE. Never eat what you did not carry yourself.
Carry your crust in your own two hands,
Carry it over the empty lands,
If you didn't carry it, it's not bread,
If you didn't carry it, it carries you instead.
THE FOURTH RULE. If you smell bread, walk into the wind.
Sniff, sniff, what's that smell?
Somebody's baking, and baking well,
Turn your face where the cold wind blows,
And never mind what's under your nose.
THE FIFTH RULE. If a chair is pulled out for you, do not thank it.
Chair pulled out and the cloth laid white,
Lamp in the middle, burning bright,
Don't say thank you, don't say please,
Don't you dare go bending your knees.
[In pencil, beside the fifth, Abby: why not thank it. It would be polite. Mistress Holt said because. That is not a reason.]
[/fiction]

### Lesson the Third: Find the Laid Table

The centre of the book is a double page: a single woodcut, crowded and busy, of a farmhouse yard on a Carving afternoon. Children are made to stand before it and point. The cut is crude, black and grey, with the cross-hatched shadows of an old block, and every child in the Fast knows it as well as they know their own hands. The caption reads, *There are nine things in this picture that want you. Find them all. Then turn the page and see if you are still here.* Abby's copy is described here as the Hostwatch tracker who received it described it, item by item, in his report.

[fiction]
THE PICTURE (as described for the Hostwatch). A farmyard, square, stone-walled, the house at the back with two windows and a door. A well in the left foreground. A cart, empty. A row of washing on a line. Two children in the middle ground, a boy and a girl, thin, in Unfed coats, walking away from the house toward the viewer, holding hands. The sky is blank paper. The things, as numbered on the answer page:
1. The chimney. A thin line of smoke goes up from it. There is no one in the house.
2. The door. It stands open a finger's width. In the black slit of it, if you hold the page close, the cutter has carved two letters, very small: C and I.
3. The window on the left. A woman's face, soft and kind, cut in a few lines. She looks like everybody's mother. That is the point.
4. The window on the right. On its sill, a round loaf, steaming; three wavy lines go up from it to show the smell.
5. The well. A white cloth has been laid over its lid, folded neat at the corners, like a cloth on a table.
6. The washing. Among the shirts on the line is a long white tablecloth, and it is not blowing the same way as the shirts.
7. The cart. Its tail is down, and on the tail, as on a step, is a little cup.
8. The ground before the children. There is a chair in the yard, behind them, turned toward them, pulled out. They have walked past it. They have not looked back. This is the one the children find last.
9. The girl's hand. In the hand that is not holding her brother's she has a small card, white, the kind that is set at a place. She has picked it up. She is not reading it. Good girl.

**Getting a copy.** Every Unfed Guest learned to read from this book and can recite the rhymes; every Unfed household has one. A Guest from elsewhere can be given one by a Wanting schoolmistress (a kindness, offered three times and accepted on the fourth). Abby Rooke's own copy is held by the Hostwatch in Wanting, by Spare Tolland, who has not burned it.
**Rule: the rhymes.** A character who knows the Five Rules rhymes by heart (all Unfed; anyone else after a week in a Cold Yard school) may chant one aloud when Orrum offers, once per offer: a bonus die (the Tooth's d6, but it costs no Hunger) on the Invitation check, provided the rhyme fits the offer (the Fourth for the smell of bread, the Second for a voice at a door). A companion may chant it for them instead, as speaking the Decline. Children chanting it for an adult add two dice; the Unfed say the god likes children best, and listens to them hardest.
**Rule: Find the Laid Table.** The woodcut is a game the Carver can play at the table: read the description of the yard aloud, once, and ask the players to name the nine things. Every Guest who names at least seven gains +2 on their next Search to notice one of Orrum's offers. A player who names a tenth thing not in the list has found what will be offered to their character next; the Carver should note it and use it.
**Hooks.**
- *The Bitten Page.* Abby Rooke has been leaving a crust on her windowsill for a year, and each morning it has been replaced with something better, as the core book says. On the night her colouring page was bitten, nothing was left in its place. Abby is frightened for the first time. Whatever has been answering her has stopped, or been interrupted, or been replaced at her window by something that takes and does not give. Spare Tolland asks the Guests to sit up at her sill. What comes to the sill is courteous and hungry and does not lay anything.
- *The Spoon.* The boy in the woodcut is holding something different in every copy, and it is not a stone. Mistress Meagre Holt cut the blocks in 412; her house in Wanting is a hearthless ruin, and the original block is in its cellar, in a box, with the boy's hand cut out of it. Somebody has been printing new copies from a recut block. The children who learned from the new copies are the ones who have started walking west.
- *Forty Names.* Abby wrote a name under each figure on the colouring page. Some are names nobody in Wanting knows. Reft Lammas, Roll-Keeper of Barrowgate, recognises three of them from the Barrow rolls of the Refusal, and they are the names of three who stood at Hallowboard in the ninth winter and whose names have been lost from every record for six hundred years. Abby says she was told them. She will not say by whom.
**Secret.** The answer page lists nine things that want you. The book's first printing in 412 listed ten. The tenth was cut from the block before the second printing by order of the Abstinent, and its entry on the answer page was chiselled out, so that in the oldest surviving copies there is a blank rectangle after the ninth entry with the faint ridges of words in it. It said what was standing at the eighth side of the table in the colouring picture. It is the same thing a child finds when they find a tenth thing in the yard, and it has never once, in two hundred years, been the same object twice, because it is always the thing the child wanted most.

## The Seamer's Pattern Book

[sigil: sallowreach]

Every licensed shop on Needle Street keeps a pattern book, and no two are alike, because the Seamers' Guild teaches its craft by hand and by rhyme and leaves the writing-down to each shop. The books are working things: thick, swollen, greasy, smelling of cedar and the brown, their pages stiff with lacquer from fingers that turned them mid-job. The pattern book of the Harl shop, at the sign of the Needle and Eel on Needle Street, Lastgate, was begun in 344 by Master Gideon Harl and has been kept since by his daughter Ottilie, Kept since 402, who still works at the bench by lamplight with hands her father reattached, and by Ottilie's apprentices' apprentices, among them the present Mistress Harl. Its last pages hold Ottilie's notes in a thin, exact, slightly shaking hand, and an inspection note from Thessaly Mort, Master Seamer of the Guild. What follows is extracted. The book has over four hundred pages. Nineteen are sewn shut, with the Lid, and the Harls will not open them.

[fiction]
THE PATTERN BOOK OF THE NEEDLE AND EEL, Harl's, Needle Street. Begun by G. Harl, Master of the Seam, in the 344th year, for the use of this shop and no other. Brown is still hers. Black is the god's. Grey is the guild's business, and white is nobody's, and you burn it. A seam is not a healing. A seam is a lid. Begin at the head and work down, so that what must run out runs out at the bottom.
[/fiction]

### The Stitches

[fiction]
THE LID. The first seam and the commonest. For any wound in a Kept body, which will not close of itself in this country, and will stay a wet mouth in the flesh for as long as the flesh lasts. Clean to brown: cut away the black with the curved blade, lift what moves with the tweezers into the vinegar, and leave the brown, which is still the client's and will be wanted. Pack the wound with lint soaked in cedar-and-pitch until it is level with the lips. Draw the lips together. Blanket stitch, waxed gut, eight to the inch, wire at the corners, tied off with the Mourner's Loop. The client will feel every stitch. Talk to them while you work. Ask after their grandchildren. Do not ask after their great-grandchildren, who are often upstairs.
GRANDMOTHER'S PURSE. For the cheek, the jowl, the breast and the belly, which in the Settling come away from the bone along old seams and hang in pockets. Do not cut the pocket. What is in the pocket is the client's. Empty it into the vinegar with two fingers (the green-bottle favours these; mind the eyes). Pack it. Thread a running stitch of doubled gut through the old holes all the way round the mouth of the pocket, as a purse-string, and draw it up to the bone, and tie it to the wire you have set in the bone. A good Purse holds a face up for a year. A great one is invisible from across a dinner table, which is what families pay for.
THE LADDER. For the long belly seam, chest to groin, in clients past the Bloat. Set rungs of brass wire across the opening every inch, bent over at each end into the flesh like staples. Lace between the rungs with tarred thread. The Ladder is made to be opened, which no other seam is: each spring the family pays for the evacuation, when the lacing is drawn out and the cavity flushed with vinegar and repacked with cedar lint, and the client lies on the kitchen table with the family at the far end of the room, and the smell goes out of the window and down the street, and the neighbours say, "Harl's are at the Pennocks' again," and shut their own windows. Re-lace from the top. The client will want to watch. Let them.
THE HERON. For knees and elbows past the Long Grey, where the joint has rotted to string. Two splints of whalebone, wired either side of the joint, hinged with a brass rivet, so that the limb bends one way and only one way. A client in Herons can walk, sit, and climb a stair, slowly, with a ticking sound. (A factor of Oathen has asked this shop whether a Heron could be set backwards, to straighten the knees of the Forsworn. We declined. It would be the god's knees we were bending, and the god set them so on purpose. G.H.)
THE FLY-FENCE. A close whip-stitch of silk, very fine, twenty to the inch, around the rims of the eyes, the nostrils and the lips, set in the fat just inside the lid, so that the green-bottle cannot get in to lay. It does not stop them. It slows them. Re-set every Plenty, before the breeding.
THE COURTESY. A single strong stitch of gut through the floor of the mouth and the point of the chin, tied loose, under the beard if there is one. It holds the jaw up at rest, so that the client's mouth does not hang open at table, and gives when they speak. Most of the Unvacated of the Upper Boards wear one. It can be tightened. This shop does not tighten it. (O.H.: this shop has been asked to, by families, forty-one times since 402, and by the Court, twice, of a witness. See the sewn pages.)
THE MOURNER'S LOOP. The last knot of every seam. There is no last stitch in Sallowreach; nothing here finishes, and a knot tied off square is held by the Guild to be bad luck, a sort of lie. We tie off with a loop, open, through which another thread might later be drawn. Every seam in this country is, by the Guild's rule, unfinished. Gideon Harl said it was the only honest thing the Guild ever made law.
THE BLIND. Two stitches through each pair of eyelids, closing them. FORBIDDEN by the Guild since 301, except by the client's own request, said aloud, before two witnesses, one living. Families ask for it often: those with Hand-line blood at the Appetite, who cannot bear an open eye in the house, and those who cannot bear to be looked at by Gran. The Kept almost never ask. They are already in the dark of a shuttered attic most of their days. They want to see the light come through the shutters. (O.H.: we have done six Blinds in three hundred years. Four were asked for. Two were asked for by the families, in the client's voice, by a cousin who could do voices, and Father did not know until the second winter, and then he undid them, and the client, Mistress Agathe Rook, looked at him for a long time and said, "Thank you, Gideon, I had begun to think I was dead.")
[/fiction]

### Lacquers

The Guild's lacquers are its proudest secret and its worst smell. Every shop boils its own, in the back yards of Needle Street, in copper pans over peat, and the street can be told from a mile off in the fog by the smell of hot cedar, pitch and something under them. None of these are to be eaten, drunk, or tried on the living. The Harl book has thirty receipts. Five are given.

[fiction]
CEDAR COMMON. The everyday coat for the families of the Middle Boards. Cedar oil, three parts. Pine pitch, two parts. Beeswax, one part, melted in last. Boil until a drop on cold slate goes hard and does not take a thumbnail. Brush on thin, in three coats, each dried with the bellows. Smells of a church. Lasts a season, more indoors. Wants redoing in Restitching Week.
COURT LACQUER. For the Upper Boards, and for any client going before the Hall of Hands. Amber resin, ground fine, dissolved in cedar oil over a slow fire for three days. Myrrh, a pinch per pint, for the smell. Gold leaf laid on the cheekbones and the backs of the hands while it is tacky. Nine coats. A client in Court Lacquer shines like a sideboard and smells of nothing a living nose can name, which is the point: the Court does not like to be reminded. Forty lacks the face, a hundred and twenty the whole. We have done the Regent's hands. See the sewn pages.
POOR MAN'S PITCH. For the Sinks, and for those who pay in thread. Pine tar and rendered tallow, half and half, with a cup of vinegar to the gallon. It goes on black and stays sticky and cracks in a month. It keeps out the flies a little and the damp hardly at all. (O.H.: do not take the tallow from Wick Tallow's yard, whatever the price. His tallow was people, and it feels itself go on, and so does the client, and they talk to each other through the coat at night, and neither will say about what.)
FLY-BANE. A wash, not a lacquer, laid under the first coat in Plenty. Vinegar, a quart. Lavender, wormwood and rue, a fistful each, steeped a week in the dark. Strained through linen. Brushed into every seam and fold. The flies hate it. So do the clients; it stings in the open places like salt in a cut, and they will curse you for an hour, and they will thank you at the end of Plenty, when their neighbours are full of grubs.
THE BRIGHT COAT. Gideon Harl's own receipt, from 344, for which this shop is known. Cedar and amber as in the Court Lacquer, with fish-glue to set it hard, and with the ash of the client's own hair, burned and ground, worked into the last coat. Why the hair: because, Father said, it is theirs, and a coat with something of theirs in it holds better. It does. A Bright Coat holds for nine years. No one else on Needle Street can make it hold for more than two, though they have stolen the receipt, because no one else will tell you the last ingredient, which is not on this page, and which Father wrote only on the sewn pages.
[/fiction]

### The Master's Notes

[fiction]
O.H., in the back of the book, the last written pages, Carving 641.
Father began this book three years after the Vigil's First Night, and he said that in a land where nothing ends a Seamer's only true work is to keep things the shape they were. I have kept it two hundred and thirty-nine years. My hands are his work; I lost them in the Reckoners' raid of 402, and he sewed them back that night, and they have never been quite mine, but they are better than mine, which I have never told anyone. I want to set down what is happening at the bench.
Since the Hushes began, thread goes grey in the needle near the Hand-line estates. It goes the colour of ash and it stops being thread; it holds, but it does not hold the way thread holds, it holds the way a door holds that has been shut for good. Thirty-one clients from the Vane and Fallow wards have finished on the bench this year, mid-seam, under the needle, with the shop's own gut still in them. They do not scream. They let out a breath they have not drawn in a century and they are gone, and the smell comes up out of them all at once, all of it, two hundred years of it, and then nothing, nothing at all, the room smells of nothing, and the Threaders run into the yard. I have stopped sending Threaders to the Vane ward. I go myself.
The Guild's stock of spare parts on the third floor of the Guildhall is three times the size it was in my apprenticeship. Thessaly will not say where it comes from. I know where hands come from. I have seen the Sump dredgers at the Guildhall's back door at night with sacks that are not dripping fen-water. I have seen what the Court sends down from the Jar Room, when a jarred man's pieces are "surplus to sentence."
And I want to set down that Father's book has a stitch in it that I have never used, on the sewn pages, which he called the Last Loop. It is a Mourner's Loop that is drawn closed. It is the only knot in this book that finishes. He said he invented it in 344 for one client only and that it held a man together who could not be held any other way, and that if the loop is ever drawn out, the man will come apart all at once, every seam of three hundred years at once, and that the man was the Regent. I am the only one who knows which thread. I am old. I am not afraid of the Court. I am afraid of the Heirs, who are young, and have begun bringing their grandmothers to the shop, and staying to watch me work, and asking, very politely, about the sewn pages.

## The Register of the Called

[sigil: brinehollow]

The Netwatch keeps a register on every beat of the Mile, a tall narrow book bound in tarred sailcloth, kept on a hook inside the watch-box at the head of each section and filled in by the Watch-Sergeant at the end of every night by the light of a storm-lantern. The logs record Callings as numbers. The families record them as names. The register does both, in five columns ruled by hand: the name and house, the rope (how the Called got loose), the colour of the last bail-water (which the hookers ask for at the door, because the families always look), whether the Called was recovered, and the signature of the hooker who made the recovery or saw the loss. It is the coast's most exact record of the Calling, and the Admiralty has never published one. The pages below are from the register of the Mile Twelve beat, between the Cooper's Gap and the Sixteenth rail, kept by Watch-Sergeant Corra Vell, from Lack 640 into Carving 641. They were found on the bench of the watch-box at dawn, with the ink of the last entry still wet, and the watch-box empty, and the storm-lantern still burning.

[box: Carver's Notes: The Register of the Called]
**Rules: reading the bail-water.** The register's third column is a forecast. A Guest who reads a household's bail-water at dawn (Eye + Search, Routine 10; Tolly Brack does it without rolling) learns its colour: **clear** (no change), **green** (that household's next Calling roll is at +2 Difficulty), **blue** (at +4, and the Carver should call a Calling roll that very night). Blue in a Guest's own bucket is a Dread 2 check, and a decision about the rope.
**Rules: serving on the beat.** A Guest who enlists in the Netwatch (Mile Seven barracks; always short of hands) walks a beat at night and signs the register. Hauling a Called home is an opposed Gut + Labor roll against the Called's Lung + Athletics, as in the core rules; the Called feel no pain and do not tire, and on a Lack the hooker is pulled off the boards with them. Every recovery a Guest signs for is a Dread 1 check, from the look on the face of the recovered. Every loss a Guest signs for is a Dread 2.
**Hooks.**
- *The Signatures.* Jem Sallow and Esmé Pardoe, entered as lost, are signing recoveries on Mile Twelve, and the recovered come home wrapped in nets, dripping, sometimes Tenanted on the same night. The Admiralty wants the register sealed and the matter ended. The families want to thank the young hooker. Sitting up a night at the Cooper's Gap with a lantern (Caul + Resolve, Hard 14, not to look west when the waving starts) shows the Guests two lanterns coming up out of the dark of the Uncovered, carrying something between them, and a third, newer one coming behind.
- *Ivo Carrack.* A boy of nine whose family came home without him and say they never had a son. He was recovered twice in Lack, asleep, asking for his sister. He has no sister in any record. Nobody in No. 6 Tarry Row will open the door to him; through it, the family can be heard saying his name, in turn, slowly, the way a household calls the morning count, and not answering.
- *The Lieutenant.* Sable Reef was at the Admiralty all night, before nine witnesses, and signed the last page of the register at Mile Twelve. She does not remember it. She dreams every night that she is walking west with the three hundred she saved, and that they are glad she came. The Guests may be the ones who tell her about the signature, or the ones the Watch-Captain sends to watch her sleep.
**Secret.** The hookers who walk off the beat do not stop being hookers. On the Uncovered, past the Sixteenth gap, the lost of the Netwatch patrol a beat of their own, in a line of lanterns that the Mile can see on still nights and does not mention, and they go on doing the only thing they were ever trained to do: they bring the Called home. They are very good at it. They are better at it than the living, because the Called do not fight them. The register's "recovered" figure on Mile Twelve has nearly doubled since Lack 640, and the Admiralty has been quietly proud of it. What the drowned hookers carry back up the boards is the Called, in their own bodies, with their own memories, and with someone else looking out through their eyes; and the families, who have been trained for six hundred years not to ask a returned person where they went, are taking them home and thanking the Netwatch. The thirty-one hookers of the Great Pressure Day of 627 are still on the beat. They have signed for more people in the last year than the living Netwatch has saved in ten. Brenna Kelp's arithmetic says the sea is moving into the people a bucket at a time. The register of Mile Twelve says somebody has begun carrying the buckets.
[/box]
