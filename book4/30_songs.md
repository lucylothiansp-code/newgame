# Hymns and Lullabies of the Table

> Everybody on the Table sings. They sang on the night of the Gorging, all seven peoples, though none of them could hear the others. They sang to keep their hands moving. They are still singing for the same reason.
>> — Bram Hollin, the Last Mourner, to a College clerk who asked him why the fen-songs are so long

[fiction]
*Pencilled inside the lid of a hurdy-gurdy case left unclaimed at the Groaning Board, Carving 638. The instrument inside had nine strings. Two had been cut.*
Do not play the Lamb in Lampwick. Do not play the Hauling past Lowmark Spur unless you want to be thanked by a man with wet eyes and no breath. Do not play the Ferrying in the fen, because it will not stop when you do, and you will hear it the whole road south. Do not play a wake-song for anyone who is not dead yet, even as a joke, even for money, ESPECIALLY for money. Do not finish the Kest Count. Do not sign along with an Oathener's poem unless you mean every finger of it. Do not hum the Decline where a chair is pulled out. Do not drink the Second Table's toast when a stranger buys it.
Play the drinking songs. Play the chair song on Tablenight. Play it loud. They will throw lacks.
I made eleven hundred lacks in nine years on this road and I never once played the eighth verse of anything. That is the whole secret of a long career.
[/fiction]

This chapter is a songbook. It gathers the hymns, lullabies, work songs, curses and toasts that the peoples of the Table sing over their meals, their dead, their ropes and their beds, and it gives each one in full (or as full as any living singer can give it), with a description of its tune, a short history, and a rule for what happens when a Guest sings it at the table. Some of these songs are sung in every kitchen on the Rim. Some are hanging offences. Several have a verse that nobody sings. The Table is like that: every song on it was written after the Gorging or survived it, and both kinds remember what they were sung over.

The tunes are described in words, the way the Rim's ballad-sellers describe them on their sheets: the meter, the mode, the shape of the line, who sings and how. No notation survives the Long Grace in any form a Rim fiddler would recognise. Carvers who want to sing at the table should borrow any old tune in the right meter. Every one of these songs was borrowed from something older once.

[box: Singing at the Table]
Most songs need no roll. A Guest who sings a song from this chapter for its own sake, badly or well, simply sings it, and the Carver describes the room. A song's **effect** (given under *At the Table* for each song) applies when it is sung with intent, in the right place, by someone who knows it.
**Knowing a song.** A Guest knows the common songs of their own land and any song a player chooses at character creation for 1 point of Lore. Learning a song from another singer takes a scene and a willing teacher. Learning a forbidden song takes a teacher who is willing to hang.
**Singing well.** When it matters (to sway a crowd, to soothe a wake, to lead a hauling line), roll **Tongue + Rites** at Routine (10) for a familiar crowd, Hard (14) for strangers. Each Helping adds 1 to the song's listed bonus, to a maximum of +2 more. A Lack means the singer has started a verse they should not have, or the wrong one; the Carver chooses which.
**Singing together.** A company that sings together counts as one singer, rolling with its best voice; any Guest who does not sing along gains none of the song's benefits and pays none of its costs, but may be noticed not singing. On the Table that is often worse.
**Songs and Pall.** A song sung properly at a rite of mourning counts as that rite for the Pall: it lowers the community's Pall by 1d3. A song that is forbidden, sung publicly in the place that forbids it, raises the Pall by 1 whatever its other effects.
[/box]

[pull] There is no song on the Table without a verse missing. If you think you know one, you have not been told. | — Rim ballad-seller's proverb

## The Ferrying Song

[sigil: sallowreach]

> Six for the living, one for the hum, and nobody knows if the eighth will come.
>> — fen children's skipping count

The oldest of the fen-songs and the one every Sallowreacher knows first, before their own name, before *may you finish*. It is sung by eel-catchers when the traps are lifted, by ferrymen on the black water under Lastgate, by Seamers' apprentices bored at the tweezers, and by mothers over the cradle, although it is not a lullaby and every mother knows it is not. It has six verses. The seventh is hummed. Nobody knows if there was ever an eighth.

[fiction]
*The Ferrying Song, as sung on the Sump Landing. The first two verses are those printed in every Seamers' primer since 130 A.G.; the rest are given as Bram Hollin sings them, who learned them from a ferrywoman of Dunmere before it went silent.*
Down to the mud with the hook and the pot,
Nine days long and the meat not hot,
Grey as a stone and cold as a well,
And it smiled, my darling, and nobody tell.
Hand for the Grue and hand for the Vane,
Hand to be eaten and never again,
The face to the fen-folk, boiled in the peat,
And it smiled, my darling, and still we would eat.
Down to the mere with the punt and the pole,
Ferry the living and ferry the whole,
Ferry the hanged man who asked to come down,
And it smiled, my darling, as we poled him to town.
Grandmother's sewing and Grandfather's wire,
Never the water and never the fire,
Rot to the elbow and rot to the knee,
And it smiled, my darling, and so must we.
Lift up the trap, love, and count what you've caught,
Eels in the willow as thick as a thought,
Fat on the drowned folk who never lie still,
And it smiled, my darling, and it smiles still.
Pole to the line where the red paint is new,
Over go the living, and over go you,
Never the Kept, love, they'd drop on the shore,
And it smiled, my darling, and asked for no more.
*(hummed)*
[/fiction]

**The tune.** A slow, rocking triple time, the beat falling on the dip of the pole, in a minor mode that sits low in the throat and never quite resolves: every verse ends a step above the note it began on, so that the song seems to climb without going anywhere. The verses are sung by one voice, usually the eldest in the boat; the whole boat joins on *and it smiled, my darling*, which is sung softly, almost spoken, with a smile, because it is bad luck not to. The seventh verse is hummed by everyone, mouths shut, on the same climbing figure, and it does not end. In Sallowreach nothing ends. The hum goes on until the singer is interrupted, or hands it on.

**History.** The first two verses are older than the Stitching Laws and are almost certainly older than the Glad Century: they describe the Meal, the nine days of carving Ossel on the mere, the Hands divided between the great houses, and the face boiled by the far-fen Scraplings. The third verse remembers the First Morning, when the hanged thief at the Lastgate landing asked to be cut down. The fourth and fifth are Keeping verses, sung by the poor, and the sixth is the newest: it names the red line at Fogmouth called the Drop, renewed every Grace, across which the living may go and the Kept may not. Each generation has added a verse and none has added an ending.

Eel-catchers pass the hum from boat to boat across the mere at dusk, like a coal carried in a pot. Among the ferry families of the Sump it is said that one hum has been passed hand to hand since the First Morning and never once let fall, and that Tam Ruddock, the Lastgate Ferryman, is carrying it now: he hums it under his breath while he poles the dying of other lands across the border, and he has not stopped since his father put it in his mouth forty years ago. Grandfather Peat, the bog body from the Sump, does not hum the seventh verse when he is sung the Ferrying Song. He sings it, in his dead dialect, to a different figure with eight notes in it instead of seven, and the Seamers who have heard it say only that it is shorter than the others, and sounds like it is asking someone to sit down.

[pull] My father put the hum in my mouth when I was nine and said, don't drop it, Tam. I haven't. Some nights I think it's the only thing in the fen that's still alive. | — Tam Ruddock, the Lastgate Ferryman

**At the Table.** The Ferrying Song is a song of carrying. Sung in a boat or on the road by a company, it grants **+1 to Wayfaring and Labor** for the journey, as everyone falls into the pole's time. Sung to a Kept person, it calms them as nothing else does: **+2 to Persuade, Stitching or Rites** used on any Kept listener for the scene.

Inside Sallowreach, the song cannot end. When the six verses are done, one of the singers must carry the hum. That Guest hums under their breath, softly, all the time, suffering **−2 to Stealth** and to any roll that needs silence, until they **pass the hum** to another willing singer (who takes it in the same way) or leave the land. Stopping deliberately requires **Caul + Resolve at Hard (14)**; on a failure the hum goes on in the throat anyway, the jaw aching with it, and the Guest gains 1 Fray.

Outside Sallowreach, the hum ends like any other tune. A Sallowreacher who carries the hum across the border and lets it finish has *closed something* and may treat it as their land's **Sop**: remove 1d6 Hunger, and feel the Hand-god in the blood lean toward the next thing that could be finished. Any Kept Sallowreacher who hears the song finish (from just across the Drop, say) weeps, and asks to be told how it ends, and cannot be told.

Anyone, anywhere, who sings an eighth verse invented at the table hears Grandfather Peat's eight-note figure come back to them from the nearest dark water, in a voice that is not their own. **Dread 2.**

## The Lamb and the Lid: the Ludmere Lullaby

[sigil: vigil]

> It is said the lambs go first. It does not say where.
>> — Cutwright marginal note, Regard Street

The lullaby that sang a god to death. In the nine nights that Ludmere spent butchering Iss, four hundred and twelve children stood around the hollow in shifts of forty and sang it, softly, without stopping, so that the god would think itself still safe. It worked. Iss never woke. Three hundred and forty years later, on the First Night, every living descendant of those children went to the long room in their first sleep, whole lines at once, and the Watch made singing the lullaby a crime the following spring. It has been forbidden in the Vigil ever since. Singing or humming it costs a season in the Rattlehouse; a second offence, the Lantern. It was the one song permitted in the city on the days the condemned were Bedded, and since Bedding was suspended in 590 there has been no lawful day for it at all.

The Rattlehouse register of forbidden matter keeps the only copy the Watch permits to exist, and that copy has two verses. The Bedded, whose second rank is called the Hummers, know four.

[fiction]
*The Lamb and the Lid. The first two verses as entered in the Rattlehouse register; the third and fourth as sung in a Slope cellar by a Hummer of the Bedded and set down afterward by a Waker who was present, before he was relieved of his post.*
Hush now, lamb, the lid is low,
the long room's warm and the lamps burn slow;
lie you down where the sleepers keep,
and the eye won't open while the lambs still sleep.
Hush now, lamb, and count to three,
there's a bed for you and a bed for me;
and when the beds are full, my sweet,
the eye will open, and we'll all go to eat.
Hush now, lamb, the knife is wool,
the bowl is warm and the Lid is full;
nine nights long and the cream is sweet,
and the Lidded won't wake while the lambs still eat.
Hush now, lamb, the lamps are lit,
there's a chair at the end and nobody in it;
and when you wake, my sweet, my own,
*(the Hummers hum the last line. They say it has never been sung, because nobody who knew it was awake.)*
[/fiction]

**The tune.** A cradle-rock in a slow compound time, two dotted beats to the bar, the rhythm of a foot pushing a rocker. It is in the old Ludmere minor, which the Vigil's music-masters call the *drowsy mode*, and every phrase falls. The song is famous for its first three notes: a high note, a lower one, and a lower one still, sung on *hush now, lamb*, falling like a lid. Those three notes are the most dangerous sound in the Vigil. People cross the street rather than hear them whistled, and a tenement where someone hums them will be empty of its Wakers' patience within the bell. The song is sung by one voice, low, very close, almost into the ear; a crowd singing it together, as at the old Beddings, sounds like wind in a chimney.

**History.** Ludmere's children sang it for the Nine Nights, and were given warm milk between shifts to keep their voices sweet. The tune is older than the Gorging; the words were made for the occasion, and the third verse remembers the felt-wrapped knives and the cream-pale flesh of the god. Since the First Night it has been sung lawfully only at Beddings, by the crowd outside the Dormitory while the condemned lay down; unlawfully it is sung by the Bedded in their cellars, by mothers on the Slope who cannot help it, and by the old Ludmere houses of the Height on the Day of Nine Nights, who hum only the first three notes, over and over, while their eldest carves. Ansgar Wakely, condemned to a Bedding that could not be carried out, has hummed it on the lowest floor of the Rattlehouse for nineteen years.

The fourth verse is the Hummers' secret and their sorrow. Its last line has never been sung, they say, because nobody who knew it was awake. In the Dormitory, Matron Cecily Dorm has written down the words that Aveline Morne, the Sleeper in Bed One, has begun to shape with her lips. The Matron has not told anyone that one of the shapes, repeated night after night, is a line in the cradle-rock meter, and that it begins *and when you wake*.

[pull] Don't whistle that. Don't. Not even the first bit. My nan whistled the first bit once, on the stair, and the whole stair yawned, and Mrs Callow's boy went down on the landing and never got up. | — a woman of the Slope, to a Rim carter

**At the Table.** In the Vigil, the first three notes alone are a scene of comfort: every Vigilant who hears them gains **1 Weariness**, and must make a blink check if already Drifting. The whole song, sung through, calls for **Caul + Resolve at Grim (18)** from every listener; failure adds **3 Weariness** and an immediate blink check, and a listener at Grey (7+) when the song ends falls asleep where they stand. Vigil-born listeners suffer this wherever they are on the Table, because they have never slept and the song knows it. The singer is not exempt. A Hummer who sings it gains 2 Weariness and must roll like everyone else.

A foreigner who has never been to the Vigil hears only a pretty, sad lullaby, and may use it as any lullaby (the Vigil's own rules count lullabies as comfort for Weariness). Sung to a frightened child or a Guest who has just failed a Dread check, outside the Vigil, it removes **1 Fray**, and the listener dreams that night of a long dim room, warm, with lamps, and wakes rested and uneasy.

Sung in the Dormitory, the song does something else. Every Counted sleeper in earshot falls into its rhythm, sixty thousand chests rising and falling in compound time, and the sound of it is Dread 3. Anyone who reads Aveline Morne's lips while the fourth verse is sung learns the missing line. The Carver decides what it is. It should not be comforting. **Dread 4.**

[box: Carver's Note: Lullabies in the Vigil]
The Lamb and the Lid is a weapon. A Guest who learns it can put a Waker patrol into a blink, empty a street, or end a chase, at the cost of their own eyelids and a season in the Rattlehouse if they are caught. Let them. Then remind them that the Bedded know the song too, and the Second Table keeps a choir of twelve trained in it on the Height, and that both of those groups want the beds filled. Every time the song is sung in the Vigil, the Carver may roll a d10: on a 1, somewhere in the city, a sleeper is carried to the Dormitory who would otherwise have woken, and the number moves one closer.
[/box]

## The Hauling Song of the Beaching

[sigil: brinehollow]

> Haul and hold, and do not look.
>> — the Longslab hauling song

The great work song of the coast, sung by the starving lines that hauled Dromm ashore on ten thousand hooks, and still sung in the first week of Plenty on the Headland at Lowmark, where the Admiralty parades its dry fleet and the names of the seven Hook-Brides are read with honour. Every family keeps some fragment of it. The six lines printed in the Admiralty primers, and chanted by schoolchildren in the Mile, are not the song but its **burden**: the refrain sung between verses, one line to each heave on the rope. The song itself has eight verses. The sixth was struck from every written copy before 100 A.G., sometimes scored through so hard the pen went through the page, and on the Headland the crowd hums it.

[fiction]
*The Longslab hauling song, entire, as the Sounders of the Deepening teach it on the shell road at Low Water. The burden is sung after every verse. The struck verse is given as the Deepening gives it; the Admiralty denies that this is the verse, and will hang anyone who sings it to prove otherwise.*
*(Burden)* Haul and hold, haul and hold,
the deep is warm and the shore is cold.
Haul and hold, and lean you back,
the Fathom comes for the Lack, the Lack.
Haul and hold and do not look,
there's one of us on every hook.
*(I.)* Nine winters dry and the nets come thin,
who'll go down and bring him in?
Sounder, Sounder, go you down,
the Fathom's lying off the town.
*(II.)* Ten thousand hooks from the smokehouse wall,
gaff and anchor, set them all,
set them deep in the plate and slate,
the Fathom's come, and the Fathom's late.
*(III.)* Cold, it's coming from every seam,
the front ones drown and the front ones dream,
lean you back, though your mouth fill up,
the deep is wine and the shore's a cup.
*(IV.)* Mam at the front and she's gone to froth,
lay her down on the drying cloth,
take her place and take her rope,
haul for Mam and haul for hope.
*(V.)* He's on the bar and he won't come in,
the hooks are torn through the slate and skin,
Sounder, Sounder, what's he say?
A keeping wants a keepsake. Pay.
*(VI.)* Tamsin, Orry, Annick Fell,
Hob and Dennet, ring the bell,
Lowdy Kettle, Bran's own son,
tie the knot, and the knot is done;
walk the line to the plates, my sweet,
and we'll sing you in, and then we'll eat.
*(VII.)* The tide came up a fathom and a half,
the Sounders turned away to laugh,
haul and hold, he's off the bar,
and the brides are where the brides are.
*(VIII.)* Eight ropes down on the Longslab shore,
seven we hauled, and one hauled more;
don't you count and don't you look,
there's one of them on every hook.
[/fiction]

**The tune.** A heaving song in a heavy two, the stress landing hard on the first beat of every bar, where the whole line leans back on the rope together, and a long silent breath on the second, where they take up the slack. It is in a bright, hard major mode that has nothing of the sea's sadness in it, the mode of men and women too hungry to be sad. The verses are called by a single leader, traditionally a Sounder with bleeding ears; the lines answer with the burden, and a good hauling line can be heard a mile off. On the Headland the crowd sings the burden and the first five verses, then hums the sixth through closed lips (a low, tuneless drone, in which the old people of the coast can be heard weeping), and sings the seventh and eighth very quietly, as if they did not want to be overheard.

**History.** The first five verses tell the Beaching as the Admiralty teaches it: the empty nets, the Sounders going down, the hooks, the water pouring out of the god so that the haulers at the front drowned standing on dry shingle, and the god grounding on the bar on the third night. The struck sixth names the seven betrothed young people whom the Sounders asked the villages to give, tied each to a hook-line with a lover's knot, who walked out along the lines into the god's gills. The Admiralty's official history says the verse was struck from shame. The Deepening, who claim to have kept it in the mouths of the drowned, give the version above, whose last line says plainly what the Lung-line chronicles took two centuries to admit: that the founders found the seven in the god's lungs, in the dark, and ate them with the rest.

The seventh verse is sung everywhere and understood almost nowhere. Abiah Steep, the Purser's clerk who worked the tides back to the year 0 and was hanged for it in 215, wrote in his last letter that he had first grown suspicious because of the seventh verse: *the Sounders turned away to laugh*. Nobody laughs in a hauling song unless the song is telling you something.

The eighth verse is shorter in older versions and longer in newer ones, and in the oldest copy of all, a scrap of sail-canvas in the Hanging Abbey, it is only two lines: *eight ropes down on the Longslab shore, seven we hauled.* The frieze on the Sounding Hall shows eight lines of haulers, the eighth chiselled flat, its rope still carved running into the god. Children in the Mile skip to the burden's sixth line, *there's one of us on every hook*, and some of them, without being taught, sing it the eighth verse's way.

[pull] I've sung it on the Headland forty years. You hum the sixth and you sing the seventh and then everybody goes quiet for the eighth and you can hear the gulls. And every year there's one voice still singing when the rest have stopped. Every year. You never see whose. | — a Mile rope-maker

**At the Table.** As a work song, the hauling song gives **+2 to Labor and Athletics** on any group task of pulling, hauling, rowing or dragging, and on long tasks it reduces the time per roll by a quarter. In Brinehollow, a line of people singing the burden while hauling back a Called sleeper (with nets, ropes or bare hands) gains **+2** on the opposed roll; the Called seem, for a moment, to remember the rhythm, and to want to be one of the line.

Singing the burden is lawful. Singing the struck sixth verse aloud in Brinehollow is treason under the Admiralty's law of 215, and something more under the sea's. Every Brinehollower within earshot must make a **Caul + Resolve** check against the pull, **Hard (14)**, or **Grim (18)** on the Uncovered or at Low Water; failure means they turn to face west and stand, and at night they will walk. Every Tenanted person within a mile stops what they are doing, turns their head toward the singer, and listens with an expression of great courtesy. A Lung-line noble who hears it coughs up, with the next bailing, something that is not water, and does not show anyone.

Anyone who sings the eighth verse and is listening for it hears one more voice than there are singers. **Dread 2**, and the Carver notes the singer's name.

## The Wake-Songs of the Fatlands

[sigil: fatlands]

> We'll sit at board and say your name, and you'll be what we eat.
>> — the Board Song

There are no graves in the Fatlands. The dead are served at the wake, by the family, on the best plates, and every cut has its song. A wake begins at noon on the day after death, with a Bailiff's licence pinned to the door, and runs until the board is bare; the jointer leads the songs as he carves, and the family takes them up with their mouths full, so that a Fatlands wake heard from the lane sounds like a hymn sung under water. A child learns the cut-songs at its grandparents' wakes, and a jointer's apprentice is not let to carve a stranger until they have carved their own grandparent and sung every song over them.

The common wake-song, called **the Board Song**, is sung at the beginning and at the end, and between cuts whenever anyone weeps. It is given first. Then the songs of the cuts, in the order of a wake.

[fiction]
*The Board Song, sung across the plains while the copper boils.*
Remember, she was soft. Remember, she was sweet. Pass the plate along, along; there's not a bit to waste.
Remember, she was heavy, love. Remember, she was kind. Lick the bowl and crack the bone, there's marrow yet to find.
And when you're in the furrow, love, and when you're in the wheat, we'll sit at board and say your name, and you'll be what we eat.
[/fiction]

#### The Fat (the Rendering Song)

*Sung by the women of the house as the belly goes into the copper, while the fat goes clear; slow, sighing, in time with the bubbles. You start a man at the fat because the fat remembers the fire best.*

[fiction]
Into the copper, my dear, my dear,
the fire's been waiting for you all year;
go clear, go gold, go soft and slow,
and give us the warmth you'll not need below.
Skim off the temper and throw to the dog,
skim off the sorrow, the grudge and the grog;
what's left is the sweetness, what's left is the best,
we'll keep it in crocks and we'll eat all the rest.
[/fiction]

#### The Heart (the Eldest's Song)

*Sung by the eldest child alone, while searing the heart in its own fat, and not again in their lifetime. It is the only cut-song sung solo, and the only one in which the singer is permitted to cry openly; everyone else at a wake must cry while chewing.*

[fiction]
You kept me warm, you beat me fair,
you sat up nights at the end of my bed;
now I'll keep you warm in here,
and carry you on when the board is fed.
Thin as a leaf and dark as a plum,
I'll lay you in scales on the blue-rimmed plate;
whatever I am, it's what you've become,
and I'm hungry, Da. I'm hungry. Wait.
[/fiction]

#### The Hands (the Pie Song)

*For a person who worked with their hands. Sung by the whole kitchen while the crust is crimped. The hands go into a pie with onions and small purple turnips; the singers crimp in time, thumb by thumb.*

[fiction]
Hands that dug and hands that hoed,
hands that held the plough and goad,
fold them in and crimp them round,
hands go back to the ploughing ground.
Thumb for the furrow, thumb for the seed,
thumb for the hunger and thumb for the need,
a wheatsheaf of pastry to lay on the lid,
and nobody asks what the fingers once did.
[/fiction]

#### The Legs (the Spit Song)

*A turning-song for the spit, sung by the children of the house, who take turns at the handle; quick and skipping, the only cut-song that is any fun. Fatlands children sing it at play, turning a stick.*

[fiction]
Round and round and round he goes,
crackle on the knee and the knuckle and the toes,
walk to the market, walk to the shore,
he'll never walk to the Weighing no more!
Round and round and spit and spat,
blister on the shin and drip on the fat,
first one to the crackling gets the bit,
and the last one turns the spit!
[/fiction]

#### The Ribs (the Neighbours' Song)

*The ribs are the neighbours' portion, given out at the window to anyone from the lane who has brought bread. They sing it at the sill, holding the rib in both hands, before they eat; it is a song of thanks to the dead for having been good company.*

[fiction]
A rib for the lane and a rib for the street,
we never once quarrelled, we always would eat;
you lent me your ladder, I lent you my lard,
and now you're the best thing that's come down the yard.
[/fiction]

#### The Sweetbreads (the Jointer's Apology)

*The jointer's perquisite, fried in toothed-wheat batter and shared with whoever helped. Sung by the jointer alone, under the breath, while the knife goes in. The great jointer Hob Gristle sings it at every joint and not only this one.*

[fiction]
Sorry, old lad. Sorry, old lass.
It's only the knife, and the knife will pass.
I'll take what's mine and I'll give what's yours,
and I'll wash the board, and I'll shut the doors.
Sorry, old lad. I knew your face.
I'll say it again at the next one's place.
[/fiction]

#### The Marrow (the Beloved's Song)

*The marrow goes to whoever the dead loved best. Everyone at the board sings it, turning to look at that person, who must eat the marrow from the cracked bone while the song is sung to them, without stopping, to the end. It is the most dreaded honour in the south. Where the most beloved is disputed, families have come to blows over who must eat it, and who may.*

[fiction]
Who did she love the best of all?
Who did she call for at the end?
Give them the bone and let them eat,
the deepest sweet, the deepest sweet.
Suck it out and suck it clean,
the softest thing she's ever been;
now she's in you, root and stem,
and you'll be served to us like them.
[/fiction]

#### The Bones (the Field Song)

*The last song of a wake. The bones are boiled for stock, then ground fine and carried out to the family's field the next morning. The family walks the furrows scattering the meal and singing to the field by name, as at a grace, and the field is said to answer.*

[fiction]
Long Acre, Long Acre, we bring her to you,
the meal of her, fine as the dust and the dew;
take her and keep her and grow her up green,
and we'll eat her again, as she's eaten and been.
Long Acre, Long Acre, we've given her back,
so stay you away from us, Lack, Lack, Lack.
[/fiction]

There is no song for the tongue. In the plains it is eaten last, in silence, by whoever the dead last quarrelled with, so that the quarrel is finished. Hob Gristle says that in forty years of wakes the tongue has never once gone uneaten, and that he has never seen anyone eat it who looked as if they had won.

**The tunes.** Every Fatlands wake-song is in a bright, warm major, in a steady four-square beat that can be kept with spoons on plates, and most are set to tunes also used for harvest and courting. Foreigners find this the worst thing about a wake. The Fat is a slow sigh; the Heart is the only one in a minor mode, and is half spoken; the Spit Song skips; the Marrow climbs on each line and ends on a long held note while the beloved finishes eating. The Field Song is sung in the open in the early morning, and on still days the people of the plains swear the furrows hum the last line back.

**History.** The Board Song is old, older than the Wake Ordinance of 131 A.G. that made the eating of the dead the law of the land. The cut-songs grew up through the Sweet Reigns, as the wake became the most sacred rite of the south, and the Renderers' Union printed the canonical set on a broadsheet in 302 which still hangs in every Wakeside kitchen. In the year of the Long Count, the songs began to change in the far villages of Low Tilth. The Field Song acquired a new last line there, *and if you come for us at dusk, take only what is due*, and the Board has twice sent Bailiffs to discover who wrote it and twice been told: the field did.

Marigold Chine, who sings to the oldest grove in the Bleeding Orchards, has heard her trees sing the Marrow, in her own voice, with her own name in it.

[pull] The Heart song's the worst. You sing it once in your life, and you're holding your own father's heart in a pan, and you have to sing *I'm hungry*, and you are. Everybody is listening, because they all sang it once too. | — a cooper of Barrelside

**At the Table.** A wake at which every cut-song is properly sung counts as a full rite of mourning: **the Pall of the village falls by 1d3**, and Guests who sing with the family are treated thereafter as kin in that parish (+2 to Persuade and Haggle with its people for a season). A non-Fatlander who sings and eats suffers the usual Dread for eating at a wake but gains **+2** on the check, because the song carries them.

A Fatlander who walks the furrows singing the Field Song over a kinsman's meal may treat it as their **Sop** (remove 1d6 Hunger), and feels the soil rise under their feet to meet them; on a Tooth of 1 that day, the field takes a little more than bone-meal, and the Guest finds one fingernail gone when they get home, with no blood.

The cut-songs have a darker use. A cut-song sung **to a living person** is understood in every village of the south. The Marrow sung to someone is a declaration of love so absolute it is barely distinguishable from a threat; the Heart sung to a parent is a curse, meaning *I am waiting*; the Spit Song sung under a window is how the Cullmasters' men tell a household that one of them has been put on the dusk shift. A Guest who has a cut-song sung to them in the Fatlands faces **Dread 1**, or Dread 2 if it is the Spit Song and they understand it.

## The Kest Counting-Song

[sigil: cradlewrack]

> One to ten, the door is shut, and I am in, and you are out.
>> — the first line every highland child learns, before its name, which waits

The hymn every child in Cradlewrack knows before it knows its letters, sung during the pang, one line to each ten counts. The pangs come every nine days now, a long rolling quake from the deep ground that walks the crockery off the shelves and makes every Course-touched person within a hundred miles cry out, and they last for sixty seconds. For sixty seconds the whole country sings. Families gather in the strongest room of the house, hold hands, and count the quake down; the bricked in the Debt Wall count it through their slots; the Foundling Companies count it on the drill-ground at Kest, in step; the watchers at the Dilation count it at the rim of the great crowning, a thousand voices, and the hole breathes with them.

[fiction]
*The Kest Counting-Song, as the Attendance teaches it, sung through a pang.*
One to ten, the door is shut, and I am in, and you are out.
Ten to twenty, knock and wait, the Holder holds, the hour is late.
Twenty, thirty, roll and rise, Mother, Mother, shut my eyes.
Thirty, forty, down and through, whatever comes, it isn't you.
Forty, fifty, nearly done, the waters come for everyone.
Fifty, sixty, still, still, still, and if it stays, it stays, it will.
*The Going Back, sung only by the Second Opinions of the Attendance, after a birth they have not permitted, and by one voice in the Debt Wall, all day:*
Sixty, fifty, look and see, what it is and what it'll be.
Fifty, forty, ask it plain, and ask it once, and not again.
Forty, thirty, apron tied, the Opinion's on the other side.
Thirty, twenty, hush and hold, the water's warm, the clay is cold.
Twenty, ten, the door is wide, and I am out, and you're inside.
Ten to one, and one to none, whatever came, it's come and gone.
[/fiction]

**The tune.** Hardly a tune: a chant on a single note that rises one whole step every ten counts, so that the first line is sung low and steady, in a mother's speaking voice, and by *fifty, sixty* the voices are high and strained and thin, like the cry of a woman at the end of her strength. Each line takes exactly ten heartbeats, and the singers count the tens on their fingers, folding one down for each count, so that at sixty every hand in the room is two fists. Then silence, and the hands open. The Going Back descends the same steps, one whole tone every ten, ending low, almost under the breath, on *gone*. Midwives sing it with their eyes shut.

**History.** The counting-song is as old as the pangs, which is to say it was first sung when the quakes still came a season apart and the highlanders used to count them out of curiosity. It became a hymn in the Year of Doors, 310 A.G., when every lock in the country failed in one night, and its first line took on its present meaning: the door is shut, I am in, you are out, whatever you are. The Going Back is the Attendance's, and was never meant to be heard outside a lying-in room. It is sung by the Second Opinion over a birth that has been answered *no* to the second question, to the end of the count, while the knife does its work; the Attendance holds that nothing should leave the world in less time than a pang. In the Debt Wall at Kest, one of the bricked sings the Going Back all day, from one to sixty and back again, and the slot-woman who feeds it bread has never asked the family who it is, because the family pays.

When the Mothers of the Walk of Aprons went down the ninety-one steps into the Dilation in 619, one at the start of each pang, the crowd counted them down, and at sixty the steps were empty. Lark Ninedays, who times the pangs for the state with a water clock and a bell, has entered in her private ledger that the last pang of Carving 640 lasted **sixty-one** seconds by the clock. There is no line in the counting-song for sixty-one. In the silence at the end of it, she says, every family in Kest was still holding hands with their mouths open, waiting for a line that did not come, and from the Dilation, a long way off, the watchers heard a single knock.

[pull] On sixty you open your hands. On sixty-one you look at them. Nobody ever told us what to do on sixty-one. | — a night-mason of Mortar Rise

**At the Table.** A company that sings the counting-song through a pang together, holding hands, gains **+2 on all rolls to endure the pang**: Endure, Athletics to keep footing, and Caul + Resolve against what the waters bring up. When the land's rule calls for a roll to see whether healed injuries or stitched seams reopen at the pang, each singer may **reroll one d10**. Doors in the room do not drift open during a pang while the song is being sung; the moment it stops, they do.

The Going Back is not a hymn but a sentence. Sung by a midwife over a birth, it is the Attendance's lawful killing, and a Guest who hears it sung in a lying-in room has witnessed a ritual killing (**Dread 3**). Sung by anyone else, over anyone else, it is a threat every highlander understands.

If a pang lasts past sixty while a Guest is counting (the Carver may decide this happens once, as the interval shortens, or roll a d10 at each pang from Carving 641 on: on a 1, it lasts sixty-one), every singer must make a **Dread 2** check, and every door within sight, bricked or not, opens one finger's width and stays open.

## Oathen Handtalk Poems

[sigil: oathen]

> Fingers do not count. Everyone knows fingers do not count.
>> — said in Tacit, more and more often, by people who no longer believe it

Handtalk is the sign language of Oathen, used for everything that matters little, and held not to bind. Its oldest signs are the signs of the butchery: *hold*, *cut*, *pull*, *more hands here*, *it is looking at me*, *do not stop*. Because it did not bind, it was for six centuries the only language in Oathen in which a person could exaggerate, flirt, joke, curse, or make poetry, and the canyons made a great deal of poetry. A handtalk poem is a sequence of signs performed in silence, with the face still and the hands close to the chest, before a small audience who answer by touching their own lips with two fingers. It cannot be written down without being changed, because the signs carry their speed, their size and their distance from the heart; the poems below are given as sequences, with the shape of each sign and a gloss, as the Quiet record them for teaching.

The greatest handtalker alive is Mirren Osk. Last year she signed a love poem and did not mean it, and three of her fingers snapped. Since then, nobody in Tacit signs a poem without thinking first. That is the end of an art. The canyons know it.

[fiction]
*1. Figs. A children's poem, the first that is taught; harmless, or once was. Four signs.*
The right hand cupped, the left thumb pressed into its palm, once, softly (*a fig, ripe*).
The flat hand raised above the brow, fingers spread, and lowered slowly to the chest (*the sun; it is late in the day*).
Both hands palms-down before the body, parting outward (*to share; to divide*).
The shoulders raised, the fingers spread and held still, the eyes lowered (*if able*).
*Gloss: A ripe fig. The day is ending. I would share it with you, if able.*
[/fiction]

[fiction]
*2. The Butchery. The oldest poem in the canyons, and the oldest signs. Signed by the Quiet on Tablenight, when Oathen makes no sound, all through the night, by the whole order together in the Hall of Conditions. Six signs, repeated.*
Both fists closed and pulled to the chest (*hold*).
The edge of the right hand drawn down the left forearm, slowly, from elbow to wrist (*cut*).
Both fists drawn back over the shoulder, hard, together (*pull*).
The open right hand beckoning twice, toward the floor (*more hands here*).
Two fingers pointed at the signer's own eyes, then turned outward, then held still, trembling (*it is looking at me*).
The flat right hand laid across the mouth, then lifted away and pressed flat on the heart (*do not stop*).
*Gloss: none is given. The Quiet say the poem is the gloss.*
[/fiction]

[fiction]
*3. Coming to the Hearing, by an unknown pilgrim-poet, before 300 A.G. Signed at the Night of Ears, on the eve of Tablenight, by pilgrims lying with their ears to the warm sand of the Gullet. Seven signs.*
Two fingers walking across the left palm, slowly, a long way (*the walk; a whole day*).
The cupped hand held to the ear, and the head tilted into it (*listening*).
The right hand closed into a fist and laid on the tongue, then opened downward (*a true sentence, given*).
The fist on the tongue again, and kept there (*it was eaten*).
The cupped hand held to the ear, and the head tilted away from it (*it is listening from inside*).
The flat hand laid on the stomach and held (*here*).
The flat hand laid on the stomach, and the signer bowing over it until the brow touches the hand (*forgive*).
*Gloss: We walked a day to its ear. We gave it true sentences. We ate it. Now it listens from in here. Forgive us; we cannot stop talking to you.*
[/fiction]

[fiction]
*4. The Breakdowns Poem, attributed to Brakk, Voice of the Forsworn, who has nothing left to break; performed on the canyon floor by the Forsworn for coins, at speed, with great vulgarity. It is the only handtalk poem that is also a curse, and Oatheners who watch it laugh with their hands over their mouths. Five signs.*
Both hands opening outward from the breastbone like the covers of a book (*my ribs*).
The right forefinger stabbing at the Sunward Wall, where the Bench sits (*you*).
The sign for *if able*, done mockingly, with the shoulders up to the ears and the tongue out.
Both fists brought together, then pulled apart with a jerk (*broken; Forsworn*).
The hand turned palm up and flicked toward the Wall, fingers snapping out (*soon*).
*Gloss: Look at my ribs. You, up there with your if-ables: you'll be one of us soon.*
[/fiction]

[fiction]
*5. Mirren Osk's last poem, the love poem of 640. Only the first three signs are given. The Quiet will not teach the rest, and Mirren signs nothing now.*
The right forefinger pointed at the listener, held out at arm's length (*you*).
The right hand laid flat over the signer's own breastbone and pressed until the knuckles whiten (*heart; mine; given*).
Both hands circling each other, slowly, without end (*always*).
*The rest of the poem is eleven signs long. Those who saw it performed say that it was the most beautiful thing ever made in the canyons, and that she held her hands quite still afterward, and that the sound of the fingers going was like three dry twigs, one, two, three.*
[/fiction]

**The tune.** Handtalk poems have no tune and make no sound, but they have a meter, and a good handtalker is judged by it: each sign held for a heartbeat, the pauses between signs counted, the whole poem performed in one breath held and released at the end, so that the audience sees the poet's chest fall. Poems for the Quiet are signed slowly, large, from the shoulder. Love poems are signed small, close to the heart, almost hidden in the lap. Curses are signed fast.

**History.** Handtalk was born on the night of the Silent Supper from the signs the butchers made to each other over the god, and its poetry flowered in the Honest Age, when Oathen grew rich on trust and needed somewhere to put everything it could not say. The great handtalk poets were the first Clausewrights' children, raised under the bit until twelve, who learned to say everything with their hands before they had ever spoken. Since last year, when Mirren Osk's fingers broke, the Echo-Wardens have begun to log handtalk in the deep rock, the click and slap of fingers echoing in gorges where fingers make no sound, and a scribe has found that written words are beginning to bind as well. If the Regrowth is widening what Tolm counts, the poetry of the canyons is about to become law.

[pull] A poem is a promise you make to no one about nothing. That was the definition. I taught it to children for thirty years. I have stopped teaching it. | — a schoolmistress of Low Dray, in writing

**At the Table.** Performing a handtalk poem for an Oathener, well, is the highest compliment a foreigner can pay in the canyons: **+2 to Persuade and Clause** with that person for the scene, and the respect of anyone watching. The Breakdowns Poem performed among the Forsworn makes a Guest a friend of the Breakdowns for a night; performed anywhere near a Clausewright, it is an insult that will be repaid in forty pages.

Handtalk now binds a little. In Oathen, an Oathen-blooded Guest at **Regrowth 4 or more** who performs a poem that asserts or promises something they do not mean must roll **Caul + Resolve at Hard (14)**, or one finger breaks (1 Flesh, and −1 to Hand rolls until it is set and healed). A Guest at the Course breaks one finger for each sign that was false, automatically. Within the Gullet, the Ledger, or any posted silent gorge, handtalk counts as speech for echoes, and the Echo-Wardens will want to know what was signed.

Anyone who learns the remaining eleven signs of Mirren Osk's poem (she alone knows them; a Hard Tongue + Persuade and a reason she would believe) and performs them meaning every one is bound by it as by a spoken Weight 3 oath of love, to the person they signed it to, for life.

## The Long Decline

[sigil: fast]

> No, thank you.
>> — the response, sung after every verse

The only hymn the Unfed sing. It is sung at the Laying-Down, when one of the Unfed is buried, and on Tablenight, when every person in the Fast stands outdoors from dusk to dawn with their back to every door; it is sung unaccompanied, very low, almost on one note, by people who do not have the breath to sing. Its words are older than Wanting. It has eight verses. The Unfed sing seven.

[fiction]
*The Long Decline, the seven sung verses, as sung on the walls of Wanting.*
We stood on the hill in the snow, and it was laid for us.
No, thank you.
We stood on the hill, and the ones in front lay down, and we stood on them.
No, thank you.
The god lay on the stone with its throat bare, and smiled, and said, you must be starved.
No, thank you.
We were starved. We said it with our mouths full of nothing.
No, thank you.
The god wept, and the snow went soft, and some of us drank it.
No, thank you.
On the tenth morning it got down from the stone and bowed to us.
No, thank you.
It is still laying the table. It is still asking. We are still here.
No, thank you.
[/fiction]

**The tune.** A recitation on a single low note, the verse spoken-sung by a lone voice (at a Laying-Down, the dead one's nearest kin; on Tablenight, the eldest on each wall) and the response sung by everyone, on two notes only: the low note, and a note a half-step above it on *thank*, which sounds less like music than like a starving person's breath catching. The Unfed have no instruments. Some Roll-Keepers say the response was once sung three times after every verse, in the proper form of the decline, and that it was shortened when there were no longer enough people on the hill with breath for the third.

**History.** The Long Decline is the Unfed's account of Hallowboard, compressed to seven lines: the god laid out on the eight-sided stone, the front rows lying down in the snow, the second rows standing on them, the god's warm tears, the tenth morning when Orrum got up off the table, and the six hundred and forty-one years since. It is sung at the Laying-Down because every death in the Fast is the Refusal repeated; it is sung on Tablenight because on that night, in every house in the Fast, behind the backs of the people standing outdoors, the table is laid. In 559, when Wanting walked nine of its own out to the edge of the warm ground in the Giving, the town stood on its walls with its backs to the west and sang it, and the Measure-Bearers rang their bells three times and walked back, and the nine walked on.

The eighth verse is not sung. Most Unfed do not know it exists. The Roll-Keepers of Barrowgate know it, and recite it only to one another, on Tablenight, with their backs to the stone. A Hostwatch Cartographer who overheard it once wrote down only that it is the shortest verse, that it does not end with the Decline, and that it begins *And then there was one more*.

[box: Carver's Secret: The Eighth Verse]
The Roll-Keepers' eighth verse, for the Carver's eyes, is this:
*And then there was one more, on the side where the snow did not settle, and it said: Thank you.*
It is not followed by the response. It does not need one. The Unfed who know it believe it records a stranger at Hallowboard who accepted, which is the worst thing they can imagine, and they do not know who it was, and they do not want to. Do not explain it. If a Guest ever hears it, let them count the sides of the table.
[/box]

[pull] On Tablenight you stand with your back to the door and you sing seven, and you hear the plates go down behind you in the empty house, one by one. Seven verses. You count the plates. That's all. You just count the plates. | — a Bell Ward woman, to a Rim visitor

**At the Table.** For the Unfed, singing the Long Decline at a Laying-Down or on Tablenight counts as saying the Decline at a deathbed: **+1 Want**, and 1 Fray. A company that sings it together while facing one of Orrum's laid tables (with their backs to it, as is proper) gains **+2 on Invitation checks** for the scene; the smell of bread thins, and the chair, very slightly, pushes itself back in. Godeater Guests who sing the response along with the Unfed may also gain this bonus, and an Unfed who hears a Godeater sing *No, thank you* properly, three times, on the fourth accepting a hand or a cup, will think a little better of them, for a day.

The eighth verse, said aloud anywhere on the Table, is **Dread 3**. Every one of Orrum's tables within a mile acquires a place card with the speaker's name on it, and the Hostwatch report that wherever it is said, the Host itself stops walking, wherever it is, and stands very still, the way a man stands in a dark house when he hears a floorboard. For one night it lays no tables at all. The Carver should treat that as the most frightening thing in this book.

## Over, Under: the Seamers' Stitching Song

[sigil: sallowreach]

> Over for the ones who stand, under for the ones who sit; pull it tight for Gran, my love, and don't you look at it.
>> — the burden

The work song of the Seamers' Guild, sung in every stitching-shop on Needle Street and in every kitchen in Sallowreach during Restitching Week, when grandmothers are restitched each spring and the Seamers do a year's trade in a fortnight. It is sung in the rhythm of the needle: in on the first beat, through on the second, pull on the third, and the knot on the fourth. Apprentices learn the count of a running stitch, a whip stitch, a wire-tie and a lacquer-seal from the verses, and a Threader can tell from across the shop, by the singing, which of her apprentices is going too fast.

[fiction]
*Over, Under, as sung in the Guildhall on Needle Street on the first day of Restitching Week.*
Over for the ones who stand, under for the ones who sit,
pull it tight for Gran, my love, and don't you look at it.
Here's the thread of the Seamers' Guild, waxed and black and strong,
it held her when the wire went, it'll hold her all along.
Over for the ones who stand, under for the ones who sit,
pull it tight for Gran, my love, and don't you look at it.
Here's the jaw that's working loose, here's the hip that's gone,
whip it round and tie it off, and lacquer it, and on.
Over for the ones who stand, under for the ones who sit,
pull it tight for Gran, my love, and don't you look at it.
Thessaly Mort has a ledger, and the ledger's very short:
what was hers and what was ours, and what was simply bought.
Over for the ones who stand, under for the ones who sit,
pull it tight for Gran, my love, and if she screams, then quit.
[/fiction]

**The tune.** A brisk four-square beat, every stroke of the needle on the beat, sung in a cheerful, clipped major by a shopful of voices; the tune is the old Lastgate fishwives' reel slowed down to a working pace. The last line of the burden changes at the end of the song, as above, and the change is always sung softer, almost a mumble. Apprentices sing the last line louder for a joke, once, and are not let to sew for a day.

**History.** The song dates from the Stitching Laws of 130 A.G., when the state took responsibility for keeping the dead in one piece and chartered the Seamers' Guild; its verses have been added to by every generation of Threaders since, and there are said to be more than two hundred of them, of which a shop will know fifty. The verse about Thessaly Mort, who has replaced so much of herself that her ledger of original parts is very short, is recent and sung only when she is not in the room. She knows it. She has, it is said, added a verse of her own, which she sings alone, after hours, over a part of her own body that she is replacing, and nobody has heard the words.

The changed last line, *and if she screams, then quit*, is the only mercy in the Seamers' rule: a Kept person under the needle who screams is not to be sewn further that day, whatever the fee. In the Guildhall this is called the Grandmother's Clause. In the back-alley shops where Marrow Jack sells his stolen limbs, it is not observed.

**At the Table.** Singing Over, Under while stitching (a wound, a Kept body, a sail, a sack) grants **+1 to Stitching** and, on long tasks, one free Helping toward the total. A Seamer who hears a stranger sing it correctly, with the right last line, will assume they were apprenticed and treat them as a member of the trade (+2 to Persuade and Haggle with Seamers). A Kept person being restitched to the song suffers half the Fray they otherwise would from the procedure.

## Pinch Me: the Nudgers' Round

[sigil: vigil]

> Pinch me.
>> — the Vigil's commonest endearment, and its marriage vow

The street-children of the Vigil, hired by the hour to pinch strangers awake, sing this round as they work, in pairs and threes on the Slope's stairs, and it is the only cheerful sound that city makes after the bells. It is a round: the second voice comes in when the first reaches the second line, the third at the third, and the round can go on as long as there are voices and breath, which in the Vigil is a long time. A pinch is given on the last word of every line, to whoever is nearest.

[fiction]
*Pinch Me, as sung by the nudgers on Wicker Stair; a round in four parts.*
Pinch me once and I'm awake, *(pinch)*
pinch me twice for the city's sake, *(pinch)*
pinch me three and I'll pinch you back, *(pinch)*
lids up, lamps lit, Lack, Lack, Lack! *(slap)*
Bell for the quarter, bell for the hour,
rouse in the cup and the cup gone sour,
needle for Waker and pin for the guest,
and nobody, nobody, nobody rest!
[/fiction]

**The tune.** A quick, bright, jigging tune in a high register, made to carry over street noise and bells, sung by children's voices in a strict round of four. When it is going well, the four parts overlap so that there is always someone singing *pinch*, and someone being pinched, and the whole stair is a ripple of small sharp hurts and laughter. When a child in a round blinks, the round falters on her line, and the others hear the gap. That is what the round is for.

**History.** Nobody knows who made Pinch Me; the nudgers say it is older than rouse, which makes it older than 390 A.G. It is taught by nudger to nudger, and every child on the Slope knows it by five. The Wakers hate it, because it mocks them in the second verse, and love it, because it works. Pip Eleven-Bell, nine years old and two weeks unpaid, sings the round on the Slope stairs with two others. Lately the other two have been coming in early on her line, to cover the gap.

**At the Table.** A company singing Pinch Me as a round, with at least three voices, may remove **1 Weariness from every singer** once per scene, as a nudger's pinch (the land's rule allows one such removal per scene; the round counts as it). If any singer is at Grey (7+), the round may also stand in for their blink check for that scene: the singers roll it together, using the best Caul + Resolve among them, because the gap in the round wakes the blinker before the blink is over. On a Lack, the round has gone on with one fewer voice for some time and nobody noticed when.

## Hook Him Gentle: the Netwatch Shanty

[sigil: brinehollow]

> Subject appeared expected.
>> — the standard phrase of the Netwatch logs

The Netwatch, the Tidal Admiralty's night patrol, hunts the Called along the shore and across the Uncovered with nets, hooks and rope, and drags them home. They sing as they go, low, in a shanty rhythm, and they sing for two reasons. The first is pace: the Called walk west at a steady unhurried gait, and the song keeps a patrol to the same gait, so that a hooker can come up beside a sleepwalker without startling them into the sea. The second reason is the one the officers do not put in the training: the Called are listening to something, an invitation, and a song sung close to them in their own coast's tongue covers it, for a few breaths, enough to get the rope on.

[fiction]
*Hook Him Gentle, the patrol-song of the Netwatch, as sung on the Raw Mile. The first lines are called by the lantern-bearer; the rest is answered by the patrol.*
Lantern up and lantern low, (*hook him gentle*)
there's pink prints on the salt below, (*hook him gentle*)
walk him easy, walk him slow,
the sea's not where it used to go.
Hook him gentle, hook him kind,
he's left his shoes and his name behind;
tie him off at the wrist and knee,
and haul him home from the empty sea.
She walks with her face to the west wind, warm, (*hook her gentle*)
like a girl going home from the harvest farm, (*hook her gentle*)
don't you shout and don't you call,
she can't hear you, love, at all.
Hook her gentle, hook her kind,
she's left her shoes and her name behind;
lay her down on the drying rack,
and if she weeps, don't give her back.
[/fiction]

**The tune.** A slow walking shanty in two, at exactly the pace of a sleepwalker on salt-crust, in a dark Dorian mode that every Brinehollower hears as homesick. The lantern-bearer calls; the patrol answers *hook him gentle* (or *her*) between lines, under the breath, and joins in full on the second half of each verse. It is never sung loudly. Netwatch patrols on the Uncovered can be tracked across a mile of dark by the song alone, a murmur coming and going on the wind, and the scavengers out there call it *the gentle* and keep clear.

**History.** The shanty is as old as the Netwatch, which was founded after the First Calling of 118 A.G., when four hundred walked into the sea at Lowmark and eleven came back. It was adapted from a hauling tune of the old fishing fleets, before the sea left; the old verses were about bringing in a catch, and the Netwatch changed *it* to *him* and *her*. The last line, *and if she weeps, don't give her back*, is the Netwatch's whole doctrine in seven words. Lieutenant Sable Reef, who has pulled three hundred Called off the shore with hook and rope, sings it as well as anyone in the service. Her patrol has noticed that she has started to leave off the last line.

**At the Table.** A patrol singing Hook Him Gentle together gains **+2 to resist the pull** (as a Caul + Resolve check against the Calling) for the night, and **+2 to Stealth** when approaching a Called sleepwalker, who will not turn until the rope is on. A Called person who hears the shanty sung close to them hesitates: the Carver gives the hookers one free action before the first opposed roll. The last line is optional. A Guest who leaves it off, as Sable Reef does, and lets a weeping Called person walk on into the dark, gains 1 Fray, and the household's Pall rises by 2 as for a loss to the Pull; a Guest who sings it and keeps the rope tight on someone begging to be let go gains the same 1 Fray, and the household's thanks, and must live with both.

## May Your Door Stand Open: a Highland Curse

[sigil: cradlewrack]

> You knock to warn whatever is on the other side.
>> — highland teaching on manners

In Cradlewrack it is the gravest rudeness to open a door without knocking, and the commonest curse is a wish that every door stays open. This is the formal version, a cursing-song chanted under a neighbour's lintel at night by a wronged woman (it is almost always a woman, since descent and grievance run through whoever bore you), with a nail in her fist, tapping the door once on every line. A cursed household will hear the tapping and lie still until it is over, because to answer it is to open the door.

[fiction]
*May Your Door Stand Open, as cursed in Rudge, where a well once gave birth; given by a Tallyman's clerk who heard it under his own lintel.*
May your door stand open and your lock come undone, (*tap*)
may your bricks fall outward, every one, (*tap*)
may your wounds come open on the pang, (*tap*)
and the waters bring up what you threw in the dark, (*tap*)
may you bear a hand, and the hand be mine, (*tap*)
may it knock at your bed at the turn of the night, (*tap*)
may you ask the Two Questions and get no reply, (*tap*)
and may it stay. And may it stay. And may it stay. (*tap, tap, tap*)
[/fiction]

**The tune.** A monotone chant on a low note, rhythmless except for the tapping of the nail, which falls on the last syllable of every line like a knock. The last line is spoken, not chanted, three times, each time quieter, and the three taps that follow are the loudest: three hard knocks, the traditional warning given before opening a door onto something unknown. The curser does not open the door. She walks away. That is the point.

**History.** The cursing-song dates from the Year of Doors, 310 A.G., when every lock in the country failed in one night and the custom of bricking up rooms began; its sixth line is newer, from after the Gathering of Parts in 500 A.G., when it was first noticed that Parts from unrelated families fit each other. The Attendance has outlawed it twice and stopped trying, because the midwives use it themselves. Agnes Latch, the Doorwright who builds doors meant to stay shut, guarantees her best work against it; her best work held for nineteen days.

**At the Table.** A Cradlewracker cursed with the song (by an NPC with a real grievance, or by a Guest who has one) suffers **−1 to all rolls to keep a door, lock, bricked room or wound shut** until the curse is lifted by the curser's knocking three times on the door again and walking away, or by the next Unbricking. The cursed household's doors open by themselves at night, a finger's width. A Guest who curses someone with it, and means it, gains 1 Hunger and must make a **Caul + Resolve** check at Routine (10); on a failure the Guest goes into false labour that night, of any age or sex, as the Appetite rehearses, and the body is very sure of what it would like to bear.

## What Goes Down: the Purgation's Emetic Hymn

[sigil: rim]

> What goes down can come up.
>> — painted on the Purgation's altar-bowls

The Purgation believes the gods can be thrown back up. At the Weeping Post on Retching Day, and in its halls the length of the Rim, its Penitents kneel in rows before their bowls and drink the Black Draught, a purgative of bitter roots, salt and lamp-oil, and then they retch, for hours, while the Purgers walk the rows with the bleeding-cup, and the whole hall sings. The hymn is set to the rhythm of vomiting. That is not a figure of speech. Its lines are short, its breaths are placed where the body heaves, and the Penitents are taught to bring the god up on the downbeat.

[fiction]
*What Goes Down, the hymn of the Black Draught, as sung at the Weeping Post on Retching Day. The Purger calls the lines; the Penitents answer on the heave.*
What went down, (*come up*)
in the dark of the Lack, (*come up*)
in the nine days' meat, (*come up*)
give it back. (*come up*)
What we stole, (*come up*)
from the stone and the shore, (*come up*)
from the field and the bed, (*come up*)
we'll take no more. (*come up*)
Bitter root, (*come up*)
and the salt, and the oil, (*come up*)
turn the belly out (*come up*)
like a ploughman turns soil. (*come up*)
Bowl, bowl, (*come up*)
take what we bring, (*come up*)
and if it is grey, (*come up*)
then bless the grey thing. (*come up*)
[/fiction]

**The tune.** A call-and-response in a slow, labouring three, the third beat left empty for the heave. The Purger calls on a high, clear, almost sweet note; the response, *come up*, is not sung at all by the end of the first hour but forced out, a choked rising grunt from two hundred throats at once, followed by the sound that gives the hymn its rhythm. Visitors who hear a Purgation hall at service from the road say it sounds like a field of animals being butchered slowly, in time. Penitents who have been through it say that after the third hour it begins to sound beautiful.

**History.** The hymn was first sung in 233 A.G. at the Great Retching, when Hurlo Sweyn, a Fatlands physician, drank his own draught before a congregation of Confessors at the Weeping Post and, after six hours of retching, brought up a hard white thing the size of a thumbnail which he swore was one of Ummer's teeth. The congregation sang him through it. The fourth verse, *if it is grey, then bless the grey thing*, was added in the following century, when the Purgation's halls began to find that what came up, now and then, was godflesh: a sliver of grey, a fleck of loam, a curl of caul. The Voided, the inner council, have known for a hundred years that they sell those slivers to the cure-shops and the Second Table, and the hymn's fourth verse has paid for a great many halls.

**At the Table.** A Penitent who drinks the Black Draught with the hymn sung over them gains **+2 to Gut + Endure** against the retching (normally Hard 14 or lose 1d6 Flesh). If the Penitent has Regrowth 4 or more, the Carver rolls a d10 at the end of the service: on a 1–2, something comes up, a sliver of the Penitent's god, and they lose 1d6 Hunger and 1 Flesh and gain 1 Fray. It purges nothing of the Regrowth. It is worth, to the right buyer on the Rim, between 50 and 500 lacks, and the Purgers will offer to take it off the Penitent's hands, as a kindness. The hymn is also a method of the **Long Night** for building Hold: a Guest who attends three services in a week with the Purgation suffers −2 to resist their Hold, because by the third hour of the third night the hymn does begin to sound beautiful.

## It Worked Once: the Second Table's Toast

[sigil: eighth]

> It worked once.
>> — the whole of the Second Table's creed, engraved inside its members' signet rings

The Second Table, the hidden cult of nobles who intend to eat the gods again, meets a dozen at a time in private dining rooms on the Rim and in the cellars of great houses, and every dinner ends with this toast. It is spoken, not sung, in seven parts, one for each course, each part said by the member who holds that course's name: the Fish, the Bread, the Sweet, the Lid, the Word, the Hand, the Host. The glasses are raised at each course and lowered without drinking. They are drunk only at the end, all together, in silence. The toast is the one thing the Second Table says aloud that is in any sense true, and its members are sworn in Oathen, on their own tongues, never to repeat it to anyone not at the table.

[fiction]
*The Toast in Seven Removes, as recited by a Taster who did not finish his plate, to a Cutwright who had paid for his last meal; the Taster was served at the next dinner.*
*The Fish:* To the deep, which held us. We held it better.
*The Bread:* To the field, which fed us. We feed on it still.
*The Sweet:* To the door, which let us in. We have not left.
*The Lid:* To the sleeper, who did not wake. Sleep on.
*The Word:* To the witness, who heard us. Nobody else will.
*The Hand:* To the closer, who finished things. We are not finished.
*The Host:* (*Silence. The Host's glass is raised by the Carver of the table and set down before an empty chair.*)
*All, together:* It worked once.
*(They drink. Then one of them, in recent years, it is said, always the youngest, turns to the end of the table, where nobody sits, and raises their glass a second time, and does not say anything at all.)*
[/fiction]

**The tune.** None. It is spoken softly, in the clipped Table-speech of the High Cuts, with long pauses between removes, at a table set with seven courses and a grey bone knife. Members who have eaten something at the dinner that affects their voices (a sliver of Oathen tongue, a Tenanted heart, a Vigil eye) are excused speaking and raise their glass alone.

**History.** The toast is as old as the Second Table's first menu, found in the coat of a murdered Gut-line lordling in 412 A.G., and its form has never changed. The second raising, toward the empty end of the table, is new: the Cutwrights who have interrogated former Tasters say it began in the last ten years, at about the time the menus began to carry an eighth line, left blank. Nobody at the tables admits to having started it. Every table does it. Members asked why say that it seems polite.

[pull] We raised the glass to the end of the table. There's nobody there. There's never anybody there. Only, the last few dinners, the Host's chair has been warm when the servants clear it. | — a Taster, to the Cutwright who bought his supper

**At the Table.** A Guest who knows the toast can identify a Second Table member by it (any member hearing it quoted will react; **Eye + Instinct, Hard (14)**, to spot it). A Guest who recites the toast in the hearing of a member, without being one, will receive an invitation to dinner within the month, and will be served something without being told what it is. Sincerely drinking the toast at a Second Table dinner, all seven removes, is a method of the **Warm Table** for the Second Table's Hold. Raising the glass a second time toward the empty end of the table, at any table on the Table, is a small thing, and costs nothing, and is noticed. The Carver should note who does it.

## Turn the Chair Round: a Rim Drinking Song

[sigil: rim]

> On the Rim we just turn a chair around and drink.
>> — Rim innkeeper, the Groaning Board

The Fatlands fast on Tablenight, the Fast fasts, the fens feast their dead, the Vigil goes dark, the highlands leave their doors open, and Oathen keeps its mouth shut. On the Rim, in every Company inn and waystation, the custom is simpler: the house turns one chair at every table to face the wall, so that nobody can sit in it, and everybody drinks. This is the song they sing while they do it. It is sung at the Groaning Board by six hundred voices at a single table, at Thimble Cross over the dicing-board, at Mumchance in silence on slates, and at the Open Door at Knocking Gate with the curtains pulled back.

[fiction]
*Turn the Chair Round, as sung at the Groaning Board on Tablenight. Each verse is followed by the chorus; at the chorus, the nearest chair is turned a quarter-turn, so that by the fourth chorus it faces the wall.*
Oh the fens feast their dead and the dead cannot taste,
and the south fasts a day and gives up by the noon,
and the coast leaves a bowl with the door open wide,
and we're all of us drunk by the rise of the Plate!
*(Chorus)* So turn the chair round, lads, turn the chair round,
we've counted the guests and there's one too many found;
whoever you are, you can stand by the wall,
for there's lacks on the table and drink for us all!
Oh the Lid puts its lamps out to prove that it can,
and the hills prop their doors and the canyons go dumb,
and the Fast stand in snow with their backs to the house,
and the Rim stays inside where the barrels are warm!
*(Chorus)*
Oh the god in the middle is laying its plates,
and they say it's come out on the road in the night,
and it stoops at the window and looks at the light;
well, we'll give it a song, but we won't give it bread!
*(Chorus)*
Here's the grace that we say when we sit down to eat,
with a stranger or brother or whatever's at the door:
Lack keep away, and the chair face the wall,
and nobody sit in it, nobody, never at all!
*(Chorus, sung twice, the second time very quietly.)*
[/fiction]

**The tune.** A rollicking drinking song in a swinging six, major, loud, with a chorus that everyone bellows and bangs on the table; it is set to a Fatlands harvest tune that the Groaning Board's first innkeeper brought north. The last chorus is sung twice, the second time in a whisper, with everyone looking at the turned chair, and nobody can say when that started either. At Mumchance in Oathen, where nothing loud may be said, the song is written out on slates and passed from hand to hand, and the customers turn the chair in silence and drink.

**History.** The custom of turning a chair to the wall on Tablenight is older than the Company, from the Thin Years when the caravaneers who built the Rim Road slept in the heartland's old ring-road inns, and found, on the longest night of their first Lack on the road, a chair pulled out at every table that nobody had pulled out. The song is younger, from the Sweet Reigns. Its third verse was added within living memory, when Orrum's tables were first found laid at crossroads outside the Fast and the Hostwatch began to report the Host walking the Rim at night, stooping to look in at inn windows. Since 638, the Company has issued a standing instruction to its innkeepers that the chair on Tablenight must be turned before dusk, not during the song. It does not say why.

**At the Table.** Sung in a crowded inn on Tablenight, Turn the Chair Round counts as a feast for the Pall (**−1**), and every Guest who joins in removes 1 Fray. A chair turned to the wall by the song cannot be laid by Orrum's Steward that night: no table appears at that seat, and a Guest who would otherwise face an Invitation there faces none. A chair *not* turned, at an inn where the song has been sung, will be found pulled out at dawn, with crumbs on the seat. On a Lack while leading the song, the singer counts the company at the end of the last chorus, aloud, as the song invites, and there is one too many found.

## Clean Hands: the Reckoners' Walking Song

[sigil: fast]

> We carry our own.
>> — the Reckoners' word, given at the border in place of a password

The Reckoners cross the Rim Road in bands of five to twenty, on foot, carrying their own food, to end the high Cut bloodlines of the Godeater lands in their beds. On the road home they sing this. It is a walking song, sung low, at night, in the rhythm of a long march on short rations, and its verses are a list: the bloodlines the band has ended, one line to each house, named by the Kin-Reader who holds the stolen genealogies. Every band's version is different. The burden is the same.

[fiction]
*Clean Hands, the Reckoners' walking song; the burden as sung by every band, the verses as sung by Reckoner-Captain Scant Hollis's band on the road home from the Fatlands, Carving 640.*
*(Burden)* Clean hands, clean mouth, carry your own,
eat what you brought and leave them alone,
we said no on the hill in the snow,
and we say it again on the road as we go.
One for the house on the Brawn by the weir,
the grandmother first and the cradle last year.
*(Burden)*
One for the house with the field in the hall,
we weeded it down to the root of it all.
*(Burden)*
One for the cousin at college in Quire,
he wept for his mother. We put out the fire.
*(Burden)*
Clean hands, clean mouth, carry your own;
we never once ate of them. Leave them alone.
[/fiction]

**The tune.** A steady trudging two, in a bleak, narrow, Fast-born mode built on only four notes, because the Unfed have never had instruments to teach them more. It is sung barely above speech. The burden is sung by everyone; each verse by the Kin-Reader alone, flatly, as if reading a list, which is what it is.

**History.** Clean Hands dates from the first armed crossing of the Rim in 402 A.G., the Reckoning, and its burden quotes the Long Decline (*we said no on the hill in the snow*). The Abstinent have never approved it: it is the only song in the Fast besides the Decline, and the Keepers say that the Reckoners have made a hymn of murder. Scant Hollis, who has led forty raids and ended nine bloodlines, sings the verses herself and keeps no other trophies. The last line, *we never once ate of them*, is sung by every band. Scour, the Returned, who ate Fatlands meat for a year to keep his cover and came home with godsign under his sleeves, sings it louder than anyone.

**At the Table.** A Reckoner band, or a company travelling with one, that sings Clean Hands on the march gains **+1 to Wayfaring and Endure** on short rations, and the Unfed among them may treat a day's march on half a ration as *ending a day hungry* (+1 Want) even if they were fed. An Unfed Guest who sings a verse naming a bloodline they have helped end gains **+1 Want and 1 Fray**. A Godeater Guest who hears the song sung near them, and is of a High Cut line, makes a **Dread 2** check; if the verse names their own house (the Carver decides, or rolls a d10, and on a 1 it does), it is **Dread 3**, and the band has a Kin-Reader who knows their face.

# Said at Table: Proverbs, Graffiti and Bedtime Stories

> Say it plainly. We were hungry, and they were there.
>> — first line of every history written on the Table, and the first proverb taught to Rim children

[fiction]
*From the preface to THE TABLE ALMANACK, OR A SAYING FOR EVERY DAY, printed at Quire for the Rim Road Company and sold at every waystation for two crumbs, 637 A.G.*
The traveller on the Rim will find that every people of the Table has a proverb for every occasion, and that most of them contradict one another, and that all of them are true in the country where they are said. The Company has therefore compiled, for the convenience of its patrons, a saying for every day of the year, drawn from all seven lands and from the Road itself, so that the traveller may greet his host with something apt. The Company accepts no responsibility for sayings repeated in the wrong land. A saying of the Fatlands spoken in the Fast has been known to start a fight. A saying of any land spoken in Oathen is binding on the speaker if it can be construed as a promise, and the Company's notaries advise that most of them can. The compilers have omitted the eighth of every list. They were asked to.
[/fiction]

This chapter collects the small talk of the Table: the things people say over meals, scratch on walls, and tell their children at night. It is meant to be stolen from. A Carver who needs a line for an innkeeper, a carter, a dying Kept grandmother or a Netwatch officer on a cold pier will find one here, and every line is meant to carry a little of its land's horror in it, the way a proverb carries a whole history in a sentence.

It has three parts. **The Almanac of Sayings** gives a proverb for every day of the year, by season, numbered from the first day of Grace (when the Kept Rain falls on Oathen, or is supposed to) to the last day of Lack. **Graffiti of the Rim** gives forty lines scratched on walls, milestones and privy doors along the ring road, with where they are found and what they actually mean. **Bedtime Stories of the Seven Lands** gives one tale from each land, as a parent tells it at a child's bedside, followed by the true event behind it, which the parent does not tell.

## The Almanac of Sayings

The Company's almanac counts the year from the first day of Grace and gives each season ninety-one days, with the last day of Lack left over as the year's ninety-second day of winter, which the almanac calls *the leftover* and the Unfed call the ninth of the Nine. Tablenight, the longest night, falls by the Company's reckoning on the three hundred and twentieth day. Where several lands keep a holy day on the same date (the first of Grace is the Kept Rain, the Unbricking and the Burying all at once), the almanac gives the saying to one land on the day and to the others on the days that follow, which has offended all three.

Each saying is tagged with the land it belongs to. The Rim's own sayings are tagged *Rim*. Festival days are marked in italic at the head of the line.

[box: Proverbs at the Table]
A Guest who quotes a proverb of a land to someone from that land, aptly, at the right moment, gains **+1 to the next Persuade, Haggle or Deceive roll** with them in that scene, once per scene; they are, for a moment, someone who knows how things are said here. Quoting a proverb of the wrong land (a Fatlands saying about appetite in the Fast; a Sallowreach saying about long life to anyone who is not Kept) gives **−2** instead, and may start a fight. In Oathen, a proverb that can be read as a promise is a promise: the Carver may rule it a Weight 1 oath. The Clausewrights recommend only the sayings that begin with *if*.
The Carver may also roll a d100 (or d366, for the dedicated) to find the day's saying when the Guests wake at an inn. The innkeeper has it chalked on the slate by the door.
[/box]

## Graffiti of the Rim

> The house fed me once. It was good.
>> — scratched under the house rules at Thimble Cross

The Rim Road is two thousand four hundred miles of fitted grey stone, and every mile of it has been written on. Carters scratch their marks on milestones with a nail; Crumb-Runners chalk their codes on the backs of tollhouses; lovers carve the walls of privies; the condemned, the Called, the Reckoners, the Second Table and the cults of every land leave signs for each other in places the Company's whitewash crews never quite reach. The Company paints over what it can find. It cannot find most of it, and some of it comes back through the paint.

Each line below gives the text as it is scratched, where it is found, and what it means to those who know. Guests who learn to read the Rim's walls (a scene with a Crumb-Runner, a Lineman of the College, a Netwatch officer, or any carter who has done the full circuit) gain **+2 to Search and Instinct** for finding trouble, shelter, or a contact at any waystation, because the walls are telling them.

### The Northern Stretch: Fogmouth to Lampwick

- **"Hum for me."** *Scratched on the back of the red-line post at the Drop, outside Fogmouth, low down, where a person sitting on the Sallowreach side could reach.* The Kept who come to the Drop to see their living kin off cannot cross; they ask the living to carry the Ferrying Song's hum across the line and let it finish on the far side. A hum finished south of the line is the nearest thing to a Closing the poor can afford.
- **"MAY YOU FINISH — and you, and you, and you."** *Painted on the Fogmouth gate-arch, renewed every Grace in a different hand.* A blessing, the fen's politest. The repetitions are a Finishers' count: one *and you* for every pilgrim who walked into a Hush that year. The arch is getting crowded.
- **A pair of closed eyes, carved, with a nail driven through each lid.** *On milestones from Fogmouth south to the Weeping Post.* The mark of Marrow Jack's part-runners: a Kept limb is for sale nearby. The nails mean the seller knows the owner can feel it.
- **"6 V. 9 L. 0 W."** *Pencilled inside the door of the Inkhorn's privy, Quire.* A Lineman's tally of three College ledgers: six Vane-descended bloodlines entered at the Course this year, nine Lung-lines, no Wendles. Every Lineman who reads it adds to it. The Provost has had the door replaced four times.
- **"It's not deafness."** *Chalked on the Weeping Post's chapel step, in a careful surveyor's hand.* Edda Thrush, the Hushwalker, passed this way. Those who follow her say the chalk does not wash off in the weep-rain, and that standing on it, you hear nothing at all for a moment, and then something listening.
- **A bowl, upside down, scratched with a stone.** *On the walls of the Weeping Post, by the hundred.* The Purgation's mark: *here a Penitent brought something up.* An upright bowl means a hall is near and will feed you thin soup. An upside-down bowl means the thing that came up was sold on this spot, and the buyer may still be in the inn.
- **"LAMB."** *Scratched very small on the inside of a lamp-glass at Lampwick Halt, where the Company keeps a hundred lamps burning for Vigil merchants.* A Bedded mark: this inn has a cellar with no lamp in it, and a Shepherd who will sit with you if you want to lie down. Wakers who find it smash the glass and fine the house. The Halt goes through a great many lamp-glasses.
- **"Pip owes me a pinch."** *Scrawled in a child's hand on the studded wall of the nudgers' yard, Lampwick Halt.* A nudgers' debt-wall; a child who has been pinched awake by another and not repaid writes it here. Some of the names have been there for years, and the children who wrote them are in the Dormitory, and the Halt's nudgers still honour them: when they pass the wall, they pinch the name.
- **Three short lines and a long one, repeated: ‖‖ ‖‖ ‖‖.** *Scratched along the coping of the Halt's east wall.* A Seemer's record: on these nights, the travellers lodged here all hallucinated the same thing. The long line means it was the tall figure laying a table. The marks have been growing more frequent since 638.
- **"The hours are longer here. Ask Maud."** *Chiselled small into the face of the Lampwick milestone.* Maud Sexton's pendulum measurements, as passed from mouth to mouth by the clock-menders of the plateau. Travellers who time the walk between this stone and the next by a good watch find it takes a little longer every year, and the distance has not changed.

### The Eastern Stretch: Mumchance to Wanting Post

- **"IF ABLE."** *Carved deep into the lintel of Mumchance, the quietest inn in the world.* An Oathen joke and a warning. Every promise made under this lintel is, by local custom, understood to be conditional, and the innkeeper believes (and the Bench has not ruled otherwise) that the carving makes it so. Oatheners come a long way to say something unwise under it.
- **A hand drawn with two fingers bent back.** *Scratched on the felted walls of Mumchance's taproom, under the felt.* A Sureties' mark: a professional who will carry your oath for a fee is drinking here. The number of bent fingers is the number of defaults the Surety can still survive.
- **"Say less."** *On the Treaty Stair, on almost every step, in a thousand hands.* What every Oathen child is taught. Foreign clerks carrying contracts down to Jessamy Quill's office scratch it with their own nails as they go, partly as a joke, partly as a prayer. Nobody has scratched it on the last step, outside her door. Nobody will say why.
- **A fist with the thumb between the fingers.** *Scratched on the long stone wall at Quillhouse, among the travellers' carved conditions.* The Forsworn's sign. A traveller who has broken a promise and carries the break visibly is welcome in the Breakdowns; the sign points down-canyon, and someone will meet you.
- **"I promised her I'd come back. I came back. Somebody tell her."** *Cut into the Quillhouse wall in an Oathen hand, without a single condition, dated 627.* An oath kept, recorded by the man who kept it. Nobody knows who *her* is. Oatheners stop and touch it with two fingers, as they touch a handtalk poem, because a promise kept without a condition is the rarest thing in their country.
- **"WE CARRY OUR OWN."** *Scratched on the inside face of the Hem, opposite Wanting Post, where only someone stepping over the Hem from the Fast side would see it.* The Reckoners' word, and their road-sign: a band crossed here going out. If the line under it has a number, that is how many came back. Most of the numbers are smaller than the bands.
- **"no thank you no thank you no thank you"** *Written in charcoal around the inside rim of the water-trough at Wanting Post.* An Unfed child's work, renewed by other children. The Fast's children believe that a trough with the Decline written round it cannot be filled by anyone but the Company. They are wrong. Some mornings it is found full of warm milk.
- **A small circle with eight short lines around it.** *Cut on the doorpost of the Company factor's office at Wanting Post, and on doorposts at four other stations.* Nobody admits to it. The Company says it is a wheel and the vandal miscounted the spokes. The Hostwatch say that it appears on the doorposts of buildings where, within a month, a table is found laid inside. The factor at Wanting Post has had his doorpost replaced. The mark was on the new one in the morning.
- **"BREAD → 2 mi."** *Chalked on the Saltlick Cross lamp-post that faces the heartland road.* A Crumb-Runner's code for a cache of grain hidden off the road. Or a trap. Real Crumb-Runner caches are marked with an arrow pointing *away* from the bread, because if you smell bread in the Fast, you walk into the wind. A Rim traveller who follows this arrow will find a table two miles in, laid.
- **"This one was mine. Pray for the next one."** *Scratched with a blade on the inner wall at Saltlick Cross, where the old heartland road runs straight in toward Hallowboard.* Written by a Tablebreaker after his four-hundredth table, and copied since by others after theirs. The first one is in the hand of Decline Marsh. He has not been seen at the Cross since the four hundred and first.

### The Southern Stretch: Thimble Cross to Toll Nine

- **"The house fed me once. It was good."** *Scratched under the house rules above the dicing-board at Thimble Cross.* A ruined gambler's last line. The rest of it reads *I lost the rest of my life here anyway, but it was very good soup*. The innkeeper has never painted it out; he says it is the best advertisement he has.
- **Nine strokes, then a tenth through them.** *On each of the nine stone markers in the little graveyard behind Thimble Cross.* A Rim carters' count: nine travellers buried here, and the tenth stroke means *one of them got up*. Nobody agrees which. The graveyard is the only one on the Rim that has its grass cut, and nobody on the staff cuts it.
- **"YOU LOOK HEAVY."** *Carved, with love, into the six-hundred-seat table at the Groaning Board.* The Fatlands' kindest compliment. Drovers carve it beside the place where a friend sat, after that friend's wake. There are three hundred and twelve of them. Rim travellers who sit at a carved place are served first, and given the best cut, and watched.
- **A two-tined fork, very small, scratched beside a window-latch.** *At the Groaning Board and nine other inns.* A Second Table sign: a Taster or Trencherman lodged in this room and found the inn safe for a dinner. If there is a line under it, the dinner happened. If there are two lines, someone at the dinner did not finish their plate.
- **"Barrel 4417 — notch."** *Pencilled inside a cooper's stave in the Chute's yard, and copied onto the yard wall.* Quartermaster Ivo Stockpot's private sign, imitated by the yard-gangs: this barrel is one he would not eat from himself. The gangs mark the wall too, so the carters will know. The wall is very full of notches.
- **"Long Count: Low Tilth 41. Gristle End 17. Furrowby 9."** *Chalked on the back wall of a Gristmoot sausage-stall, renewed every Carving.* Pell Hogget's tally of the Reaping, copied from village to village. Stallholders rub it out when the Bailiffs come by and chalk it up again afterward, a little higher. The numbers are always higher.
- **A dripping spoon.** *Scratched on the doorposts of Gristmoot cookshops.* A Second Helping sign: this cook is of the faith, and will feed you as an act of love, and may ask, very sweetly, if you would like to be part of the next meal. Most Gristmoot cookshops have one. Some have scraped it off, and the scraping is the same shape.
- **"Who counts the counters?"** *Scratched on the lintel of Toll Nine, the Company's windowless counting-house.* A clerk's joke, or a clerk's confession. Inside Toll Nine, according to those who have worked there, the Company keeps a real ledger pricing the end of each land, and the clerks who copy it are not let out until the copy is finished. Someone scratched this on the way in.
- **"Sallowreach 9. Vigil 11. Cradlewrack ?"** *Scratched faint into the stone of Toll Nine's west wall, at waist height.* A copy of the summary page of that ledger, in years. Somebody got out with it in their head. The question mark has been gone over many times, by many hands, deeper each time.
- **"MEAT IS MEAT."** *Painted in red on the Chute's gate, in letters a yard high, and painted out every week.* The Renderers' Union's unofficial motto and the Fatlands' answer to every other land's disgust. Under the paint, if the light is right, there is an older line in a different hand: *meat was someone.*

### The Western Stretch: Saltbridge to Knocking Gate

- **"BONE: MIXED."** *Stencilled on the sealed carts at Saltbridge, and scratched by carters on the bridge parapet.* The stamp on the bone that comes up from the Chute to the Company's Cure-House to be minted into lacks. Carters who scratch it on the parapet are making a joke about money. Every lack on the Table is somebody. The carters say *mixed* means *you'll never know who*.
- **A rope tied in a lover's knot, carved.** *On the eleventh arch of Brackwater Bridge, the one over where the river used to run before it followed the sea out.* A Wreckwife's mark: a woman lives near here whose spouse came back Tenanted, and she will talk to another. The Wreckwives say that on the anniversary of the Beaching the carved knots are found wet.
- **"Subject appeared expected."** *Scratched hundreds of times along the inn wall at Lowmark Spur.* The Netwatch's standard phrase for the Called, written in their logs thousands of times. Netwatch officers on leave scratch it here, once for each Called they could not stop, and nobody knows whether they mean it as mourning or as a joke. They do not know either.
- **"West is the way home."** *In salt crystals, as if scratched with a wet finger, on the inside of the Lowmark Spur inn's west windows.* Nobody writes it. The staff wipe it off every morning. It forms again overnight, on the glass, from the inside. The wardens watch which guests stand at those windows, and the guests who read the line aloud are the ones they watch hardest.
- **"Borrow a bucket, leave a name."** *Painted above the rack of bail-buckets at the Lowmark Spur inn, kept for coast travellers who forgot their own.* The rule is genuine: you scratch your name on the rack when you take a bucket and cross it out when you bring it back. The names not crossed out belong to travellers who were Called somewhere on the road inland. The innkeeper keeps the oldest ones uncrossed on purpose, and on Tablenight sets their buckets out by the west door.
- **"There's one of us on every hook."** *Chalked by children on the walls of the Spur, in the skipping-rhyme's rhythm, with hopscotch squares beneath.* The sixth line of the hauling song's burden, as skipped in the Mile. Some of the chalk is in a child's hand. Some of it says *one of them*. The children say they did not write those.
- **"Not yet."** *Scratched very small on the inside of the Open Door's curtain-rails at Knocking Gate, the inn with no doors.* The first words ever spoken by a Cradlewrack newborn, in Kest in 94 A.G. Highland travellers scratch it as a prayer on the way out of the country: let whatever is in me not come yet, not here, not until I am home. Marshal Breech's men check every traveler for signs of carrying, and they read the rails.
- **Three knocks, cut as three short notches, then a door drawn ajar.** *On milestones from Knocking Gate north to the Weeping Post.* A highland warning: something has been born on the road near here, and was not answered by a midwife, and is walking. The more ajar the door is drawn, the bigger it has grown.
- **"Mam, I'm sorry. I did it on the Rim so it wouldn't hold."** *Scratched on the wall of a privy at Knocking Gate, dated 633.* Petra Quick runs expectant mothers across the Rim so they can deliver somewhere the rule does not hold. She has found that it holds: the country goes with them. This line was written by one of hers. Underneath, in another hand, months later: *It held.*
- **"8."** *Scratched on the Hem itself, the knee-high wall facing the Fast, at intervals the length of the ring, by no one anybody has seen.* There is one every hundred miles or so, all the way round, in the same size and the same depth, in stone that the Company says has not been recut since it was laid. The Company's crews grind them out every year. They do not come back. They are simply found again, a little way along. Rim folk do not discuss them, and step over the Hem, when they must, a little way from the nearest one.

## Bedtime Stories of the Seven Lands

> Tell me the one about the hand again. No, the real one. No, the one where it gets in.
>> — a child of Kest, at bedtime

Every land tells its children stories at night, even the Vigil, where night is a lamp-lit stretch of the same long day and the stories are told to keep the child awake rather than to send it off. The seven stories below are the commonest of each land, as a parent tells them at the bedside, word for word as they were taken down by a College clerk making a survey of nursery-lore in 630 and 631. After each is the true event the story grew from, as the College's own ledgers record it. Parents do not tell that part. Most of them do not know it. Some of them do, and tell the story anyway, because a story is the only safe way to tell a child what happened.

Each story ends with a short note for the Carver: what happens when a Guest tells it, or hears it, at the table.

### Granny Wick and the Fly King

[sigil: sallowreach]

[fiction]
*As told in the Sinks of Lastgate, by a mother to two children in a shared cot, with the shutters latched and a smudge-pot going on the sill.*
Once upon a time, in a tall thin house on the Sinks, there lived a Granny called Granny Wick, who had been Kept for two hundred years, and she was the oldest thing in the house, older than the stairs. She was very tidy. Her stitches were black and small, and her jaw was wired with silver wire, and in Restitching Week the Seamers came and sewed her up as neat as a parcel, and she smelled of cedar for a fortnight, and then she smelled of Granny.
Granny Wick sat in the window, in the big chair, with her mittens on, and she told the children stories, and she never once complained. Not once. Only, every night, when the children kissed her, she would say, very quietly, *open the shutter for me, my lamb. Just a crack. Just tonight.*
And the children would say, *no, Granny, it's the Shutter Days, the flies are out*, and they would latch it tight, and kiss her, and go to bed. And Granny Wick would say, *never mind, my lamb. May you finish.*
Now, outside the shutter, in the Shutter Days, there lived the Fly King. He was very tall and very thin and he had a coat made of a thousand thousand wings, all humming, and a crown of bluebottles, and he was terribly polite. He had been courting Granny Wick for a hundred years. Every Plenty he came and stood outside her window in the warm dark and hummed to her, and she hummed back, and the children never heard.
One night the littlest one, whose name was Moss, could not sleep, and she came down the stairs and found Granny Wick crying. Kept people cannot cry, because their eyes have gone dry, so it was only the shape of crying, but Moss knew it.
*What's the matter, Granny?*
*Oh, my lamb,* said Granny Wick. *I am so very tired of the stairs. And the Fly King has asked me to dance, and I've no shutter to dance through.*
And Moss loved her Granny very much. So she climbed up on the sill, and she unlatched the shutter, just a crack, just that night.
In came the Fly King, humming, in his coat of a thousand thousand wings. He bowed to Granny Wick, very low, and he bowed to Moss, and he said, *thank you, little one. You have a kind heart.* And he held out his hand, and Granny Wick took off one mitten, and put her grey hand in his.
And they danced. They danced round the kitchen and up the stairs and down again, and every time they turned, a little bit of Granny Wick went away on a wing, a crumb, a thread, a stitch, carried off by one of the Fly King's thousand thousand courtiers, and Granny Wick laughed and laughed, and she got lighter and lighter, and smaller and smaller, until at the last she was as light as a moth, and the Fly King lifted her up, and they went out of the window together into the warm dark, humming.
In the morning there was nobody in the big chair. There were only two mittens, one on the cushion and one on the floor. And Mam cried and cried, and the Court came and fined the house a whole lack, and Moss was sent to bed without supper.
But that night, when Moss lay in bed, she heard a humming at the shutter. And it was not one voice, it was a thousand thousand voices, very small, and every one of them was Granny Wick's, and every one of them was saying, *thank you, my lamb. Thank you. Thank you.* Because in the fen nothing ends, not even a Granny; she had only gone very small, and very many, and very far.
So that is why, my darlings, you must never, ever swat a fly in Plenty. Because it might be Granny. And that is why you latch the shutter tight in the Shutter Days, and kiss your Kept goodnight, and never, ever open it, however nicely they ask. Because the Fly King is still out there, in his coat of wings, and he is very polite, and he is courting all of them.
Now go to sleep. No, leave the smudge-pot. Leave it. Goodnight.
[/fiction]

#### The True Event

In the Shutter Days of 487 A.G., in a tenement on Clench Row in the Sinks, a Kept woman named Ottilie Wrayburn, two hundred and eleven years upright, persuaded her great-great-great-granddaughter, a girl of eight called Mossy Wrayburn, to unlatch the shutter of her room for one night. A Black Noon was moving through the Sinks. The family found Ottilie in the morning stripped by the swarm to wire, lacquer and bone, and wholly aware: the Seamers' report records that the flies had taken everything soft, and laid in what they did not take, and that she spoke throughout the restitching, saying only *thank you*. The Mortuary Court fined the household one lack for an *unlicensed attempt at finishing*, and passed the Shutter Ordinance of 488, under which shutters in the Shutter Days are latched from the outside by the parish. What was left of Ottilie Wrayburn was Set Aside in the Lofts in 489. Old Bettany Shroud's night-book records that she still says *thank you* whenever a fly lands on her shelf, and that lately, like all the others, she has begun to whisper something else. Mossy Wrayburn lived to be ninety-one, and was Kept, and asked every one of her own descendants, every Shutter Day for a century, to open the shutter, and nobody ever did.

**At the Table.** A Sallowreacher Guest who hears this story told by a Kept relative, and understands what is being asked, faces **Dread 2**. A Guest who opens a shutter for a Kept person who asks, in the Shutter Days, may give that person the nearest thing to a Closing the poor can have: the Black Noon finishes nothing, but the Kept who go out with the flies are, the fen believes, dancing. It costs the Guest 2 Fray and the household's good name.

### The Boy Who Wanted to Be Full

[sigil: fatlands]

[fiction]
*As told in Low Tilth, by a father to a boy of six, between the fourth and fifth meals of the day, with a heel of toothed-wheat bread in the boy's fist.*
Once there was a boy called Ned Dumpling, and he was the hungriest boy in all the south. He ate his five meals and he ate his mam's five meals, and he ate the crusts off the board and the dripping out of the pan and the candle-ends out of the chapel, and still his belly said *more, Ned, more*.
So one morning Ned Dumpling put on his boots and went out to find somebody who could tell him how to be full.
First he went to the Miller. The Miller was as wide as his own millstone, and he was eating bread made of his own flour, with the teeth still in. *Miller, Miller,* said Ned, *how do I get full?* And the Miller laughed so hard his chair cracked, and said, *Full? Nobody's full, boy. There's no such word.* And he gave Ned a loaf, and Ned ate it on the road, and he was just as hungry.
Then he went to the Bailiff. The Bailiff had a funnel and a ladle and a kind soft face. *Bailiff, Bailiff,* said Ned, *how do I get full?* And the Bailiff patted his head and said, *Full is against the law, lad. It'd mean you could stop.* And he poured Ned a ladle of gravy, and Ned drank it, and he was just as hungry.
Then he went to Old Mother Mound, who was a hill at the end of the lane, a little green hill with an apple tree on top of her and a mouth in her side. *Mother Mound, Mother Mound,* said Ned, *how do I get full?* And Old Mother Mound opened her mouth very wide, and Ned could see down and down and down into the warm dark, and she said, in a voice like soil settling, *I've been asking that for forty years, poppet. When you find out, come and tell me.*
And Ned Dumpling sat down on the side of the lane and cried, because he was so hungry, and nobody in the whole south knew how to be full.
Now, in the far field, the one past the last hedge, there stood a Scarecrow. Nobody had put it there. It had a hat and a coat and a face made of a sack, and it stood with its back to the village, and every Carving it was a little nearer. And the Scarecrow heard Ned crying, and it turned round, very slowly, which scarecrows are not supposed to do, and it said:
*Ned Dumpling. I know where it is.*
*Where what is?* said Ned.
*The pudding,* said the Scarecrow. *The great pudding, at the bottom of the furrow. Everyone who eats it is full forever. Come at dusk, and I'll show you.*
So Ned Dumpling went home and ate his fifth meal, and when the sun went down red over the fields, and his mam was busy at the copper, he slipped out of the back door, and over the last hedge, and into the far field, where the Scarecrow was waiting.
*Lie down in the furrow,* said the Scarecrow, *and close your eyes, and open your mouth.*
And Ned lay down in the warm brown furrow, and the field was soft as a feather bed, and it went up and down under him, slow, like breathing. And he closed his eyes, and he opened his mouth. And the furrow folded over him like a blanket, the way your mam tucks you in, all warm and heavy and sweet.
And for the first time in his whole life, Ned Dumpling was full.
In the morning they looked for him, and they didn't find him. But the next Grace, the wheat in the far field came up taller and sweeter than any wheat in the south, gold to the ear, with little white teeth all smiling in rows. And Ned's mam cut it, and milled it, and baked a loaf, and when she ate it she wasn't hungry for a whole hour. A whole hour! Nobody in the south had ever heard of such a thing. She sat by the window for the whole hour, very still, with her hands in her lap, and she said it was the best hour of her life.
So that is the story of Ned Dumpling, who found out how to be full.
And that is why, my lad, you come in by dusk. Every night. However the Scarecrow asks. Unless you want to be full.
Do you want to be full?
No. Good lad. Eat your bread.
[/fiction]

#### The True Event

The story is told across the plains, but the College traces it to the parish orphan-house at Furrowby, in Low Tilth, in the bad Carving of 517 A.G., when the Feasting Laws' fines for under-feeding fell on houses whether or not they had food to give. The matron, Dorcas Breadwell, could not feed her forty-one charges five meals a day and was fined twice in a month. On the third occasion she sent the hungriest boys out to glean the far field at dusk, telling them this story, which she claimed afterward to have invented to give them heart. Eleven did not come back. The far field yielded that next Grace at a rate the Board's assessors called *unexampled*, and an assessor named Varn Sweetbread the Elder, grandfather of the present Cullmaster, wrote a memorandum on the coincidence of yield and absence which the Board filed under *agriculture*. The office of the Cullmasters was established in 519. The orphan-house at Furrowby was fined again the following Carving, for under-feeding, and closed. Its far field is still the best in the parish. The eleven gave the Board its policy and saved their house nothing.

**At the Table.** A Fatlander Guest of the Appetite or deeper who hears this story told at dusk must make a **Caul + Resolve** check (Hard 14) or go to the window and look toward the far fields until someone speaks their name. A Guest who repeats it to a Cullmaster's man and mentions Furrowby will find that the man knows the name, and the year, and is very interested in who taught the Guest both.

### The Girl Who Held the Rope

[sigil: brinehollow]

[fiction]
*As told in the Mile, by a grandmother to a girl of seven, while tying the girl's anchoring-rope to the bed for the night; the knots are tied as the story goes.*
Once, in the hungry time, before the sea went away, there was a girl called Jenny Rope, and she was going to be married to a boy called Orry, the handsomest boy on the whole coast, who could swim like a seal and sing like a kettle.
Now the Fathom was lying off the beach, big as a hill and grey as a slate, and it wouldn't come in to be eaten, no matter how the people pulled. And the Sounders came up out of the water with their ears all bleeding and said: *the Fathom wants a keepsake. Seven of your best, your youngest, your promised. Send them out on the ropes.* And one of the seven they chose was Orry.
So on the night before, Jenny Rope took a piece of hemp, so, and she tied it round Orry's wrist, so, in a lover's knot, like this one, see, over and under and through and back, and she held the other end in her own two hands. And she said, *Orry, I'm holding the end. You go where you have to go. But I am holding the end.*
And Orry kissed her and walked out on the hook-line into the dark water, hand over hand, singing, and went in under the Fathom's great grey side, into a door that opened for him, and he was gone.
And Jenny Rope stood on the shingle and held the end.
All night the haulers pulled, and the song went *haul and hold, haul and hold*, and the water came pouring out of the Fathom, and people fell down and were laid out on the stones. And Jenny Rope held the end. Her hands bled. Her feet froze. Her mam came and said *let go, Jenny, he's gone*, and Jenny Rope said *I'm holding the end*.
And at the turn of the tide, when it was darkest, she felt the rope go tug. Tug, tug. Just like that.
So Jenny Rope went in after him.
She went hand over hand along the rope, into the water, under the grey side, through the door that opened, and inside the Fathom it was dark and cold and silver, and there were rooms, great soft rooms like the inside of a shell, and the water was up to her knees, and she could hear singing a long way in. And she held the rope, and followed it, room after room, deeper and deeper, in the dark, until she came to the end of the rope.
And there was Orry, at the end of the rope, sitting in the silver dark, with his wedding shirt on, and he looked up and smiled at her.
*You came,* he said.
*I was holding the end,* said Jenny Rope.
And she took his hand, the one with the knot on it, and she led him back, room after room, along the rope, out through the door, up through the water, onto the shingle, just as the sun came up. And the Fathom came in on the tide, and the people ate, and the hungry time was over. And Jenny Rope and Orry were married on the beach, with the knot still tied, and they never untied it, not ever, not even when they were old.
And that's why, my duck, at every wedding on the coast, they tie the lover's knot. And that's why we tie you to your bed at night, over and under and through and back, by somebody who loves you. So that if you go walking in the night, out to the water, there's somebody holding the end.
There. That's the last knot. Who's holding the end?
That's right. Gran is.
[/fiction]

#### The True Event

Orry Weir was one of the seven Hook-Brides of the Beaching, given by the north-shore village of Weirholm and tied to a hook-line at the wrist with a lover's knot. The chronicles of the Lung-lines record that his betrothed, a girl of nineteen whose name they do not give, followed the line in after him during the night, hand over hand, into the god's gill-openings. She was found at dawn among the founders of the Lung-lines, in the flooded chambers of Dromm's lungs, where they had spent the night eating raw in the dark, by touch, with the water to their knees. She was carried out half-drowned with Ondric Vasht and the rest. In her hand was Orry's wrist-knot, still tied, and nothing else of him was ever found. The Vasht chronicle's struck sentence says that *the lights were full of sweetmeats*, and that every one of those who went in ate in the dark and blessed the god for its provision. She lived to be seventy-eight. She bailed more than a gallon at every dawn and dusk of her life, more than any Scrapling on the coast, and the Debt chapels say she was bailing Orry. She never married. The knot is in the Admiralty's Sounding Hall in a case, labelled *a relic of the Brides*. The Weirholm story calls her Jenny Rope. Her own family called her nothing at all, afterward.

**At the Table.** Telling this story while tying a sleeper's anchoring-rope gives the sleeper **+2** against the pull that night. A Guest who has read the Vasht chronicle, or Abiah Steep's letter, and then hears the story told to a child, faces **Dread 2**, and must decide whether to say anything.

### Count the Sheep, Bryony

[sigil: vigil]

[fiction]
*As told on the Slope, by a hired nudger of fourteen to a girl of five, sitting up on the sloped floor of a tenement at the twenty-second bell, with a pin in the nudger's hand.*
Eyes open. Eyes open, Bryony. That's it. I'll tell you the one about the sheep, and you keep your eyes on me the whole time, and if they shut I'll pin you, and you'll say thank you. Ready?
Once, in the soft old days, before the lamps, there was a girl called Bryony, the same as you, and she was a shepherd's girl up on the plateau, and she had a hundred sheep. White ones. Fat ones. And every night her da said to her, *Bryony, count them in.* So she stood at the gate of the pen, and the sheep came over the hill, and one by one they jumped over the gate into the pen, and she counted. One, two, three. All the way to a hundred. And then she shut the gate and went to bed, and slept like a stone.
Eyes open.
Then one year the bad thing happened, the First Night, and her da lay down and didn't get up, and her mam lay down and didn't get up, and Bryony was all alone with the sheep. And she was so tired. You know the tired. Like a hand on top of your head, pushing.
So she went out to count the sheep in, because her da always said to. And the sheep came over the hill, white and fat, and jumped the gate, one, two, three. And when she got to a hundred, there was another one. A hundred and one. And another. A hundred and two. And they kept coming, white and soft and woolly, jumping the gate, and she counted and counted, because her da said to, and her eyes went heavy, and heavier.
And she looked into the pen.
And the pen was very long. Longer than a pen should be. Long and dim, like a room, with lamps burning low along the walls. And all the sheep were lying down in it, in rows, in the warm, breathing all together, in and out. And at the far end of the pen there was a shepherd.
He was very big. Bigger than the hill. And he was sitting in the dark at the end, with his hands on his knees, and he had one great eye in the middle of his face, and it was shut.
Eyes open, Bryony.
And the shepherd said, in a voice like a pillow, *Keep counting, little one. When the pen is full, I'll open my eye, and look at all my sheep, and then we'll all go home.*
And Bryony was so tired. And the sheep were so soft. And she wanted to see the shepherd open his eye. So she kept counting. A thousand. Ten thousand. Fifty thousand. Sixty thousand, nearly. And every sheep that jumped the gate lay down in a row and went to sleep, breathing in and out with all the others.
And the shepherd's eyelid fluttered. Just a little. Like a moth.
And Bryony, the clever girl, the brave girl, she took her da's shearing-pin out of her pocket, and she stuck it in her own thumb, hard, like this.
Yes, like that. Say thank you.
And she woke up. And she was standing at the gate of the pen, in the grey morning, and the pen was empty, and there was nobody at the end of it, and her thumb was bleeding. And she knew the number. The number of sheep. She'd counted them all, right to the edge. And she ran all the way to the city, to the people with lamps, and she told them.
And the lamp-people said thank you, Bryony. And they gave her a lovely bed.
Eyes open.
So that's why we never count sheep, not in your head, not ever. Because they jump into the long pen, and the long pen is nearly full. And if you have to count, you count them out. Out, Bryony. Over the gate and away. Sixty, fifty-nine, fifty-eight. Out of the pen. Never in.
That's it. Count them out. I'll pin you at the bell.
[/fiction]

#### The True Event

In 401 A.G. a Dream-Diver of the Watch named Bryony Lisle went under on the tether, and was dragged up after a dive of nineteen minutes, longer than anyone had lived through, and said she had the number: the count of sleepers at which the eye in the long room opens. The Watch had her say it once, to three officials, in a sealed room on the Nodding Stair. One of them wrote it down. One was charged to remember it. One, the Matron of the Dormitory, took it in her head back to the beds, and it has passed from Matron to Matron ever since. Bryony Lisle was then given warm milk and a bed, and her bed was added to the count, so that she could never be made to say it again to anyone else. She is Bed 8,012. The official who wrote it down burned the paper the same evening and walked through the Pillow fence before the morning bell. The other went mad by the Seeming within the year, and spent the rest of his life counting aloud, backward, in the Glass House. The Slope's nursery story began within a generation. The hired nudgers tell it best, because they have to keep the child awake to the end of it.

**At the Table.** In the Vigil, telling this story to a Weary listener and having them *count the sheep out* backward from sixty, aloud, removes **1 Weariness** from the listener, once a day, as a nudger's pin would; it is a technique the Slope's nudgers swear by. Counting them *in*, aloud, past sixty, is a scene of comfort (+1 Weariness) for everyone listening. A Guest who reaches Bed 8,012 in the Dormitory and looks at Bryony Lisle's lips will find them moving, very slightly, in a count. It is not going up.

### The Little Hand That Knocked

[sigil: cradlewrack]

[fiction]
*As told in Kest, by a mother to two children in a bricked room, after the night-mason has laid the last course and before the candle is out.*
There. That's the last brick, and nothing can get in, and you're both in, and I'm in, and the rest is out. Snug as stones. Now, which one? The hand? Again?
All right. Once upon a time, high in the red hills, a woman lay down to bear, and the midwife came with her basin and her apron, and asked the first question. *What is it?* And what came out was a hand. Only a hand. A little one, a left one, warm and pink and wriggling, and all its fingernails perfect.
And the midwife asked the second question, very quietly, and the mother said, *let it stay.* So it stayed.
The little hand was a good hand. It could knit, and it could scratch backs, and it could pat a cheek. But it was lonely. Because everyone else in the house had a body, and the little hand had only itself. So one night, when the pang had passed and the doors were all drifting open, the little hand climbed down off the cot and went out of the open door to look for the rest of itself.
It walked on its fingers, tip-tap, tip-tap, down the lane, and it came to the first house, and it knocked. Low down on the door, right at the bottom, where a hand would knock if it had no arm. *Knock.*
And a voice said, *who's there?*
*A hand,* said the little hand. *Have you got the rest of me?*
And the voice said, *not here,* and knocked back once. *Knock.* And the little hand went on.
It went to every house in Kest. Tip-tap. *Knock.* *Not here.* *Knock.* Only once, in a house at the bottom of the town, a woman opened the door, because she thought it was her own little one come home. And the hand looked up at her, and she looked down at it, and she said, *you're not mine.* And the hand said, *no. But you've got two,* and it took hold of her finger, very gently, and it would not let go, and in the morning the woman's left hand was gone from the wrist, as neat as a midwife's knot, and the little hand had a friend. So the little hand went on with its friend. And it went out of Kest, and up the hills, and down the ravines, all through the highlands, knocking, knocking. And in one house it found a foot, all alone, that had been born just like the hand; and the foot said, *I'm looking too*, and they went on together. And in another house they found an ear, and in another a length of back that crawled, and in another a whole arm with no hand on the end of it, and the arm and the hand looked at each other, and they *fit*.
Click. Like a key.
And they all went on together, a bigger and bigger thing, knocking on doors, looking for the rest. And it's out there still, my darlings. Walking through the highlands at night. Getting bigger. Every pang it finds a little more of itself.
So if ever you hear a knock in the night, low down on the door, right at the bottom, where a hand would knock, you mustn't open it. You mustn't. You knock back, once, like this, *knock*, and you say, nice and clear, *not here.* And it'll go on to the next house.
And one day, when it's found every bit of itself, all its hands and all its feet and its ears and its back and its mouth, it won't knock on houses any more. It'll go up to the big round hole in the middle of the hills, and kneel down at the edge, and it'll knock on that.
And then we'll see what answers.
Now. Candle out. What do we say if we hear a knock?
That's right. *Not here.*
Goodnight. The brick's sound. Nothing gets in.
[/fiction]

#### The True Event

In 500 A.G. a midwife of the Attendance at Mortar Rise named Wenna Clay was keeping, in a lined basket under her bed, two Parts she had been ordered to give the Second Opinion and had not: a warm left forearm born to a tanner's widow in Rudge, and a hand, a left one, born to a shepherd's daughter of Tarnbrow thirty miles away. One night in Carving she found them in the basket joined at the wrist, the skin knitted without a seam. She took the joined thing to the Lying-In, where the Attendance's surgeons established over the following month that Parts from unrelated families fit each other, everywhere, exactly; this is the event called the Gathering of Parts. The joined arm was kept at the Lying-In under glass. It knocked on the glass, softly, at the height of a door's lowest panel, at every pang until 612, when it was stolen, and the Assemblers have never admitted to taking it. Wenna Clay was given the Going Back for disobedience. Dov Marrin's assembled figure in the Assemblers' Barn has a left arm whose hand is missing at the wrist, and the Barn's keepers report that on pang nights the stump taps, very gently, on the boards.

**At the Table.** In Cradlewrack, a Guest who answers a low knock in the night with one knock and *not here* is left alone: no roll. A Guest who opens the door, or answers *who's there?*, faces whatever the Carver has been saving. A Guest who knows where Hester Lowe keeps the hand she bore, and tells this story near her, will make an enemy who has promised to kill anyone who comes for it.

### The Boy Who Promised the Plate

[sigil: oathen]

[fiction]
*As told in Tacit, in writing, on a slate, by a father to a bitted boy of nine; the father writes a line, the boy reads it and wipes it, and the father writes the next. Copied by the College clerk from the slates before they were wiped, with the father's leave, if able.*
Once there was a boy called Ash who lived high on the Sunward Wall, and he had a little sister called Wren, and he loved her more than figs.
In the year of this story, children did not wear the bit. Their mouths were free. They could say anything. Imagine it. It was a frightening time.
One Lack, Wren took the fever. She lay in her cot and burned, and she would not eat, not bread, not figs, not honey. And Ash sat by her and said, *what would you eat, Wren? Anything. Tell me.*
And Wren looked out of the window at the Plate, which was full that night and white and round over the canyon, and she said, *I would eat that.*
And Ash said, *I promise I will bring you the Plate to eat.*
You know what happened then. The god inside him heard. What it heard was so.
That night, nothing. The next night, Ash's little finger cracked, like a twig, because the Plate was in the sky and not in Wren's cot. The night after that, the next finger. Every night he did not keep his promise, another bone in him went, a little at a time, as the god counted.
His mother held a lamp up to the window and said, *there, Wren, there's the Plate*, and the god did not believe her. His father baked a round white loaf and set it in the cot, and the god did not believe him either. The lawyers of the street came and stood about the cot in a row, and drafted, and argued, and went home, and in the night another bone went. Ash did not cry out. He had promised enough.
So Ash climbed. He climbed to the top of the Sunward Wall, where the canyon ends and the sky starts, and he stretched up his hands to the Plate, and it was very far, and his fingers were broken. And he sat down on the top of the world and he wept, and the canyon wept back at him, over and over, because canyons repeat everything.
And up there, on the rim, there was an old woman sitting on a stone. She was a Clausewright, the oldest one there ever was, so old that she had used up all her words but a very few, and she was saving those.
She looked at Ash's fingers. She looked at the Plate. And she spent four of her words on him. She said:
*Bring her the Plate.*
And she handed him a bowl. A plain clay bowl. And she poured into it water from her own flask, and set it down on the stone in front of him, and Ash looked down.
And there, in the bowl, round and white and full, was the Plate.
So Ash carried the bowl down. All the way down the Sunward Wall, a thousand steps, with his broken fingers, without spilling a drop, and the Plate lay in the water the whole way, and it did not go out. And he set it down on Wren's cot, and he said, *I brought you the Plate.* And the god inside him listened, and it heard, and it was so: he had brought the Plate. And it let him go.
And Wren sat up, and she drank the Plate out of the bowl, all of it, in one long drink, cold and white and sweet. And her fever went out of her like a lamp. And in the morning she was well, and Ash's fingers were mended, every one.
So: remember. A word is a bone. Say less. But if you must promise, think first, and promise something you can carry down the stairs.
Now wipe the slate. That is the end. If able.
[/fiction]

#### The True Event

In Lack of 214 A.G., a boy of eleven in Tacit named Ashe Varro, unbitted, as all children then were, promised his sister Wrenna, who was dying of the canyon fever, that he would bring her the moon. There was no Clausewright on the rim. Over nine nights his body broke to match: first the fingers, then the hands, then the bones of the forearms, while his family tried every interpretation the street's lawyers could devise, a lamp held up to her window, a round white loaf, a mirror. None was accepted. On the tenth night, the god in him having found nothing that would serve, his eyes clouded white and round, perfectly round, like two small plates, and he could see nothing ever afterward, and it is said that his sister, looking into them, said they were beautiful. She died the next day. The fever would have taken her whatever he had promised. The Bench of Clauses passed the Muzzling the following year, requiring all children to be bitted until twelve. Ashe Varro lived to be sixty and taught handtalk to bitted children in Low Dray, and never spoke again. The story's Clausewright, the old woman who saved her words, does not appear in any record. The Quiet believe she was invented by Ashe himself, in handtalk, for the children he taught, because he wanted one story in Oathen where a promise could be kept by being clever.

**At the Table.** An Oathener Guest who has made a rash promise and hears this story told may roll **Tongue + Clause** at Grim (18) to find, as Ash did, an interpretation the god will accept; the Carver must agree that the interpretation is honest. A success keeps the oath. A failure costs the next bone. The story is also how every Oathen child learns that a rash promise has an *if able* in it somewhere; the father writes *if able* on the slate at the end, and the boy is supposed to notice.

### The Crust on the Sill

[sigil: fast]

[fiction]
*As told in Wanting, by a mother to a girl of eight, both standing, the girl in her sleeping-shift, in a room with no hearth, before the girl lies down on her board; the Unfed tell stories standing.*
Once there was a girl called Tarry, the same as your aunt, and she lived in a cold stone house in the heartland, with her mam and her da and her brothers, and they were very thin and very good.
One night in Lack, Tarry saved the crust of her one meal, the way you're not supposed to, and she put it on the windowsill for the birds, because she felt sorry for them.
In the morning the crust was gone. And in its place, on the sill, there was a better crust. Soft, and white, and warm, as if it had come out of an oven, though there was no oven anywhere in the whole Fast.
And Tarry looked at it, and she said, *No, thank you.* Like a good girl. And she left it.
That night, she put her own crust on the sill again. And in the morning, there was a bun. A sweet one, with a glaze on it, shining. And Tarry said, *No, thank you. No, thank you.* And she left it.
The next night, she put her crust on the sill. And in the morning there was a pie. A whole pie, with steam coming out of the slits, and the smell came in through the shutter and went all through the house, and her brothers woke up and their mouths were wet. And Tarry said, *No, thank you. No, thank you. No, thank you.* And her mam came and threw the pie in the snow.
And the next night, Tarry didn't put anything on the sill. But in the morning, when she opened the shutter, there was a table in the yard. A long table, with a white cloth, and candles lit, and dishes on it, all steaming, and four chairs, and a fifth chair pulled out, and at the head of the table there stood somebody very tall, stooping, with a kind face that you couldn't quite see. And it was warm. The snow had all melted round it, like a hem.
And the tall one said, *Tarry. You've been so kind to me. Won't you come in?*
And her brothers went to the window. And her mam and da went to the window. And they were so hungry. And they looked at Tarry.
And Tarry said, *No, thank you.*
And the tall one said, *please.*
And Tarry said, *No, thank you.*
And the tall one said, *there's plenty. There's always plenty. Please.*
And Tarry said, *No, thank you.*
And then, my love, the tall one asked her a fourth time. It asked very softly, the way you'd ask a person who was dying. And everyone knows that on the fourth time, it's manners to say yes.
And Tarry said it a fourth time. *No, thank you.*
And the tall one stood very still. And then it bowed to her, low, the way it bowed to us on the hill, and it began to cry. Its tears were warm and they ran down onto the table and the candles went out. And it picked up the whole table, cloth and candles and pie, and folded it up like a letter, and walked away into the gold, stooping, and it never came back to that house again.
And Tarry's mam held her so tight her ribs creaked. And they were hungry for the rest of their lives, all of them, and they were clean. And that is the happiest ending there is.
So. If anything is left on the sill, what do you say?
That's right. Four times. Even on the fourth. Especially on the fourth.
Lie down now. Back to the door.
[/fiction]

#### The True Event

In the winter of 43 into 44 A.G., in the village later called Corve Hatch, the youngest daughter of Abel Corve, a girl named Tarry Corve, left the crust of her ration on the windowsill on several nights running, for the birds. Her elder sister Stint, who did not sit and whose descendants live in Wanting and Hillward, later testified to the Abstinent that the crusts were replaced, and with better things, and that Tarry hid them from the family and shared them with her brothers. In Lack of 44 the family found a table laid in their own kitchen. They were very hungry. Stint was outside fetching snow to melt when they sat. She came in to find her mother, her father, Tarry and the three boys at the table, eating from empty plates and smiling, and they asked her to sit, and she said no, three times, and on the fourth she was not asked again: the chair at the table had been pushed in. The neighbours watched the family through the window for eleven years before they understood it could not be undone, and bricked the window up from outside. The Corve kin stand at the bricked window one night a year, on Corve Night, with their backs to it. Every visitor adds a brick. Stint Corve told this story to her own children with Tarry's name in it and a different ending. She never said which part was the lie.
