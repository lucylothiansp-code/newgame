# The Lexicon of the Table: L–Z

> A word is a mouthful you have agreed to share. Chew it before you swallow it, and know whose it was.
>> — preface to the Quire Word-Book, second half, fourth recension (641 A.G.)

The second half of the College's word-book picks up where the first left off, at the letter that begins *Lack*, which is as good a place as any to begin a description of the Table. Like its sister volume, it was compiled by Copyists and Linemen of the Cutwrights' College on their rounds, from the mouths of innkeepers, Seamers, nudgers, Renderers, Clausewrights, Crumb-Runners, children at doorsteps and the dead on their shelves, and corrected (where the Compiler could bear to) against the College's own ledgers. It is a working book. The College wants to know what people call things, because what people call a thing is what they are afraid it is.

The Compiler is a Cutwright of the Chapter-House at Quire, and has asked that her name be left off the title page, on the grounds that several of the words below are about people who would recognise it. Her marginal notes are kept, set off in brackets, because the Archprovost found them useful and because she would not let them be cut.

### How to Read an Entry

Each entry gives the word, its kind (*n.* noun, *v.* verb, *adj.* adjective, *interj.* interjection, *phr.* phrase, *prov.* proverb, *excl.* oath or curse, *title*, *child.* nursery word, *cant* thieves' or trade jargon), the land or road where it is heard (*common* means everywhere on the Table), and a definition, often with an example *in italics* as it was overheard. **Bold** names point to another entry, in this half or the first. Words marked *cant* should not be used in front of the people they describe. Words marked *child.* should not be used in front of their mothers.

[pull] I have written down every word they would say to me. The ones they would not, I have written down too, from behind doors. | — the Compiler, in the margin of the L proofs

[box: Using the Lexicon at the Table]
The Lexicon is a prop and a toolbox. Read an entry aloud as an NPC's line; hand a player the insult their character would know; let a Guest who speaks a word correctly in the right land take +1 to a Persuade or Deceive roll there, and let a Guest who uses one wrongly (calling an Oathener *talkative*, wishing a fen-widow *long life*) face a Hard 14 Tongue + Persuade roll to undo it. Every few entries carries a hook: a name, a place, a rumor. Where an entry gives a rule, it uses the standard numbers.
[/box]

## L

[entry: Lack | n., common | 1. The nine-year famine before the Gorging, the **Long Lack**. 2. Winter, the fourth season. 3. Poverty, hunger and bad luck, which on the Table are one condition and need only one word. 4. On the dice, a failed roll showing doubles, the worst plausible complication. *Lack sat down at our table in my grandmother's time and never got up* (a Rim way of saying a family has always been poor). See also **lack**, **Licked Clean**.]
[entry: lack | n., common | The bone coin: a salt-cured disc the width of a thumb-joint, pierced for the string, stamped with the Plate on one face and an empty circle on the other, worth a day's bread. Ten **crumbs** to the lack, twenty lacks to the **platter**. Struck at Saltbridge since 212 A.G. from bone the Company calls cattle. *The lack is thin this year* means bread is dear. (Compiler: the empty circle is either "the bread to be bought" or a chair seen from above. I have looked at it from above. I recommend not doing so.)]
[entry: lack-bitten | adj., Rim | Of a coin: clipped at the edge by someone shaving bone dust to sell as tooth-powder. Of a person: thin in a way that shows at the wrists and the temples. *Lack-bitten lacks for lack-bitten folk* (said of the change in a Thimble Cross tavern).]
[entry: Lack keep away | phr., common | The grace said before eating with strangers, everywhere on the Table, and the oldest sentence anyone still uses. Omitting it is the universal insult, worse than a blow, and in some inns a cause for the bill to be doubled. Said fast in the Fatlands, signed in Oathen, mouthed in the Vigil, and in the Fast followed by *and keep the Host away*, under the breath. See **grace**.]
[entry: lackling | n., Rim, cant | A child born in the Lack season, held unlucky on the Rim and lucky in the Fast. Crumb-Runners prefer lacklings as carriers, because they are used to cold and expect nothing.]
[entry: ladder-hand | n., Sallowreach | The lowest Loft-Warden: one lamp, one key to one hall, and a list of the Set-Aside to be read aloud to. Ladder-hands are mostly orphans and the very old. Their wage is three lacks a week and whatever the shelves whisper.]
[entry: Laden | n. and title, Fatlands | 1. Ummer, the eaten god, *the Laden*. 2. A Fatlander at **the Course**, swollen past proportion, feet rooting, skin gone soft and loamy. Among the Gut-lines it is a title, used on letters: *To the Laden Dame Marrable, at table*. Among Scraplings it is said with the hand over the mouth, as you might say *dying*. A Laden person who roots becomes a hill; see **Dowager Hills**, **Seating**.]
[entry: ladle | n., Fatlands | 1. A Bailiff's badge of office, the lowest rank of the Board's men, carried at the belt. 2. *v.* To force-feed under the Feasting Laws, gently or otherwise. *They ladled him at the crossroads and he thanked them for it after; he had no choice, his mouth was full.* See **Gavage**.]
[entry: Lain Book | n., Vigil | The ledger kept by Teodor Rell of the Slope, in which the notes the Lain-Down leave behind are copied out and sent with them to the Dormitory. It is the saddest book on the Table and the Watch has twice tried to have it burned. People queue on the Slope to read a page aloud, so that the note will have been heard once.]
[entry: Lain-Down | n., Vigil | A Vigilant who lay down on purpose. The law says **voluntary rest** and fines the nudger; the city says *went to the room* and does not discuss it. Those left go and stand at the foot of the bed, which has a number and no grave, for the rest of their lives. *My brother is Lain-Down. No, he is well. He is at bed fifty-eight thousand. He looks very well.* See **Lain Book**.]
[entry: Lain Lane | n., Vigil | Any street where a Lying-Down has happened; specifically the lane behind Gallow Street, where the neighbourhood walks the long way round by Thimble rather than pass it. *Don't go by the Lain Lane, love; take the stair.*]
[entry: lamp-friend | n., Vigil | A stranger you have stood beside under the same lamp for long enough to have seen the same Seeming. A lamp-friend has a claim on you for one favour. Lovers on the Slope begin as lamp-friends; the phrase is also used, bitterly, for people who only know each other through shared hallucination.]
[entry: lamp-meat | n., Vigil, cant | The tallow in the lamps of the Row and the poorer streets, which the Lamplighters' Guild buys from wherever it can. *Smell that? Lamp-meat. Somebody's uncle is lighting the corner.* (Compiler: the Guild calls it economy. Wick Tallow calls it a partnership.)]
[entry: Lancing | n., Rim | A massacre carried out by the **Purgation** against a high-Cut family, named for the lancet and the lancing-spear. Lancings are announced the night before by a pewter bowl left upturned on the doorstep. The family usually knows what the bowl means. The neighbours always do. The word is also used for any act of violence done *for someone's own good*, and the Vigil's Wakers have adopted it with pride.]
[entry: land-drowned | adj., Brinehollow | Drowned in bed for want of bailing, the lungs filled from the inside with sea that came from nowhere. The body is easy to know: the lips are white with salt and there is kelp in the throat. *Land-drowned* is the coast's commonest death and its least spoken of, because someone was supposed to wake them.]
[entry: lardlord | n., Fatlands, insult | A Gut-line landowner, heavy with rent and fat, who will not leave his bed to see the land he owns and could not if he wished. Said by tenant-farmers behind the hand, and once, in 455, through a speaking-trumpet in the square at Sated, after which the speaker was fed until he stopped.]
[entry: Last Bell | n., Rim | The dusk bell that closes the lawful market at Gristmoot and other Company towns. What is sold after it is sold in the pens behind the stockyards, a bowshot and a half from Company law. *After Last Bell* is Rim slang for anything you do not want written down.]
[entry: Last Cake | n., Rim | The final thing a Crumb-Runner eats at Wanting Post before crossing inward with the grain, bought from the Company counter and never finished; the half left on the plate is for luck. Innkeepers at the Post keep the half-cakes on a shelf, by name, and throw them out only when the Runner comes back.]
[entry: Lastgate | n., Sallowreach | The capital of the fens, on pilings, where the border road ends. Its lower storeys have been sinking for centuries and are inhabited all the way down. *Lastgate manners*: courtesy so slow that it takes a century to deliver an insult, and does.]
[entry: Lasting | n., Sallowreach | The dread of a living fen-child that she will never be allowed to finish. Mothers whisper *no Lasting for you* over cradles, as an Oathener would whisper *if able*. See **Long life**.]
[entry: Last Verse | n., Sallowreach | The final verse of a song, which in the fens does not exist, since nothing there can finish. Bram Hollin, the Last Mourner, knows the old funeral rite with its Last Verse, and is the only man alive who does. To *sing someone the Last Verse* is to Close them, or to wish it. A Last Verse heard sung aloud in Lastgate is a Dread 1 event; people weep in the street.]
[entry: Lean | n. and title, Fatlands | The second rank of **the Thin**: one who has been taught where the safe barns are and been given a share of the ash-bread. Also the Board's word for any fasting rebel, used in warrants. *Wanted: one Lean, sex uncertain, weight unrecorded, believed to be eating nothing in the Bleeding Orchards.*]
[entry: Lean House | n., Fast | The building at the edge of Wanting where the omitted go when their names are left off the Pantry list, to lie down among others in the same case and wait, with dignity, in clean linen. It has never been full and never been empty. *She walked to the Lean House* is the Fast's commonest obituary.]
[entry: Lean Levy | n., Fatlands | A tax on the thin, assessed on the bones that can be counted from across a room. The Board calls it a tax on the withheld portion; the Thin call it a bounty on ribs. Levy-men carry callipers. *He paid the Lean Levy and still had money left; he couldn't have been very thin.*]
[entry: Leathering | n., Sallowreach | The stage of the long rot at which a Kept body has dried, darkened and toughened like an old saddle, around the second century of being upright. Leathered grandmothers are sharp as vinegar and hard to restitch, because the needle will not go through. *She's at the Leathering* means she is old, proud and impossible.]
[entry: Leavings | n., common | Any part of a high-Cut body, a Seated place or a god-touched thing that still holds some of the god, and is cut, kept, traded and used: a ninth joint, a Laden pear, a Tenanted tear. The trade has its own book (*Seven Tables*, *The Carving Knife*). On the Rim the word also means simply what is left on the plate. *Leavings to the dogs and the dogs to the Board.*]

[pull] On the Rim, three words in five are about food, and the other two are about who is going to pay for it. | — the Compiler

[entry: Ledger, the | n., Oathen | The cliff face at Tacit into which every vow of public record has been carved: four miles long and growing by a yard a week. Scribes cut it by lamplight in the season of Carving. To *be on the Ledger* is to have made a public promise. To *be off the Ledger* is to be a child, a foreigner or a liar. A miscut on the Ledger in 640 broke a man who had kept his word perfectly, and nobody has explained it.]
[entry: Left Hand, the | title, Sallowreach | The chief Closer of the Mortuary Court, at present Lady Corrow Vane, whose gloves are sewn on. *Gone to the Left Hand* is a polite way of saying someone has bought a Closing and is, at last, about to be properly dead. Children play *Left Hand, Right Hand*, a touching game in which the one who is touched has to lie down and not get up until the next round.]
[entry: Lending, the | n., Brinehollow | The coast's name for the doctrine of the Gift: Dromm lent itself to the hungry, and the sea is coming back to collect what was lent. Lending-folk are gentle with the Tenanted. *It's not stealing if it was lent* is what Lending-folk say of the Calling, and what nobody else on the coast can bear to hear.]
[entry: lending a light | phr., Vigil | Keeping a stranger awake for a while, unpaid, as a kindness: walking beside them, pinching them, talking. *Lend us a light, mister, my nudger's gone.* It is the commonest beggar's line on the Slope and the commonest way robbers open a conversation.]
[entry: Lessening, the | n., Vigil | The quiet party in the Watch of Watches that would like the number of the Counted to be reduced. Its members never say how. Its badge, never worn openly, is a pillow embroidered with a single stitch. *A Lessening sort of man* is a man you would not leave alone at a bedside.]
[entry: Licenser | title, Sallowreach | A clerk of the Mortuary Court who drafts and seals Closing licenses, and may move one name a season up the waiting list. Licensers are the most bribed people in the north and the most hated, after the dead.]
[entry: Licked Clean | adj. and n., common | 1. A new moon, the Plate empty; nobody sets out on a licked-clean night, doors are barred, plates are not counted. 2. On the dice, double ones: automatic failure and a Lack. 3. Utterly ruined, with nothing left on the bone. *We came back licked clean: no mules, no coin, and Harrow's left hand.*]
[entry: licker | n., common, insult | A Scrapling, in the mouths of the high: one descended from those who licked the bones. Among Scraplings, the word is a boast at harvest and a fighting word in the mouth of anyone else. *Licker's luck*: little god, little to lose.]
[entry: Lid, the | n., Vigil | 1. The plateau on which the city stands. 2. The great closed eye at the far end of the long dim room in the sleepers' dream. *Under the Lid* means asleep, gone. *Over the Lid* means abroad, anywhere but the Vigil. Divers who have come within thirty paces of the Lid say it is not quite shut.]
[entry: lid-salt | n., Vigil | The salt crust that forms on lidless eyes between wettings, scraped off by the Eye-Wetters and sold as a love-charm on the Slope. Real lid-salt from a noble eye fetches a lack a pinch. Most of it is scraped from the corners of beggars. See **tear-salt**.]
[entry: Listener | n., several lands | A rank, or an office, in at least four orders: the Wreckwives (who listen at the wall for a Tenanted spouse's second voice), the Dream-Divers (who sit at the top of the tether and hear the diver's breath), the Barren Order, and the Echo-Wardens of Oathen. All four kinds of Listener go deaf in the end, in different ways.]
[entry: listen twice | phr., Oathen | Advice to anyone in the canyons: a thing you hear once may be a person; a thing you hear twice is an echo, and binds. *Listen twice before you nod.*]
[entry: Lists, the | n., Fatlands | The reading of the Reaping's missing in every village square on Carving afternoons. Not a holy day; everyone attends. The reader is chosen by lot and may not stop to weep. *She went off the Lists* means she was found, which happens about once a decade, and was not glad of it.]
[entry: Lofts, the | n., Sallowreach | The warehouses of the Set-Aside in Lastgate: nine storeys of shelving, forty thousand of the too-far-gone wrapped and labelled and awake, whispering. The sound is audible from the street. *Shelf-talk* is what the Lofts say; *Loft-wise* is what you are after a week of listening to it. Lately the whole building says one word. See **whisper-book**.]
[entry: Long Count, the | n., Fatlands | Pell Hogget's tally of the Reaping, begun in Low Tilth and copied now in two hundred villages, on notched staves, on barn doors, in the backs of Feasting Law primers. The numbers rise every year. *Keep the Long Count* is a Thin blessing and a Board offence.]
[entry: Long Grace, the | n., common | The age itself: the six hundred and forty-one years between the meal and the bill, a long pause like a grace said before eating, before the gods finish growing back. *The Grace is ending* is said at every bad harvest, late rain, odd birth and new Hush, and is said more often every year.]
[entry: Long Lack, the | n., common | The nine years of famine before the Gorging, numbered backward by scholars (Lack 1 to Lack 9) and by nobody else at all. Each year has a name: the Thin Harvest, the Empty Nets, the Barren Stalls, the Ash Bread, the Leather Winter, the Kitchen Year, the Bone Dole, the Eldest Walk, the Turning. *As long as the Lack* means forever.]
[entry: long life | excl., Sallowreach | The worst curse in the fens, and the most dangerous thing a foreigner can say there by accident. Said to a Kept person it is a slap; said to a living one it is a threat. Fen children who quarrel say it to each other and are whipped. The proper wish is **May you finish**.]
[entry: Long Table, the | n., Fast | The valley road where six hundred years of the Seated sit at one continuous table, nine miles end to end, lifting empty forks together. The sound of them chewing is like a herd grazing. *Gone to the Long Table* means lost to the Host. Do not look for anyone there for long. People who look for long enough find a chair.]
[entry: loose | adj., Sallowreach | Of the Kept: coming apart at a seam, in public. Falling loose has been a fineable offence since the Stitching Laws of 130 A.G. *He's gone loose at the jaw again, poor soul; fetch the Seamer and a cloth.* A *loose-parts man* is a part-thief or a back-alley seamer; see **Marrow Jack**.]
[entry: lose the bottom | phr., Brinehollow | Originally a pilot's phrase, when the sounding-lead finds nothing. Now: to sense the pull, to stand too long at a west-facing window, to be on the way to being Called. Hask Tarn will not go near the places where he once lost the bottom, though they are dry land now.]
[entry: lowing | n., Fatlands, cant | Drovers' word for the sound Sal Trotter's herds make on the road at night, which is not quite the sound cattle make. *They were lowing in words again by the river.*]
[entry: Lowmark | n., Brinehollow | The capital: a pier-city nineteen miles long built out across the dry seabed after the retreating sea, a mile a year. Its quarters are called Miles, numbered from the old shore. *Living on the Twelfth Mile* is living in the slums; *living on the First* is living on the old money and the old smell.]
[entry: Low Water | n., Brinehollow | The lowest tide of the year, in late Carving, when the Tenanted gather on the Uncovered and sing, and families watch from the rail of the Mile. *She sings at Low Water* means she is Tenanted. Ilse Marl's transcriptions of the singing, laid end to end, make a chart.]
[entry: Lullaby, the | n., Vigil | The Vigil's name for the doctrine of the Gift: Iss gave its sleep so that the city could be awake, and the room is where it keeps what it was given. The faith of most ordinary Vigilants, and the one hymn the Wakers allow to be hummed, never sung.]
[entry: Lung-lines | n., Brinehollow | The noble houses descended from those who ate Dromm's lungs, chief among them the Vashts. They cannot breathe air for long, and hold court from glass tanks. *Lung-proud* means vain, and the poor of the Mile use it of anyone who holds their breath to look important.]
[entry: lying-in | n., Cradlewrack | Any confinement at all, of anyone, of any sex or age, for any reason, since in the highlands anyone may come to bear. *The Lying-In*, capitalised, is the Attendance's fortress hospital, where the births nobody speaks about are taken. *She's gone to the Lying-In* is said of a woman who will not be seen again, and of her child, and of whatever it was.]

## M

[entry: maggot-wise | adj., Sallowreach | Wise in the way of the very old dead: slow, cold, entirely right and of no use to anyone living. From Justice Ever Lathe, the Honorable Maggot, whose rulings are buzzed and translated. *Maggot-wise counsel* is advice that will be correct in a century.]
[entry: make them last | phr., Fast | What Unfed mothers say when they hand children a crust. Also the motto over the Pantry door. In the Vigil the same phrase is said of rouse-tabs, bitterly.]
[entry: Making Good | n., Fast | The Unfed rite by which an omitted household that has survived a winter without the Pantry is put back on the list, and must thank the Abstinent who left them off, by name, aloud, at the Pantry window. *She made good last Grace and has not spoken to Elder Wend since, except to say thank you.*]
[entry: Mangling | n., common | The table of Injuries the body takes at Ruin. Physicians' slang: *a Mangling* is any wound bad enough that the surgeon reaches for the saw before the needle. *Mangled* in the fens is a compliment to a Seamer's work: so ruined that only a master could have made it stand.]
[entry: Marbling | n., Fatlands | The first frost, when the fat in the soil whitens and the plains look like a cut of meat from horizon to horizon. On Marbling Morning children run barefoot on the white veins. Some years the veins form shapes. *Marbled* means rich and also means very old.]
[entry: mardy | adj., child., Fatlands | Sulky from hunger, which in the south is the only kind of sulk there is. *Don't be mardy, there's a fifth meal coming.* Fatland grandmothers say a mardy child has *the field in its belly*, and give it a heel of bread to chew while it cries.]
[entry: marrow-jack | n., Sallowreach, cant | Any part-thief: one who takes limbs from the Kept while they shelve or sleep and sells them to back-alley seamers. From the most notorious, whose victims wake able to feel what their missing hands are being used for. *Marrow-jacked*: missing a piece, and aware of it.]
[entry: marrow-mouth | n., Fatlands, insult | A greedy person, one who cracks the bones at the wake before the family has had the joints. Also used, approvingly, of babies.]
[entry: marrying the water | phr., Brinehollow | Being Called. A gentle phrase, said by the Deepening and by grandmothers. *She married the water in her sixtieth year and we think she was happy.* The Netwatch use it with contempt.]
[entry: Matron | title, Vigil | The Keeper of the Dormitory, at present Cecily Dorm, the only living person who knows the number. *Matron's count* is any total you are not allowed to hear. *Matron's pillow*: an act of mercy that the law calls murder. Nobody says the second phrase above a whisper.]
[entry: May you finish | phr., Sallowreach | The polite greeting and farewell of the fens. Said to the Kept with feeling, to the living with affection, to the Hand-lines with care, since from them it can sound like a request. The reply is *In time*. See **long life**.]
[entry: meal-mouthed | adj., Rim | Speaking of horror only in food-words, so as never to say it plainly: *she was cleared*, *he's been served*, *they were picked*. The Compiler is told she is meal-mouthed herself. The whole Table is. The first line of every history says it plainly, and then nobody does again.]
[entry: Measure | n., Fast | The Pantry ration: one oatcake, a handful of grain, sometimes a strip of dried Rim fish, weighed out by Mother Stint Avery's clerks. *Short measure* is the worst thing that can happen in a winter. *Full measure*, said of a person, means honest. An Orrum table sometimes lays out a Measure, but whole, and hot, and buttered to the edges, the measure as it should have been.]
[entry: Measure-Bearer | title, Fast | The lowest servant of the Pantry, who carries the day's rations to the window and is searched on the way in and the way out, by two clerks, every day of their life. *Honest as a Measure-Bearer*: honest because watched.]
[entry: meat scrip | n., Fatlands | The Board's money: greased paper slips redeemable for a weight of meat at any Board store (the pound, the stone, the side). Its value falls every Carving with the Reaping, and Pell Hogget's tally-keepers chart the two together. *Scrip-fat*: rich on paper. *Paid in scrip*: paid in somebody.]
[entry: Member | title, Fatlands | One who holds a vote on the Board of Plenty by weight. A Member is immune from the dusk shift. *He's Member size* is a compliment. *He's lost his seat* means he has lost forty pounds, or died, which in the Fatlands are about equally shameful.]
[entry: Mercy | n., Rim | 1. A Fast name, given to girls born in a hard winter. 2. In Purgation cant, the Black Draught, the emetic given free on Retching Day at the Weeping Post. *Take your Mercy* means drink and bring it up. Dr. Mercy Thrane, who is from the Fast, does not find this funny.]
[entry: mercy-pinch | n., Vigil | A pinch given to a child who has begun to blink, hard enough to bruise, by a stranger in the street. Mothers thank the stranger. The bruise is called a *kiss*.]
[entry: mere-light | n., Sallowreach | The glow on the fen at night from Wick Tallow's candles drifting on cork, upright and lit, leaning their flames toward passing punts. Following a mere-light is a fen child's dare. Children who follow it come home in the morning and say the candle told them its name.]
[entry: Middle Plate | n., common | The second month of any season, named for the moon. Dates are given *the ninth of Middle Plenty*. *Middle-plated* is Rim slang for a person of no particular class, neither Scrapling nor Prime, a common Cut 2. See Appendix, *Numbers, Measures and Time*.]
[entry: Midwife | title, Cradlewrack | A member of the Attendance, judge, physician, priest and executioner in one apron, who asks the Two Questions at every birth and answers them herself. *The Midwife's Two Questions*: *What is it? Does it stay?* Highland children play Midwife with dolls, and the dolls do not always stay.]

[pull] Fen children learn three words before they learn their names: *shut*, *stitch*, and *finish*. In that order, says Mother Gall, if they're lucky. | — the Compiler

[entry: Mile | n., Brinehollow | One of the numbered sections of Lowmark's pier-city. Each Mile has its own bell, its own netwatch post and its own reputation. *A Mile-cart* is the long rail-trolley that runs the length of the pier; *riding the Mile* is commuting, and also being slowly drawn west.]
[entry: Mile-mad | adj., Brinehollow | Restless in the way of the poor of the far Miles: walking out to the pier's end at night to watch the water, which is always a little further off. Mile-madness is the Appetite of the coast, in the mouths of people who will not say *the pull*.]
[entry: mither | v., Brinehollow | To fuss over someone's bail-bucket: to check its rope, its rim, its smell, the colour of what is in it. Mothers mither. Sweethearts who have exchanged buckets mither each other. *Stop mithering, I coughed clean this morning.*]
[entry: mittens | n., Sallowreach | Worn by sufferers at the Appetite, to stop the compulsive closing of lids, books, shutters and the eyes of strangers. Wool for the Taste, leather for the Appetite, lead for the Course. *He's in his mittens* means he is not to be trusted near the infirm. Osric Vane's are lead and locked.]
[entry: Moot | n., Rim | A Company market town, especially Gristmoot, where every stall-keeper on the road comes at last, and the Grace Bell at noon stops three miles of stalls so everyone can turn to the nearest stranger and say the grace. *Moot-honest*: honest only within earshot of the Weighhouse bell.]
[entry: morning count | n., Brinehollow | The dawn calling of names through the party wall, house to house, the length of a street, so that silence will show who was Called in the night. *Missed the count* means dead or gone. In every party wall there is a worn place, sometimes carved as an ear, where the names are called. See **plank-ear**.]
[entry: Mortuary Court | n., Sallowreach | The council of the Hand-lines under the Unvacated Regent, sellers and hoarders of death. *To have friends at the Court* is to be expected to die on time. *Court-dead*: Closed by license, as opposed to Hush-dead or foreign-dead.]
[entry: mothered | adj., Cradlewrack | Descended from a particular bearer, who may not have been a woman, or a person. *I am mothered of Hessa Gorse, and through her of the well at Rudge* is a respectable pedigree in the highlands, with a footnote.]
[entry: Mother of Cords | title, Cradlewrack | The eldest of **the Uncut**, whose cord has grown down into the red clay and become a **Root**; through it she feels the pulse of the land, and says it has two beats now. *By the Mother of Cords* is an Uncut oath; it is taken holding someone else's cord.]
[entry: Mother of the Water | title, Brinehollow | The head of **the Deepening**, at present Mother Limpet, who has walked two thousand into the sea at night with lanterns and says she will follow when the last is safely under. *Mother-of-the-Water patience*: the patience of someone who is going to win.]
[entry: moth-wife | n., Vigil | A Seeming that walks: a woman made of moths, who comes down the lane dimming each lamp she passes and smelling of flour. Several districts have entered her in the public record. She is said to stop in front of people who are about to blink for the last time.]
[entry: mourner | n., Sallowreach | A trade six centuries extinct: one paid to weep at a death, back when there were deaths. Bram Hollin is the last, and in demand now the Hushes have come. *Hire a mourner* is fen slang for *make a fuss*, and has suddenly stopped being a joke.]

[entry: warm front | n., Fast | The god passing. The Unfed track Orrum by melted frost and warm ground, and call it *company*, or *a warm front*. *There's a warm front by the mill* means keep the children in, do not answer the door, and if you smell bread, walk into the wind.]
[entry: Washing, the | n., common | The night in late Plenty when each village's walk-stone is washed by someone who will not say who. It is a custom of every land, and nobody remembers starting it. *Washed and not told*: forgiven by somebody you do not know.]
[entry: Watch of Watches | n., Vigil | The Vigil's council, which sits in shifts around the clock and has not adjourned in two hundred years, on the Nodding Stair. *The Watch's watch*: the attendants who watch the Warden-Prime's eyes. *Who watches the Watch* is a proverb in the Vigil and a job title.]
[entry: watch-self | n., Vigil | The self that has gone on watching after the mind has failed: a long-waking Vigilant who works, walks and answers questions, and is not there. *My husband's watch-self brought me tea this morning* means he is gone, though he is awake.]
[entry: wax-slate | n., Oathen | A board of wax and a stylus, for messages that can be smoothed away before they bind. Since writing began to count, wax-slates are scraped clean with frightening speed. *Wax-slate love*: a love affair conducted with deniability.]
[entry: weep | n., Sallowreach | Rain, which in the fens leaves a tea-coloured stain. *Weep-weather*: wet and warm. *Weep-stained*: of a person, old, faded, respectable. Lowlanders say that a fen woman who cries is *making her own weather*.]
[entry: Weeping Post, the | n., Rim | The waystation between Cradlewrack and Fogmouth, where weep-rain from the fens meets the warm salt springs of the red hills and the road is always wet; the Purgation's chief hall, where the Black Draught is given on Retching Day. *Weeping-Post wet*: soaked through, inside and out.]
[entry: Weighing, the | n., Fatlands | The annual public weighing of the Board of Plenty, three days in Grace, at which each Member's vote is set by weight, with pie-stalls the length of Sated. *Weighed and found heavy*: approved. Garrick Tripe will fill your pockets with lead for a fee.]
[entry: welcome | n., Fast | A forbidden word. Orrum's Steward is called Welcome Ardent, which the Unfed find unbearable. *You are welcome* is never said in the Fast; the Unfed say *you are tolerated*, and mean it as warmly as anyone else means the other. See **No, thank you**.]
[entry: well-crowned | adj., Cradlewrack | Of a well: dilated into a crowning. *The well in the square has crowned* means something is coming up the rope hand over hand, knocking on the stones as it climbs. *Well-crowned* in Kest slang also means pregnant, of a man.]
[entry: west door | n., Brinehollow | The door of a house that faces the sea, barred every night of the year but Tablenight, when it is left unbarred for whoever might come home. *West-door news*: a return. *West-door niche*: the shrine by it where a bowl of seawater stands.]

[entry: wet-lung | n., Rim, insult | A Brinehollower, on the rest of the Rim. They cough in public and spit salt. *Wet-lung manners*: wiping your mouth on your sleeve after bailing at table, which on the coast is perfectly polite.]
[entry: wet-nurse | n., Cradlewrack | One who feeds what no one else will, like Dolour Pym, to whom the Attendance sends the births it permits but cannot look at. She loves all of them. Several have never let go. *Wet-nursed* means raised on pity and grown up strange.]
[entry: Wet Tom | n., Brinehollow | Any very wet beggar; after Old Wet Tom, who has never been dry and on whom it rains indoors. *A Wet Tom*: a person followed by misfortune. Children who look into his puddles say they are much deeper than the floor.]
[entry: wheel, the | n., Rim | The Company's symbol, and the seven-Spoked council that runs it. *On the wheel*: employed by the Company. *Broke on the wheel*: ruined by it, which is to say indebted to a Company inn for life.]
[entry: whisper-book | n., Sallowreach | A Loft-Warden's ledger of what the Set-Aside whisper, kept hall by hall. Senior Wardens may sell what is in them. *It's in the whisper-books* means it is known by the dead and will be known by everyone, eventually.]
[entry: whistle | v., Oathen | A forbidden act on Tablenight, when the whole country makes no sound for twenty-four hours. A whistler is shunned for a year. *To whistle on Tablenight* means to commit an outrage in innocence.]
[entry: white-glove | adj., Sallowreach | Of a Closer who has never yet closed anyone; and of any newcomer to a killing trade. *Still white-gloved*: virginal in murder.]
[entry: wick | n., Vigil | The lowest Lamplighter, who trims the ten thousand lamps on long poles, burned at the wrist and coughing smoke. *Wick-thin*: underfed and burning. *Wick-talk*: the Lamplighters' cant, of which this Compiler learned only enough to know she was being insulted in it.]
[entry: wickerman | n., Fatlands, child. | The scarecrow of any field, especially the one at Low Tilth. Fatlands children chant *Wickerman, wickerman, turn round and see*, and run before it can. In some villages they have stopped playing it.]
[entry: Wider Table | n., Rim | The whole Table, in a sermon; also a Rim euphemism for the dead of all lands together, as in *gone to the wider table*. In the Fast, nobody says it.]
[entry: Widow's Dripping | n., Fatlands | The first rendering of a wake, on hot bread; the widow skims it herself and weeps into it. *Widow's-dripping tears*: tears that are also seasoning. The phrase is used of all grief that has to be got through while cooking.]
[entry: wife-bucket | n., Brinehollow | The bail-bucket a sweetheart gives you, exchanged at betrothal. *She's still got his wife-bucket* means she has not remarried, though he is ten years in the sea. A Tenanted sweetheart's bucket is a Leaving, and the water in it does not go stale.]
[entry: window-warden | n., Brinehollow | A neighborhood volunteer who walks the street at night looking for people standing at west-facing windows, and knocks to wake them. *A window-warden's knock* is a single soft tap. The Called hear it and do not turn.]
[entry: wishbone | n., Fatlands | The forked bone of a bird pulled between two eaters at a wake; whoever gets the larger piece gets the dead person's luck. In the Gut-lines, wishbones are pulled from more than birds. *Wishbone siblings*: brothers who have fought over an inheritance at the table itself.]
[entry: Witness, the | n., common | 1. Tolm. 2. In Rim law, the person before whom a contract is sworn, at present Jessamy Quill. If she dies or breaks, half the trade of the Table comes unbound in an afternoon. *Witness-heavy*: the burden of being needed to remember.]
[entry: Wolf-Warden | title, Fatlands | A Board officer appointed to protect the fields from wolves, of which there are none. Wolf-Wardens walk the dusk lines in white smocks with a lantern, and their lists are the Cullmasters' lists. *Wolf-Warden's leave*: the dusk shift.]
[entry: wolves | n., Fatlands, euphemism | The official cause of the Reaping. There are no wolves in the Fatlands. *Wolves took him* is said with a straight face by Board officials and with a look by everyone else. The Board employs Wolf-Wardens, who hunt nothing and are well paid.]
[entry: word-bound | adj., Oathen | Bound by your own words, in the flesh. The commonest Oathen adjective, applied to almost everyone. *Not word-bound* is said only of Blanks and foreigners, and of Cassian Wry, whom everyone asks to say something false, just to hear it.]
[entry: worship | n., common | In Table-speech the same root as eating: **orrel** and **orren**. A Fatlands priest says *come and worship*; a Fatlands child asks what is for supper. [entry: wrack | n., Cradlewrack | 1. The red clay highlands themselves, *the Wrack*. 2. Seaweed, in the speech of the highland coast. 3. Ruin, labor, pain. *In the wrack* means in labor, which in Cradlewrack may be said of anyone, and of the land.]
[entry: Wreckwife | n., Brinehollow | One of the sisterhood of those whose spouses came back Tenanted; they share advice on how to live with a stranger who knows your name. Ranks run Listener, Sister of the Line, Keeper of Names, Elder Wife, Widow-Mother. *Wreckwife's tea*: kept warm for two.]
[entry: wristlet | n., Rim | The iron wrist-ring of a Bonded Hand of the Company, which never comes off. *Wristleted*: in the Company's employ for life. Retired Hands rub the place where it was, years later, without knowing they do it.]
[entry: Writ of Weight | n., Fatlands | The Board certificate recording a Member's weight and vote, renewed yearly. Forged Writs of Weight are the commonest fraud in Sated. *A Writ of Weight man*: one who is less than he claims.]
[entry: wyrm-hole | n., Brinehollow | A hole in the Uncovered where the mud has been pushed up from below, as if something had come out, or gone down. Salvagers mark them with painted stakes. *Gone down the wyrm-hole*: missing on the seabed.]

## Y

[entry: Yardmaster | title, Fatlands | A Renderers' Union officer commanding a rendering-house, with a share of export profits. *Yardmaster's word*: final, and stamped twice.]
[entry: yard-warden | n., Sallowreach | A volunteer who watches the Lofts' yards at night so that nothing is carried out, or in. The yard-wardens are the first to hear the whispering change. Lately they have asked to be paid more.]
[entry: Yawn, the | n., Vigil | The warm wind off the eastern plain. On Yawn days the Wakers double their patrols. *Yawn-weather*: dangerous comfort. *A yawn* in the Vigil is never small talk: it spreads through a room like a rumor and can put down a market. See **Glass House**.]
[entry: yawner | n., Vigil | One whose yawns are catching beyond reason, like Lukas Marre, who once put down a whole market square and lives in the Glass House, masked, alone. *Don't be a yawner* means do not spread despair.]
[entry: yawn-tax | n., Vigil | The fine for yawning in public on a Yawn day. It is a rouse-tab, paid on the spot to the nearest Waker. *He's paying the yawn-tax* means he is losing.]
[entry: Yellowing | n., Sallowreach | The season in Carving when children gather the rare leaves that fall from the yellow trees of the fens, where leaves stay on the branch for decades, and keep them in boxes. A fallen leaf is the only thing in the north that has ever been allowed to finish. *A yellow-leaf child* is a fen child who has died properly, abroad, and is envied.]
[entry: yes | interj., Oathen | A promise. Oatheners avoid it. Sorrin Vael, Chief Clausewright, has not said an unqualified *yes* since boyhood, including to his wife. The polite form is *as I now intend*, or a nod, which is held not to bind, which is being tested.]
[entry: yesterbed | n., Vigil, archaic | A bed, in the old Ludmere speech: *the thing from yesterday*. Used now only in nursery rhymes and by the Bedded, who say it with longing. *Yesterbed and yesterdream, never and never wake.*]
[entry: yield | v., Fast | To give way: to sit at the table, to answer *come in*, to accept on the first offer. *Yield* is also a girl's name, given in hope that she never will. Yield Hartsell has grown a single green sprout in dead soil, and told no one.]
[entry: yoke-ox | n., Fatlands | One of the eight oxen that draw Chairman Obb Tullow's dray. The Chairman's feet rooted through the cart last spring. *Yoke-ox loyal*: going on pulling a thing that has stopped moving.]
[entry: yolk-eyed | adj., Fatlands | Glazed and yellow from overeating, in the last hour of a Feasting Law supper. A compliment from a host. *Yolk-eyed and happy*: the ideal guest.]
[entry: young-dead | n., Sallowreach | A Kept person who died young and stayed young, like the Smalls. *Young-dead envy*: a living sibling growing older than their dead brother or sister, and the dead one's look as they do.]
[entry: yourself | pron., Oathen | In Oathen courts, an accused person is addressed only as *yourself*, never by name, so that their name will not be bound to the sentence by accident before it is passed. *Yourself has broken* is the shortest verdict in the world.]
