# Engines of the Grace: Machinery and Devices

> There is no gunpowder on the Table. There is no need of it. We have found other ways to make a thing go off in a person's hands.
>> — Ambrel Coyle, clockwright of the Vigil, in the preface to his Book of Escapements, 371 A.G.

[fiction]
**A letter from a Rim Road tinker to her apprentice, found folded in a tin of brass pins sold at Gristmoot. The apprentice's name has been scraped off.**
You asked me what the trade is. I will tell you, since you will not stop asking, and since you have good hands and a bad memory, which is the right way round.
The trade is this. Every land on the Table has a god in it that is coming back, and every land has decided to hold the god down with a machine. Not a prayer. Prayers are cheap and everybody has one. A machine. The sleepless have engines that stab them on the quarter-hour. The coast has a bell that rings off a man's chest. The highlands have a clock that runs on a dead woman's brick and a frame for sewing strangers' hands together. The red canyons have traps for words. The fen has wax that makes a small silence and mittens of lead the size of a bucket. The Fatlands have a scale that has elected three governments. The Fast has an axe and an axe and an axe.
None of them work, girl. That is the first thing to know. They hold. Holding is not working. A thing that holds is a thing that will one day let go, and you must sell it to the customer as though it never will, and you must be standing a long way off on the day it does.
The second thing to know is that every one of these engines has a part in it that is a person. Look for it. It is always there. Sometimes it is the operator, sometimes the one who is operated upon, sometimes a body that was put in at the bottom when the thing was built and never taken out again. When you are asked to mend a machine, find the person in it first, and ask whether they would like to be mended, and then do the job anyway, because you have been paid.
The third thing to know is that brass goes green, iron goes red, lead goes white and people go every colour, and you must carry polish for all four.
Mind the pins. Some are dipped.
[/fiction]

This chapter describes the great and small machines of the Table: the engines by which seven peoples have tried to hold their eaten gods in check, measure them, steer them, profit by them, or follow them down. Some are famous and singular, like the *Follow* in its slip at Mile Nineteen or the Great Scales under the bone dome of the Weighhouse. Some are common trade goods sold on every Rim market, like lead mittens and jaw-wires. All of them have a history, an operator, a cost, and a way of going wrong.

Every device is described in the same order. First comes its **lore and history**: who made it, why, and what it has done. Then a **diagram in words**: a parts table and a plain account of how the thing works, written so that a Carver can describe it to the players, or a player whose character has Craft or Reckoning can understand it, sketch it, sabotage it, or build a worse one. Then **who builds it and who uses it**; then what it is like **in operation**, to every sense; then the **rules**; then a **d6 failure table**; then **cost and availability**; then a **hook**.

[box: Engines and the Grace Roll]
Using a device that is built for the purpose is normally automatic; the rules for each device say when a roll is needed. When one is, mechanisms answer to **Eye + Reckoning** (setting, reading, calibrating, timing), building and mending to **Hand + Craft**, and anything that is fitted to a living or Kept body to **Hand + Stitching**.
**Building** a device is a Long Task (see the Rules chapter): the device lists the difficulty, the time for each roll, and the number of successes needed. Materials must be bought first, at the listed cost, and are spent whether the work succeeds or not.
**Failure tables.** Every device has a d6 failure table. Roll on it when a roll to use the device is a **Lack**, when a device is used past its limits, when a saboteur has been at it, or whenever the device's own rules tell you to. A failure on an ordinary roll is just a failure; the table is for when the thing itself goes wrong.
**Sabotage.** Fouling a device without being noticed is Hand + Craft (or Hand + Filch for a quick job) against the device's **Sabotage** difficulty. Finding the fault afterwards is Eye + Reckoning at the same difficulty. Each Helping on the sabotage lets the saboteur choose the failure result instead of rolling it.
[/box]

## The Vigil Waking-Engine

[sigil: vigil]

> Tick, and prick. Tick, and prick. The engine does not love you. That is why it never tires of you.
>> — slogan painted over the door of the Rattlehouse on the Slope

### Lore and History

In the first winter after the First Night, when the city of Ludmere had lost half its people to the long room and had not yet renamed itself, every survivor needed a nudger, and there were not enough hands to go round. Spouses kept each other awake until one of them failed. Children were paid by the hour and fell asleep on the job. Whole streets went down together in the warm afternoon of the third day of Carving 341 because the last waking person in each house had nobody left to pinch them. What the city needed, said a clockwright named **Ambrel Coyle**, was a nudger that did not get tired, did not love you, and could not be bribed with a minute's rest.

He built the first **Waking-Engine** in his workshop on what is now Lampwrights' Row in the spring of 344: a spring-driven escapement in a walnut case, strapped to the back of a chair, that every ninety beats of its balance wheel drove a steel pin a quarter-inch into the sitter's thigh, rattled a tin clapper by their ear, and tightened a leather strap across their chest until they gasped. He sat in it himself for nine days and nights and came out raving, bloodied down both legs, and awake. The Watch of Watches bought the patent in 346. By 360 there was an engine in every guardhouse, every counting-room, and every orphanage.

The engines have grown since. The personal kind, called a **coffer** after the walnut case, is now carried on the back like a pedlar's pack and wired by cords to cuffs on the wrists and thighs. The civic kind, the **Great Engine**, fills a whole building: the Rattlehouse on the Slope, a long brick hall built in 388, where four hundred of the city's poorest stand in numbered stalls on a sloped floor while a single driveshaft the length of the hall turns cams that work four hundred arms, each arm tipped with a needle, a rattle, and a cold wet sponge. They pay a crumb a watch. It is the cheapest way to stay awake in the Vigil, and every night the Wakers carry out three or four who failed anyway, still standing in their stalls, eyes shut, the needle-arm going in and out of their thigh like a sewing machine.

Ambrel Coyle died in 371, awake, of a burst heart, in his own engine. He had written in the margin of his last drawing: *I have built a thing that keeps a man from sleeping. I have not built a thing that lets him rest. The city does not know the difference. I am beginning to.*

### The Diagram in Words

| Part | Material | Function |
| Mainspring and barrel | blued steel, brass drum | stores the force; wound by crank every watch |
| Balance wheel and hairspring | brass, steel | keeps the beat; ninety beats to a "prick" |
| Cam-drum | oak, iron pegs | sets the order of insults: pin, rattle, strap, sponge |
| Pin-cuffs (four) | leather, brass, steel pins | drive pins into wrists and thighs on the cam's word |
| Rattle-box | tin, dried peas | clatters at the ear |
| Chest-strap | waxed leather, ratchet | tightens on the breath, forcing a gasp |
| Lull-catch | brass feeler on the brow | senses a nodding head and fires everything at once |
| Wake-glass | small mirror on a stalk | holds a lamp's glare in the eyes |

The coffer works like any clock, except that its hands are needles and its face is the wearer. The mainspring drives a train of brass wheels that turns the cam-drum one full revolution every quarter-hour. Pegs on the drum lift levers in a set order: at the first peg a cuff fires its pin; at the second the rattle-box shakes; at the third the chest-strap ratchets one notch tighter and then lets go; at the fourth a sponge of cold water on a stalk slaps the neck. The order can be changed by moving the pegs, and a good Waker-valet sets a client's drum like a musician sets a tune, so that the insults come irregularly and the body cannot learn them. The cleverest part is the **lull-catch**, a brass feeler resting against the forehead: if the head drops by more than a finger's width, the feeler trips a sear and the whole drum fires at once, every pin, rattle and strap together. The wearer comes up screaming. That is the point.

The Great Engine is the same thing built into a building. A water-wheel in the Slope's main sewer turns the driveshaft; the shaft runs the length of the Rattlehouse; four hundred cams on it work four hundred stalls. There are no lull-catches in the Great Engine, because the Rattlehouse cannot afford them. Instead there are walkers: Rattlehouse boys who pace the aisles with Waker's needles and look for drooping heads.

### Who Builds It, Who Uses It

Coffers are made on Lampwrights' Row by six licensed clockwright families, of which the Coyle-Harrows are the oldest, and repaired on the Slope by everyone. The Wakers use them on prisoners and on themselves; the Watch of Watches uses gilded ones with silk cuffs, in which the pins are gold and the sponge is scented; clerks, night-porters, lamp-tenders and soldiers wear plain ones. Parents buy small coffers for children, with blunt pins, and the Watch pretends not to know that the pins are always sharpened within a year. The Rattlehouse is run by a Slope family, the Grainers, under a Watch licence, and is never empty. The Tuckers hate the engines: an engine-wearer does not need a minute's guarded sleep, and an engine that has been fouled is the commonest way a Tucker's competitor is put down.

### In Operation

A coffer ticks loudly, by design, because a tick is a comfort and comfort is death, and so the tick is pitched just off the beat of a resting heart, a little fast, so that the body feels hurried by it. The wearer smells of brass polish, sweat and old blood, because the pin-cuffs are never quite clean and the punctures on the thighs of a ten-year wearer are a lace of white scar and fresh scab, weeping clear fluid into the cloth. The first prick comes with a click and a cold small bite, and then the warm thread of blood. The rattle is a sound like a handful of teeth in a tin. The chest-strap is the worst: it squeezes until the ribs creak and the breath goes out in a grunt, and holds a moment too long, and lets go, and the lungs heave in a gasp that is half a sob. In the Rattlehouse four hundred of these happen in one long ripple down the hall, every quarter-hour, a wave of grunts and gasps and little cries, and then a silence full of ticking, and then the boys walking the aisles calling *up, up, up, up* in bored sing-song voices.

Old wearers twitch in time with their drums even when the engine is off. They flinch on the quarter-hour. Some of them cannot hear a clock without their thighs bleeding.

[pull] It's not the pin. You get used to the pin. It's the click before the pin. I hear it in my sleep. I don't sleep. I hear it anyway. | — Rattlehouse stall-holder, number 211

### Rules

**Wearing a coffer.** A wound coffer runs one watch (eight hours). While it runs, the wearer gains no Weariness from the watch's passing, as though they had taken rouse, and gains +2 on all blink checks; the lull-catch fires on any failed blink check before the blink resolves, cancelling it, but the wearer loses 1 Flesh and the rest of their action. Each watch in a coffer costs 1 Flesh from the pins, which heals normally. A coffer stacks with rouse: a character on rouse and in a coffer may shed 1 further Weariness each watch, but must make a Gut + Endure roll (Hard 14) at the end of the watch or gain the *Engine-Twitch* (−1 to all Hand rolls requiring stillness, such as Shooting, Stitching and Filch, until they have spent a full day out of the coffer).

**Stealth and comfort.** A coffer is Noisy. Its wearer is at −4 to Stealth. In the Vigil this is good manners; abroad, it is a confession.

**Torture.** A coffer set to a cruel tune by a Waker or a wake-valet is a torture device (see Torture in the Rules chapter): the operator rolls Hand + Reckoning instead of the usual roll, and the subject cannot blink, faint or fall unconscious while it runs. Anyone forced into a cruel coffer for a full watch makes a Dread 2 check.

**The Rattlehouse.** A stall costs a crumb a watch. A character in a stall makes no blink checks for the watch, but the Rattlehouse is a scene of monotony, and at the end of each watch the GM rolls a d20 for each PC; on a 1 the character has gone under in their stall and must be fetched as described under Falling Asleep, by a Rattlehouse boy whose Tongue + Intimidate is +5.

**Building a coffer** is a Long Task: Hand + Craft at Grim 18, a day per roll, five successes, materials 60 lacks. Its **Sabotage** difficulty is Hard 14.

### Failure Table (d6)

| d6 | The engine fails |
| 1 | The mainspring breaks with a crack like a slapped face. The engine stops. The silence afterward is so restful that the wearer must make a blink check at once, at +4. |
| 2 | The cam-drum slips a peg. Every insult comes at once, every beat, until someone cuts the cords: 1 Flesh per round, and the wearer cannot act except to scream. |
| 3 | The lull-catch sticks. The engine runs on, but the head may drop freely now. The wearer does not know. Their next failed blink resolves as normal, in harness, and the people around them assume the engine is working. |
| 4 | The tick falls into time with the wearer's resting heart. The engine has become a lullaby. +1 Weariness every hour until it is reset (Routine 10 Eye + Reckoning; the wearer must notice first). |
| 5 | A pin breaks off in the thigh and works its way in. Nothing happens for a week. Then, each morning, roll a d6: on 1–2 the wearer loses 2 Flesh and limps all day, until a surgeon digs the pin out (Hard 14 Hand + Stitching). |
| 6 | The wearer goes under in the engine and is Counted, and the engine goes on running. Nobody thinks to unstrap it. At the Dormitory they find the cuffs still pricking a sleeping body every quarter-hour, and the body still gasping on cue. Dread 3 for whoever finds it. They may not be allowed to take it off: the Matron says it is the only bed in the row that still breathes on its own schedule. |

### Hook

**The Stall That Never Empties.** Stall 400 in the Rattlehouse, at the very end of the hall, has been rented every watch for eleven years by somebody who pays in advance and is never seen to arrive or leave. Its needle-arm is always wet. The Grainers want to know who it is. The PCs, keeping watch through a night, see the arm working on nothing, quarter-hour by quarter-hour, and on the stroke of the Tablenight bell they see the empty stall gasp. When the Grainers finally open the stall's floor they find that the stall was built over Ambrel Coyle's first workshop, and that under the boards is a walnut chair with nobody in it, still strapped shut.

## Finn Undertow's Tide-Bell Mechanism

[sigil: brinehollow]

> The sea says dawn's at the half-bell. Your chests say it's now. I ring for the chests.
>> — Finn Undertow, Tide-Bellman of Lowmark

### Lore and History

Lowmark has rung a tide bell since before the Beaching. The first was a ship's bell hung on a post at the harbour mouth, struck by hand when the water turned so the fleet knew when to sail. After the Brimming of 60 A.G., when the first generation was born coughing brine, the bell took on a second task: to tell the city when to bail. For four hundred years the two tasks were one, because the tide in the chest rose and fell with the tide in the sea, and a bellman needed only to watch the water and ring.

The bell that hangs now on the Headland, called **Old Gannet**, was cast in 61 A.G. from the bronze fittings of the Admiralty's first stranded ships, and it is the size of a small room. In 402 the Bellmen's Company built a tower for it and fitted it with a **tide-engine**: a float in a stone well, the **Tide-Well**, fed by a culvert from the harbour, whose rise and fall turned a pegged drum that tripped the hammer at each turn of the tide. The bellman's work became the keeping of the engine.

Then the sea began to leave. The culvert ran dry in 530. The Bellmen kept a clockwork reckoner set by the Salt Assay's tables instead, and that was well enough until the body's tide began to drift from the sea's. By 600 the two were half an hour apart. By 630 they were two hours apart, and the Admiralty had ordered that the bell follow the sea, because the sea was the navy's business and the chest was not.

The Admiralty had tried, before that, to make the engine ring the chest. In 604 a Tenanted volunteer named **Ysolde Prow**, a net-maker's widow from Mile Three, was sealed into a glass cistern in the base of the tower with a leather cuff around her ribs and a cord from the cuff to the escapement, so that the slow fill and empty of her body's tide would drive the hammer. The Bench approved it. The Deepening blessed it. Her family were given a pension and a plaque. For three years the bell rang off Ysolde Prow's chest, and the city bailed in time. Then it drifted, exactly as before: a Tenanted chest, it turned out, followed something else, something deeper than the city's tide, and the engine rang the wrong hours for Lowmark as surely as the sea did. The Admiralty unhooked the cord and went back to the sea-drum. Nobody unsealed the cistern. Ysolde Prow is still down there, thirty-seven years later, in the base of the tower, in green water gone thick as broth, breathing slowly, and nobody remembers whose job it is to feed her. She was the city's sacrifice to the bell, and she was worth nothing.

**Finn Undertow** took the rope in 627. For four years he rang the sea, as ordered. Then one dawn he felt his own chest fill, and heard the whole Headland cough with him, a full hour before the sea-drum tripped, and he went down into the gear-room with a knife and a coil of tarred cord and wired the engine to himself.

### The Diagram in Words

| Part | Material | Function |
| Old Gannet | bronze, eleven tons | the bell; heard to Mile Twelve on a still day |
| Hammer and sear | iron, oak beam | strikes the bell when the sear is tripped |
| Sea-Drum | oak, iron pegs, clockwork | the old engine; trips the sear at the Assay's tide |
| Chest-Drum | brass, gut cord | the new engine; trips the sear off Finn's ribs |
| Selector lever | iron, brass plate marked SEA and CHEST | chooses which drum commands the hammer |
| Rib-cuff | tarred leather, whalebone stays | buckled around Finn's chest, day and night |
| The Count Wheel | brass, scratched by hand | Finn's drift-counter; a notch for every minute the tides close |
| The Prow Cistern | glass, iron bands, lead seal | the old chest-engine; disconnected, still occupied |

The tower is three storeys of grey stone. At the top hangs Old Gannet, with its hammer on an oak beam above it. In the gear-room beneath, two drums sit side by side, and a long iron selector lever chooses which of them will lift the hammer's sear. The **Sea-Drum** is the old engine, driven now by clockwork set to the Salt Assay's tables. The **Chest-Drum** is Finn's: a small brass drum with a ratchet, connected by forty feet of gut cord, running over pulleys and down through a hole in the floor, to a buckled leather cuff around Finn Undertow's ribs. When his chest fills with the body's tide, his ribs swell; the cord draws taut; the ratchet turns the drum by one tooth; and when it has turned far enough, the sear trips, and the bell rings for the chests of Lowmark.

Finn is roped to the tower, as bellmen always were, so that the Calling cannot walk him off the Headland. But his rope is short, because the cord is short. He sleeps, eats, bails and lives within forty feet of the Chest-Drum. Beside it, on the wall, is the **Count Wheel**: a brass wheel he has marked by hand, one scratch for every minute by which the body's tide has drifted from the sea's, and a second ring of scratches counting down the days until the drift closes. By the wheel, the tide in every chest on the coast will fill at the same moment at the Low Water of Carving 642.

At the bottom of the stair, behind a door that has been bricked and then unbricked and then bricked again, is the Prow Cistern.

### Who Builds It, Who Uses It

The Bellmen's Company of Lowmark, which is now Finn Undertow and two apprentices, keeps the engine. The Admiralty owns it and has three times ordered the selector set to SEA; each time Finn has set it to CHEST again, and the city has refused to support the Admiralty's officers in arresting him, because the city coughs when Finn rings, and it is easier to cough on time. Skerrow and the other coastal towns have their own bells and bellmen, and most of them still ring the sea. The Deepening want the cistern opened, and Ysolde brought out to be walked into the Trench with the honour she was owed. The Wreckwives bring her flowers, which they push through a crack in the bricks.

### In Operation

The tower smells of tar, wet bronze, and the sour green breath that rises up the stair from the cistern. It sounds of ticking, of gulls, and of Finn's breathing, which every visitor notices: slow, and deep, and a little too long, and accompanied, at each breath's end, by the small dry *click* of the ratchet forty feet above him. He wears the rib-cuff under his shirt; the leather has worn a raw red band around his torso, weeping at the edges, and the whalebone stays have pressed white grooves into his skin like the marks of a corset. When the body's tide comes, he stops whatever he is doing. His chest fills, visibly, the ribs rising and staying risen; his face goes dark; water comes up into his throat and he bends over his bucket and bails, retching, and above him the ratchet clicks, and clicks, and the sear trips, and Old Gannet speaks, a sound so deep it is felt in the teeth before it is heard. Across the Headland, a mile of Lowmark coughs, together, at once, a sound like surf on shingle.

When the bell rings, a deep, slow knocking answers from the bottom of the stair, three times, and then stops. Finn says it is the pipes.

[pull] Every hour I'm the city's chest. Every hour I'm drowning for all of you. It's a living. | — Finn Undertow, to the Admiralty's third arresting officer, who went home

### Rules

**Ringing the chests.** While the selector is set to CHEST, everyone in Lowmark who hears Old Gannet bails on time. Brinehollowers within earshot gain +1 on Calling rolls on any night the bell rings the chest, because they are bailed and steady; they gain the same bonus against drowning effects of their own tide. When the selector is set to SEA, Brinehollowers in earshot suffer the −2 penalty for a missed bailing for 1d6 × 10 minutes each turn of the tide, as the bell lies to them.

**Wearing the cuff.** Anyone may take Finn's place on the cord. The wearer must be a Brinehollower (anyone else's chest does not have the tide) and must stay within forty feet of the drum. Each turn of the tide, the wearer makes a Gut + Endure roll at Hard 14 or loses 2 Flesh to the cuff's bite as the chest swells against it. A wearer at the Appetite or beyond feels the coast's tide as one swell; they may roll Caul + Godsign at Grim 18 to learn, for one turn, who on the Headland is closest to being Called, as a direction and a distance. A Lack gives them the Want *The West* for a day.

**Reading the Count Wheel.** Eye + Reckoning at Hard 14 gives the date of the closing. A Helping reveals that the drift is not steady but follows a slow rhythm of its own, four breaths in a day; a Grace lets the character recognise the rhythm as a single great breath taken by something very large, somewhere below, and make a Dread 3 check.

**The cistern.** Opening the bricks is Hand + Labor at Routine 10. Seeing what is inside is Dread 3. Ysolde Prow is Tenanted, at the Seating, and has not been bailed by anyone but herself in thirty-seven years. She can speak, very slowly, through water. Whatever is wearing her is courteous, and asks after the bell.

**Sabotage** difficulty: Hard 14 for the drums, Routine 10 to cut the cord.

### Failure Table (d6)

| d6 | The bell fails |
| 1 | The cord snaps. The bell rings no tide at all until it is spliced. In the silence, the Headland's chests come on unannounced, in the street, at table, in bed; d6 deaths by drowning in the night among the old and the drunk. |
| 2 | The ratchet slips backward. The bell rings twice, a turn apart, and half the city bails for nothing and half not at all. Brawls at every pump. |
| 3 | The selector jumps to SEA in the night. Nobody touched it. The ratchet has been moved by a hand that left wet prints on the lever, small and long-fingered. |
| 4 | The cuff tightens itself. The wearer's chest goes on filling past the tide and will not stop until the cuff is cut: Gut + Endure at Grim 18 or begin drowning on dry stone (1 Flesh per round). |
| 5 | Old Gannet rings for the cistern instead of the cuff. Its note drops a full tone. Everyone in earshot at the Appetite or beyond must make a Calling roll at once, in daylight. |
| 6 | The drift closes early. For one turn, every chest on the coast fills at the same moment, and the bell rings it, and in the silence after, a mile of Lowmark hears something very large, far to the west, draw breath with them. Dread 4. |

### Cost and Availability

The Tide-Bell Mechanism is **Unique**. A small chest-engine for a household (a rib-cuff, cord and clapper bell that rings the wearer's own tide for the house) can be had from a Mile Six bell-founder for **35 lacks** (Uncommon in Brinehollow, Rare elsewhere); it is popular with Wreckwives, who strap it to their returned spouses and listen, at night, to see whose tide it rings.

### Hook

**The Third Knock.** The Admiralty wants the selector set to SEA permanently and has hired the PCs to replace Finn with a reliable man, quietly. Finn's apprentices have hired the same PCs to find out what is knocking on the cistern's bricks. Inside the cistern, Ysolde Prow has been keeping her own count, scratched on the glass with a fingernail: a count that closes on the same night as Finn's, and a list of names under it, nine hundred long, every one of them a volunteer from Dagna Hull's wall.

## The Assemblers' Joining Frame

[sigil: cradlewrack]

> Whose is the hand? Every mother's. What does the needle do? It makes the god whole.
>> — the Gatherers' Catechism

### Lore and History

The first Joinings in the Barn were done on a threshing floor, on straw, by lamplight, with the Part held in place by the hands of six kneeling Seamsters for a whole night while the seam took. Hands tire. In 566 a Seamster fell asleep at the far end of the figure, and the ear she was holding against the figure's jaw crawled away into the straw and was not found for a week; when it was, it had begun to take root in the floorboards. Dov Marrin's predecessor, a wheelwright's widow named **Hanne Spokes**, built the first **Joining Frame** that winter from a cart-bed, a loom and a midwife's birthing chair, and every Joining since has been done in it or in one of its children.

There are now four frames in the Barn and perhaps a dozen elsewhere, in the cellars of Assembler households across the highlands, where Gatherers join two or three Parts together before carrying them to the Barn, so that the god arrives in larger pieces. The Attendance has found and burned nine of these lesser frames since 620. They are always warm when found, and there is always something in them.

### The Diagram in Words

| Part | Material | Function |
| The Bed | oak cart-bed on trestles, padded with fleece | holds the figure or the receiving body |
| The Arms | eight jointed oak arms with brass clamps | hold a Part exactly against its seam for a night |
| Warming Troughs | copper, under the bed, fed from a kettle-stove | keep bed and Parts at blood-heat |
| The Listening Slab | red clay, hollowed, with a brass ear-cup | where a Part is laid to be Heard before joining |
| Hum-Pipe | a bone flute with five holes | sounds the five-note tune that calls the Parts |
| Seam-rack | curved needles, sheep-gut, silver wire | the stitching itself |
| Cord-Pegs | iron, set in the bed's rim | where a Carrier's living cord is pegged, if they are Uncut |

The frame is a bed with arms. The receiving body (Herself, or a lesser assemblage, or a living Carrier who has agreed to bear the piece) is laid on the fleece and kept at blood-heat by the troughs. The Part is first laid on the Listening Slab, a hollowed brick of warm red clay with a brass cup set into its side, and a Fitter puts her ear to the cup and listens, sometimes for days, until she knows where the Part wants to go. Then the arms are swung in and their clamps set: soft brass jaws lined with lambskin, adjusted by thumbscrews, one arm for each edge of the seam. The clamps hold the Part against the body, edge to edge, raw to raw, with exactly the pressure of a mother's hand. The Fitter sews with curved needles, sheep-gut for the skin and silver wire for anything that bears weight. Then the hum-pipe is played, the five notes, over and over, by relays of brothers and sisters, all night, while the seam takes. By morning, in Cradlewrack, the arms can be swung back. The Part is part of something.

### Who Builds It, Who Uses It

Frames are built by Assembler carpenters to Hanne Spokes's pattern, and only Fitters and Assembler-Prime may join in them. Seamsters assist. The Attendance keeps two confiscated frames in the Lying-In, and the rumour in Kest is that it uses them, for the births it permits but cannot look at: to sew back what came out wrong. Lazar Voss has drawn a frame in exact detail. The Second Table has paid for a frame to be shipped, in pieces, to a cellar on the Rim.

### In Operation

The room smells of hot copper, wet fleece, milk, and a sweetish smell beneath it like a cut pear left out. The troughs gurgle. The clamps tick as they are tightened. The Part, on the Slab, is never quite still: a hand flexes, a tongue curls, an eye rolls in its socket of clay to look at the Fitter. When it is laid against the body, the edges of both wounds are seen to be wet and raw and to tremble toward each other, like the lips of a cut that wants to close. The gut squeaks through skin. Through the long night, under the hum, the seam begins to pulse, first out of time with the body's heart, then in. By dawn the scar is pink, puckered, and warm as a cheek. The worst moment, those who have seen it say, is the first twitch: when the joined hand closes, not by its own will but by the body's, and the whole figure on the bed seems to shiver once, from end to end, the way a sleeper shivers when someone sits down on the bed.

[pull] It took at four bells. I felt it take. The whole barn felt it. Everybody looked at the door, as if someone had come in. | — Seamster's account of a Joining, Plenty 639

### Rules

**The Hearing.** Laying a Part on the Slab and finding where it belongs is Caul + Instinct at Hard 14, one roll per day; a Fitter adds her rank. Each Helping reveals one true thing about the Part's bearer or its place in the figure. A Lack means the Part has been heard wrong: it will be joined where it does not belong (roll on the failure table at the Joining's end, regardless of success).

**The Joining.** Hand + Stitching at Grim 18 (Hard 14 for a Fitter in a proper frame). The frame gives +2 to the roll; without one, six steady assistants are needed and the difficulty is Dire 22. A success means the Part takes by dawn. Inside Cradlewrack this is certain; outside it, the Part takes only on a Grace, and otherwise dies in the night, cold and grey.

**Bearing the piece.** A living body joined to a Part becomes a Carrier. The Part has Taint 2 Regrowth (Cradlewrack's god, foreign to most) and counts as worn against the skin every day. It feeds through the seam: the Carrier's maximum Flesh drops by 2 for as long as it is carried. It has a will: once a session the GM may have it reach, grip, stroke or point, and the Carrier must win an opposed Gut + Brawl roll against the Part's Threat 3 to stop it. Witnesses of a Joining make a Dread 2 check; the Carrier makes Dread 3 the first morning they see it move.

**Building a frame** is a Long Task: Hand + Craft at Hard 14, a day per roll, four successes, 90 lacks of oak, brass and copper. Its Sabotage difficulty is Hard 14.

### Failure Table (d6)

| d6 | The Joining fails |
| 1 | The seam will not take. The Part is cold by morning. It is still moving. |
| 2 | It takes, wrong side out. The hand is joined palm-outward, the eye facing in. It works. |
| 3 | The clamps were too tight. The Part takes, and the brass arm takes with it: the clamp is fused into the flesh, and must stay, or be sawn. |
| 4 | The Fitter's ear was against the cup for too long. Her own hearing is joined into the Part: from now on she hears what it hears, everywhere it goes. |
| 5 | The receiving body takes the Part and then reaches past it, in the night, and takes one of the assistants' hands as well. The assistant must be cut free (Grim 18 Hand + Stitching) or join the figure. |
| 6 | The Part's bearer, wherever they are in the highlands, feels the seam take, at dawn, as a pain in the place it came from. They will come to the Barn. They will want it back, or they will want to lie down beside it. Neither ends well. |

### Cost and Availability

A frame is **Rare**, never openly sold, and costs **300 lacks** from an Assembler carpenter who trusts the buyer, or **900** through the Second Table's agents. Assembler joining-gut (sheep-gut cured in posset) is 3 lacks a skein, Uncommon in Cradlewrack. Possession of a frame in Kest is a matter for the Second Opinion.

### Hook

**The Frame in the Cellar.** A Rim innkeeper at Knocking Gate has asked the PCs to clear a cellar he has inherited. In it is a small Joining Frame, still warm, the troughs still bubbling, the kettle-stove still lit by someone who comes in at night. On the fleece, in the clamps, are two ears and a mouth, already joined into something like a face, and the face is humming the five notes. It is waiting for its eyes. The innkeeper's own children have started humming them too.

## Hush-Wax Lanterns

[sigil: sallowreach]

> It does not light the room. It finishes a little of it.
>> — Loft-Warden's description, given in sign, because there was no sound to give it in

### Lore and History

When Dunmere went silent in 633, the Court sent surveyors to the edge, and the surveyors brought back what they could reach without crossing: a dead heron with every feather perfect, a stalk of grey grass that did not wilt, and, from an apple tree just inside the line, a wild bees' nest. The bees had fallen in rows on the comb. The honey had stopped. The wax, when it was cut, did not smell of anything at all.

A chandler named **Wick Tallow** bought the comb. He had built his fortune rendering the unclaimed dead into candles that feel themselves burn, and he wanted to know what a candle would do that could feel nothing. He rendered the Dunmere wax, poured it into a single taper, and lit it in his shop in the Lower Pilings. The flame was perfectly still. Around it, for a stride in every direction, there was no sound. The flies in the shop dropped out of the air. And the Kept candles on the next shelf, the ones made from people, stopped moaning in their wax and went out.

Wick Tallow is a businessman. Within a year he had sent gatherers to the edge of every Hush in the country for comb, for tallow from things that died at the line, for the fat of birds that fell. The product is called **Hush-wax**. Rendered into lanterns, it makes a small, moving silence: a little Hush on a pole. The Court declared it a licensed good in 636, since a thing that finishes even a little is a Closing by another name. The licence is never granted. The lanterns are everywhere.

### The Diagram in Words

| Part | Material | Function |
| Hush-wax taper | wax or tallow from inside a Hush's edge | the source; burns three hours a finger |
| Lead cowl | sheet lead, hinged | shutters the silence; open, it spills, closed, it stops |
| Wick | grey grass from a Hush, plaited | will not burn without Hush-wax around it |
| Glass chimney | thick green fen-glass | keeps the flame from the air, which it dislikes |
| Pole or bail | ash, iron | held at arm's length, always |
| Cork ear-stops | cork and wax | worn by the bearer, out of habit; they do nothing |

A Hush-wax lantern is a plain fen-glass chimney in a hinged lead cowl, on a pole or a bail. The taper inside is grey, odourless, and slightly cold to the touch. The wick is a plait of Hush grass, which will not catch from any ordinary flame unless it is wrapped in Hush-wax, and which, once lit, burns with a small, upright, motionless flame, pale as milk, that casts no flicker. Around that flame, while the cowl is open, is a sphere of hush about two strides across: no sound passes into or out of it; flies, moths and fen-midges die at its rim and fall; decay stops within it; and the Kept, inside its light, grow still. Closing the lead cowl stops it at once. Lead is the only thing that does.

### Who Builds It, Who Uses It

Wick Tallow's shop makes the best, with a small mark of a closed eye pressed into the base of every taper. Hush-gatherers, mostly Heirs and Finishers desperate for money, risk the edges to supply him; a gatherer who leans an inch too far simply stops. The Loft-Wardens buy lanterns quietly and carry them through the shelving on the worst nights, so that the Set-Aside nearest the light can have an hour without pain. Burglars and assassins use them for silence. Kept families buy them to sit by a grandmother's shelf. The Finishers burn them at their services. The Court's Closers hate them, with reason.

### In Operation

Inside the sphere, the world stops making sound. A voice is a mouth moving. A footstep is a jolt felt through the heel. The bearer hears only their own heartbeat, carried through bone, very loud. The air goes perfectly dry and smells of nothing, which is the most frightening smell in Sallowreach, where everything smells. Outside the sphere, the fen goes on, buzzing and dripping and moaning; inside it, the flies come in out of the fog and drop onto the boards with tiny dry ticks that nobody hears, and pile in a ring at the edge of the light like black sand. And the Kept in the light change. Their faces slacken. The perpetual small motion of the fen dead, the twitch of the eyelid, the working of the jaw, the restless fingers, stops. Some weep. Some smile. All of them, if the light stays long enough, begin to lie down.

[pull] My mother had been screaming for ninety years. I held the lantern to her shelf and her mouth stopped and she looked at me. That was the first time she knew me since I was born. Then I closed the cowl, because it was not licensed, and she started again. | — confession to the Loft-Wardens, unsigned

### Rules

**The silence.** While the cowl is open, no sound enters or leaves the sphere (two paces radius). Characters inside gain +4 on Stealth against anyone outside and cannot be heard, speak to one another, or cast spoken oaths; Oathen echoes cannot reach them. The bearer must roll Caul + Resolve at Routine 10 each scene or gain 1 Fray from the silence.

**The Kept.** A Kept creature in the light is at −2 to all rolls and moves at half pace. Each full hour a Kept body remains in the light, roll a d6: on a 6 it finishes, properly and finally dead. This is an unlicensed Closing and a capital offence in Sallowreach. Kept PCs in the light feel no pain and must make a Caul + Resolve roll at Hard 14 to leave it voluntarily.

**Taint.** Hush-wax carries **Taint 2 Dread**, taken each scene the lantern burns. A Hand-line character at the Appetite or beyond who holds an open lantern gains +2 to any Closing touch, but also gains 1 Hunger each scene; at the Course, a Lack on any roll while holding it begins a small Hush, a pace across, at their feet, permanent.

**Burning down.** A finger of taper burns three hours. When the last of it gutters, the silence does not vanish at once: it drains out of the sphere in a sudden rush of noise that is a Dread 1 check for all inside.

### Failure Table (d6)

| d6 | The lantern fails |
| 1 | The cowl will not close. The bearer must smother it with lead, or carry it open until it burns out. |
| 2 | The flame stands still and the silence keeps spreading: a pace more each minute, until someone snuffs it with a lead cap (Hard 14 Hand + Craft). |
| 3 | The silence comes off the lantern and stays where it was set down, the size of a hearth, after the flame is out. The Court will want to know whose it is. |
| 4 | The bearer's own sound goes out with it and does not come back for a day: no voice, no footsteps, no heartbeat anyone else can hear. Physicians pronounce them dead. |
| 5 | A Kept body finishes in the light, and it was not the one meant. |
| 6 | Something answers inside the silence: a voice heard only within it, close by the ear, clear as a bell, saying one word. Edda Thrush will want to know which word. Dread 3. |

### Cost and Availability

A Hush-wax lantern is **Rare** and illegal: **120 lacks** from Wick Tallow's back room, **250** abroad, where Vigil buyers want it for sleepless sickrooms and the Fatlands want it to quiet the Bleeding Orchards at pruning. Spare tapers are **30 lacks** a finger. Counterfeits are common: ordinary wax dyed grey, which burns, flickers, and stinks of mutton.

### Hook

**The Wardens' Night.** Old Bettany Shroud wants a lantern carried up through all nine storeys of the Lofts, one shelf at a time, so that each of her forty thousand can have a minute's peace on Tablenight. It will take all night and every taper in Lastgate. The Court has heard and sent a Closer to stop it. Halfway up, the PCs begin to notice that the shelves the lantern has passed do not start moaning again when the cowl is closed. They lie very still, and very quiet, and on the fifth storey every one of them has turned their head to watch the lantern go.

## Oathen Echo-Traps

[sigil: oathen]

> A word is a bird. It must land somewhere. We build it a nest, and close the lid.
>> — Echo-Warden teaching, in handtalk

### Lore and History

The Echo-Wardens were chartered in 422 to clean the canyons of old sworn words, and for their first century they did it with felt, wool, sand and rockfall: smothering. Smothering kills an echo but takes days, and a long echo can bind a dozen travellers before it dies. In 531 a Warden-Tracker named **Iolo Thrane**, who had been a bell-founder before the knife took his right ear, built the first **echo-trap**: a long spiral horn of fired clay, lined with wax, that a living echo would fly into and not fly out of. The word went round the spiral, smaller and smaller, until it reached a wax-lined chamber at the centre, where it pressed itself into the soft wax like a seal into a letter, and stopped.

The traps worked. They also kept. A trap pulled from the rock forty years later was found to contain its echo still, pressed into the wax, and anyone who put their ear to the horn's mouth heard it, faintly, and was bound by it. The Wardens now break every full trap on an anvil in a felt-hung room, and grind the wax with sand. Not all of them do. Kept words are worth a great deal to the wrong people.

### The Diagram in Words

| Part | Material | Function |
| The Mouth | flared clay bell, a forearm wide | faces up-canyon; takes the echo in |
| The Spiral | coiled clay tube, wax-lined, nine turns | narrows the word and keeps it turning |
| The Nest | wax chamber at the centre, fist-sized | where the word lands and presses in |
| The Lid | lead plug on a chain | seals the nest; never removed in hearing of anyone |
| Felt jacket | wool felt, stitched tight | stops the trap ringing like a bell |
| Mounting | iron pins, rope cradle | set into the rock at an echo's turning-point |

An echo-trap looks like a great clay snail-shell, wrapped in grey felt, with a flared mouth. The Wardens track a living echo by ear, find the bend where it turns from one wall to the other, and pin the trap there, mouth facing the oncoming word. An echo is a sound that wants a wall to strike. The trap gives it a softer one, then another, nine turns down a narrowing spiral of wax, each turn taking a little of its force, until it is small enough to enter the nest. There it presses into the wax and is still. A Warden listens at the felt for silence, then fits the lead lid by touch. Then the trap is carried down, unspeaking, wrapped in a quilt, to be broken.

Smugglers and Forsworn have learned to build traps backwards: with a lid that can be opened. A trap with a kept word in it, unstopped in a narrow place, sends the word out again into the rock to bind whoever walks there. These are called **loaded horns**, and owning one is a crime equal to breaking the oath inside it.

### Who Builds It, Who Uses It

Echo-Warden potters at the Wardens' house in Tacit build the true traps, and each is numbered and must be returned, full, to be broken. Smugglers in the Breakdowns build cruder ones from cracked water-jars lined with tallow. Grandam Cess, the Vow-Eater, is said to keep a shelf of loaded horns in her back canyon, each holding an oath she has lifted from a client, waiting to be sold to someone who wants that client bound again. The Bench owns a single horn from Sworn Gorge, sealed in lead, taken by Halvar Stane in 611 from the outermost bend of the gorge. It holds only one word. Nobody knows which.

### In Operation

A trap being set is the quietest work on the Table. The Wardens move by touch and gesture, felt slippers on rock, breath held. Then the echo comes down the canyon: a voice, thin and distant and urgent, bouncing from wall to wall, the same few words over and over, getting louder as it nears, *I will not leave you, I will not leave you, I will not*, a man's voice from sixty years ago, and the hair stands up on the Wardens' arms, because every time it passes them they must not hear it. Then it enters the mouth of the trap and the clay begins to hum, a low warm buzzing like a jar full of bees, and the felt jacket shivers, and the word goes round and round inside, smaller and smaller, in a voice that grows tinny and frantic, like someone shouting from the bottom of a well, until at the centre it stops with a sound like a thumb pressed into butter. The Wardens say a full trap is warm for a day afterwards. They carry it like a sleeping child.

[pull] I will not leave you. Sixty years it went up and down that gorge. We trapped it on a Tuesday. I hope whoever he was meant it. I hope she knew. | — Echo-Warden Tracker's report, Low Dray, 638

### Rules

**Trapping an echo.** Tracking a living echo to its turning-point is Eye + Search at Hard 14 (Grim 18 for an echo older than a century). Setting the trap is Hand + Craft at Hard 14. Each round the work takes in hearing of the echo, every character without ear-stops must make a Caul + Resolve roll at Hard 14 or hear the words and be bound by them, at the oath's Weight (see Oaths in Oathen). Wax and lead plugs give +4 to this roll.

**The kept word.** A full trap holds one oath. Anyone who puts their ear to an unlidded full trap is bound by it as if they had sworn it, at Weight −1 (minimum 1), and makes a Dread check of that Weight. Breaking the trap on an anvil destroys the word; grinding the wax ensures it.

**Loaded horns.** Unstopping a loaded horn in a narrow place releases its word as a living echo for 1d6 hours. Everyone who walks through must resist as above. Using one against a pursuer is a Hand + Craft roll at Hard 14 to place it where they will pass.

**Building a trap** is a Long Task: Hand + Craft at Grim 18, two days a roll, three successes, 25 lacks. Sabotage difficulty Hard 14: a hairline crack in the spiral.

### Failure Table (d6)

| d6 | The trap fails |
| 1 | A crack in the spiral. The word goes in and comes out again, faster, angrier, and now it knows what a trap sounds like. |
| 2 | The lid slips in the carrying. The word escapes at the bearer's ear, at full voice, as though they had said it. Bound at full Weight. |
| 3 | The trap catches the wrong word: one of the Wardens', spoken under the breath at the wrong moment. Now it keeps that. |
| 4 | The wax was soft from the sun. The word is pressed in, but blurred: anyone who hears it is bound to something like it, and the GM decides what. |
| 5 | The trap takes two echoes at once, and they are pressed together in the nest. The oath that comes out of that wax, if anyone opens it, is a new one that neither speaker made. |
| 6 | The word is too old and too large for the nest. The trap fills and bursts on its mounting, and the canyon rings with it from end to end. Every Warden present is bound. |

### Cost and Availability

Echo-traps are **Scarce** and Warden property; a Warden-made trap sold out of the Wardens' house costs **40 lacks** and a bribe. A smuggler's jar-trap is **8 lacks**, Uncommon in the Breakdowns, and holds a word one time in three. A loaded horn with a known oath in it is **Rare**, and its price depends on whose oath it is: from **50 lacks** for a stranger's idle promise to more than a platter a word for a notary's vow.

### Hook

**The Horn on the Market.** A loaded horn has turned up for sale in the Gristmoot black market, and the seller says it holds an oath of Jessamy Quill's, caught in the Gullet in her youth. If it is real, and is uncorked in a narrow place, the Company's notary will be bound by something she said at nineteen. The Company wants it bought. Grandam Cess wants it stolen. Jessamy Quill wants to hear it first, privately, with no one else in the room, which is the one thing no one will let her do.

## The Weighing Scales of Sated

[sigil: fatlands]

> Heavy hand, heavy vote. Light hand, light supper.
>> — Sated proverb, said of Garrick Tripe's rings

### Lore and History

The **Great Scales** were cast in 261 A.G., a year after the Weighing became law, by the bell-founders of the Chute, from the bronze of every church bell in Sated that had rung for the Gorging. They stand at the centre of the Weighhouse under the dome of glazed bone tiles: an iron beam forty feet long on a knife-edge fulcrum of black steel, with two bronze pans, each large enough to hold a loaded cart. On one pan sits the member. On the other, the city's standard weights, cast in lead and stamped with the Board's sheaf, are added by Weighmen with block and tackle until the beam hangs level. The result is cried aloud and chalked on the great slate, and becomes that member's vote for a year.

Every Weighing since 261 has been rigged. The Board knows it. The crowds know it. The proverb says that the Scales have never once told the truth and never once been wrong, because the Board has always been exactly as heavy as it needed to be. The present Weigher, **Garrick Tripe**, inherited the office from an uncle and the methods from a long line of Weighers before him, and has elected three governments.

### The Diagram in Words

| Part | Material | Function | How it is rigged |
| The Beam | iron, forty feet | balances member against weights | a hollow at the member's end, filled with shot |
| The Knife | black steel fulcrum | the pivot | shaved off-true by a hair; favours one side |
| Member's Pan | bronze, cart-sized | where the member sits | wetted with dripping; a pan "sweats" pounds |
| Weight Pan | bronze | holds the standard weights | the weights are short; see below |
| Standard Weights | lead, stamped with the sheaf | measure the member | hollow-cored "light lead" kept in a second set |
| The Lever Below | iron rod through the floor | none, officially | a boy in the cellar presses up on the member's pan |
| The Slate | slate, chalk | records the result | the chalk-boy is paid too |

The Scales are honest in their bones and crooked in every joint. Garrick Tripe's methods, as recorded in a confession he has written and hidden against the day the Chairman turns on him, are these. **The pockets**: the member's Weighing-day coat is sewn with lead, at 200 lacks a vote. **The sweat**: the member's pan is wiped down with hot dripping before the Weighing "for the member's comfort," and a thick enough coat adds the weight of a child. **The light lead**: the standard weights have a twin set, identical, stamped, cored hollow and packed with sawdust, which can be swapped in by the Weighmen at the tackle. **The knife**: the fulcrum was re-ground in 588 a hair off-true, so that the beam favours whichever end the Weigher chooses to hang the member from. **The lever**: an iron rod rises from the root-choked cellars beneath the Weighhouse floor to the underside of the member's pan, where a boy lying on his back in the dark pushes up, or pulls down, on a signal from Tripe's left boot.

### Who Builds It, Who Uses It

The Board owns the Scales and Garrick Tripe keeps them, with nine Weighmen, a chalk-boy, and the cellar boy. The cellar boy is always an orphan, because it is cold and wet down there and the roots move. The present one is **Nib**, aged twelve; his predecessor went down one Weighing in 637 and did not come back up, and the roots around the base of the lever are thicker than they were. Chairman Obb Tullow's vote, at nineteen hundred pounds, is the only one on the slate that has never been rigged. The Chairman insists on this. He has sent word to Tripe, three years running, that if a Weighing ever goes against him, Tripe will be sitting on the member's pan at the next one, and Nib will be told to pull.

### In Operation

The Weighing is three days of noise and gravy. The Weighhouse is packed to the dome. The members are brought in on drays and lifted onto the pan by tackle and twenty men, while the crowd chants the weights. The beam groans like a ship's mast. The bronze pan, wiped with dripping, glistens and smells of roast. Garrick Tripe, in his brass-buttoned coat and his twelve rings, walks the length of the beam with his hand on it, *feeling her settle*, he says, and taps his left boot, and far below in the dark a boy lying among warm roots, breathing loam, puts his hands on an iron rod and pushes until his shoulders crack. The beam dips. The crowd roars. The chalk squeaks. Somewhere, a deacon of Low Tilth has lost his vote to a woman who weighs less than her coat.

[pull] She weighed nine hundred and four. Her coat weighed three hundred. The Board weighed her twice to be sure, and both times she came out heavier. That's the Scales. They know what you're worth. | — tavern talk at the Pie Gate, Weighing Week

### Rules

**Rigging the Weighing.** Each method adds weight, and votes, at a price and a risk. Pockets add up to a fifth of the member's own weight; the sweat adds 1d6 × 10 pounds; light lead adds a tenth; the knife a twentieth; the lever, up to a hundred pounds either way. Arranging each requires Tripe or his people, a fee (see below), and for the PCs to do it themselves, Hand + Filch or Hand + Craft at Hard 14 (pockets, sweat), Grim 18 (light lead, the knife) or Dire 22 (the lever, without Nib's cooperation).

**Detecting the rig.** An observer may roll Eye + Reckoning at Grim 18 to notice that the result is wrong; a Helping identifies one method. Proving it before the Board is another matter: Tongue + Clause at Dire 22, and the Board will weigh the accuser.

**Under the floor.** The cellars are dark, warm, and full of roots that flinch when touched. A character who goes down to the lever must make a Dread 2 check and, each hour below, a Lung + Athletics roll at Hard 14 or be held by the roots (Entangling, Gut + Athletics at Grim 18 to break free). After a full Weighing down there, roll a d6: on a 1 the character has begun to root.

### Failure Table (d6)

| d6 | The rig fails |
| 1 | A pocket tears on the pan. Lead shot pours across the bronze and rolls, clattering, into the crowd. The member is stripped in public, then fed, then weighed again. |
| 2 | Light lead and true lead are mixed up at the tackle. Every member weighed that hour gains or loses a tenth. Three Board seats change hands. Nobody can say why. |
| 3 | The cellar boy pushes when he should have pulled. The beam slams, and the member on the pan is thrown. Bailiffs drag Tripe's boot-signals out of him by evening. |
| 4 | The knife-edge, re-ground once too often, cracks. The beam drops a foot and stops, perfectly level, by itself, with nobody on it. Dread 1 for the hall. |
| 5 | The roots take the lever. It pushes on its own, from now on, at every Weighing, for whoever the Dowager Hills prefer. |
| 6 | The Chairman is weighed and the beam does not move. Nineteen hundred pounds of man on one pan, and the city's full set of weights on the other, and the beam level. Then it dips toward the Chairman, slowly, as if pulled from below. He has rooted through the pan. |

### Cost and Availability

| Service | Cost | Availability | Notes |
| Lead pockets | 200 L | Rare | Tripe; a vote's worth |
| The sweat | 40 L | Scarce | paid to the pan-wipers |
| Light lead for a member | 150 L | Rare | the Weighmen want a cut |
| The lever | 500 L | Rare | Tripe's left boot; and a meal for Nib |
| A Weighing-day coat | 30 L | Uncommon | empty; legal |

### Hook

**Nib.** The cellar boy has stopped coming up between Weighings. He sends his wages up the lever on a string. Tripe wants him brought out before the Grace Weighing, because a boy who will not come up will not take signals. The PCs go down among the roots and find Nib quite happy, warm and well fed, and sitting on the knee of something in the dark that he calls the old Weigher, which has been teaching him to feel what each member weighs before they are ever lifted onto the pan.

## Lark Ninedays' Pang-Clock

[sigil: cradlewrack]

> Thirtydays. Twentydays. Fourteendays. Elevendays. Ninedays. I keep the names in a drawer, like gloves I have outgrown.
>> — Lark Ninedays, to the Tally clerks

### Lore and History

Cradlewrack has always timed its pangs, at first with sun-marks and the memory of old women, later with water. The state's first **pang-clock** was a cistern on the Tally-house roof in Kest, built in 420 when the interval was a full season, with a float that fell a finger a day and a bell struck by hand. Lark's mother, **Ide Thirtydays**, inherited the post when the interval was thirty days, and named her daughter for it; when Ide died in 609 she was mortared into the clock's base, a brick at a time, as she had asked, so that she would always be where the count was kept.

Lark rebuilt the clock in 612, around her mother's brick. Her improvement was to let the pang itself strike the bell. The earth's contraction, felt everywhere in the country, sloshes water; Lark mounted a small brass **surge-cup** on the clock's rim, balanced so finely that the slosh of a pang tips it, and the tipping trips the bell. The bell no longer needs a hand. It rings at the instant of the pang, and the float tells her how long it has been since the last. Every time the interval shortens, she grinds the old graduations off the brass ribbon and marks new ones, and changes her name.

### The Diagram in Words

| Part | Material | Function |
| The Vessel | copper, tall as a woman | holds the water that falls |
| The Float | cork in a brass cup | falls as the vessel drains, marking time |
| The Ribbon | brass strip, hand-graduated in days | the scale the float points to |
| The Surge-Cup | brass, balanced on a needle | tips at the pang's slosh and trips the bell |
| The Bell | bronze, a yard across | the loudest thing in Kest |
| The Mother's Brick | fired red clay, mortared | the base; Ide Thirtydays inside |
| The Interval Wheel | wood, notched | a notch for every pang since 612 |

Water drips at a steady rate from the vessel through a narrow brass nipple into a basin. As it drains, the float sinks, and a pointer on it slides down the graduated ribbon. When the pang comes, the surge-cup tips, the bell rings, and Lark reads the ribbon: so many days, so many hours. She notches the wheel. Then she refills the vessel to its mark, resets the cup, and waits. When the pangs begin to fall consistently short of the lowest mark on the ribbon, she grinds it smooth and re-marks it, shorter.

### Who Builds It, Who Uses It

Lark alone keeps the state clock, and sleeps beside it. Copies of her design are kept in every Attendance hall, every Foundling barracks (General Tibb times his drills to her bell), and the households of anyone who can afford one. Small household pang-clocks are sold in every market in the highlands. They are all, without exception, less accurate than Lark's, because they were not built on a mother.

### In Operation

The roof room is bare and cold, and the clock ticks with its drip, a small wet sound like a tap in an empty house. Lark keeps a hand on the vessel when she sleeps. Before a pang the water warms, and Lark wakes. It begins to smell faintly of iron. The surface trembles in fine rings, then in coarser ones; the copper creaks; the brick under it is warm as a cheek. And at the pang the whole vessel bulges, visibly, the copper swelling outward between its bands like a belly, and the water inside goes pink for a breath, and the surge-cup tips with a click, and the bell goes off like a slap across the whole of Kest. Then the copper eases, the water clears, and the drip goes on. Lark notches the wheel with a knife, and if the notch is shorter than the last, she says nothing for an hour, and then begins to grind the ribbon.

[pull] The brick gets warm before the water does. I don't write that down. I just put my hand on it, the way you'd put your hand on your mother's arm in the night to see if she's still breathing. | — Lark Ninedays

### Rules

**Reading the clock.** Anyone may read the ribbon (no roll). Predicting the next pang to the hour is Eye + Reckoning at Hard 14 with Lark's clock or a good copy, Grim 18 with a household clock, Dire 22 without one. Each Helping narrows it further; a Grace gives the minute. Knowing the pang is coming lets a character prepare: re-stitch old wounds in advance (see Doors and the Body Is a Door), brick in, or be somewhere a crowning is not.

**Lark's arithmetic.** With access to the Interval Wheel and the old ribbons, Eye + Reckoning at Grim 18 over a day gives the date on which the interval will reach one day. It is in the sealed letter Orla Kest has read once. A character who works it out makes a Dread 3 check.

**The brick.** Laying a hand on the Mother's Brick before a pang gives +2 to the roll above. Doing so during a pang is a Dread 2 check, because something inside it pushes back.

**Building a household pang-clock** is a Long Task: Hand + Craft at Hard 14, a day a roll, three successes, 40 lacks. Sabotage difficulty: Routine 10 (it is a pot of water).

### Failure Table (d6)

| d6 | The clock fails |
| 1 | The drip blocks with silt. The float stops. The clock reads the same hour for a day, and anyone relying on it is caught by the pang. |
| 2 | The surge-cup tips at a cart passing. The bell rings false. Half of Kest bricks itself in for a pang that does not come, and the real one finds them in the street. |
| 3 | The water goes pink and stays pink. |
| 4 | The bell does not ring. The surge-cup has tipped, but the clapper is held, gently, as though by a small hand. |
| 5 | The vessel bulges and does not ease. In a week it bursts, and what is found in the basin is the length of a forearm and warm. |
| 6 | Two pangs. The cup tips once for the earth and once, a little after, for something underneath it, and the gap between them is shorter than the interval. Lark has heard this twice now. She has not ground it into the ribbon. Dread 3. |

### Cost and Availability

Household pang-clocks are **Common** in Cradlewrack at **40 lacks** (see the water clock in the Rim Market chapter), **Uncommon** elsewhere. A pang-bell that rings itself a breath early is 6 lacks. Lark's clock is **Unique**, and it is not for sale, though the Second Table has asked.

### Hook

**Lark Sixdays.** The interval has shortened again and Lark has not changed her name. She has stopped grinding the ribbon. She has been seen at night with her ear to the brick, talking. The Attendance needs to know the true interval, and sends the PCs to read the clock while she sleeps. The ribbon has been marked, in a careful new hand, with a single graduation near the bottom. Beside it, scratched into the brass, is a name she has never used, and it has no number in it.

## Lung-line Breather Tanks

[sigil: brinehollow]

> The prince will receive you now. Kneel at the glass. Speak slowly. He reads lips. Do not tap.
>> — usher's instructions, Hall of Tanks, Fathomhouse

### Lore and History

By 388 the Lung-lines of the House of Vasht could no longer breathe air for long, and the first prince held court from a glass tank. But a prince must travel, inspect the fleet, appear at the Lowering, review the Netwatch, and visit his estates, and the great tanks of Fathomhouse weigh as much as a ship. The Admiralty's glass-wrights built the first **breather tank** for Prince Corin Vasht in 412: a walking tank, an upright glass coffin full of brine on a wheeled carriage, drawn by eight bearers. He rode in it to the Lowering and back, and lost two bearers on the Mile when the carriage slipped, and was very pleased.

The modern breather is smaller and grimmer. There are three kinds. The **carriage-tank** is a great glass case on wheels for the highest of the House. The **water-helm** is a sealed globe of glass and brass over the head, fed with brine from a back-pack bladder by a hand pump worked by a servant who walks behind; it lets a Lung-line at the Course walk and work in air for half a day. And the **lung-bag** is a simple oiled-leather bag of brine strapped over the mouth and nose, bought by commoners at the Course, which holds an hour.

### The Diagram in Words

| Part | Material | Function |
| Water-helm globe | thick glass, brass collar | holds brine around the head |
| Neck-seal | oiled leather, tallow, brass clamp | keeps the brine in at the throat |
| Back-bladder | tarred sealskin, two gallons | the reserve of brine |
| Feed-pump | brass hand-pump, gut hose | pushes fresh brine into the helm |
| Bleed-cock | brass tap at the helm's base | lets foul brine out, onto the road |
| Tank-tender | a person | works the pump, every minute, for life |

A Lung-line at the Course breathes water and suffocates in air; the water must be salt and cold and moving, or it fouls with the breather's own sloughed skin and the body's exhalations, which are cloudy. The water-helm keeps it moving. The tank-tender walks a pace behind, one hand on the brass pump, pressing it slowly, a stroke for every breath the wearer takes, pushing clean brine from the bladder into the helm while the bleed-cock lets the foul water dribble out at the collar and down the wearer's chest. The bladder is refilled at any salt basin or from the bailing of the household. Carriage-tanks are the same, larger, with four tenders and a cistern-cart behind.

### Who Builds It, Who Uses It

The Admiralty's glass-wrights at Fathomhouse make helms and carriage-tanks for the House of Vasht and its cadet branches. Commoners' lung-bags are sewn on Mile Six. **Tank-tenders** are a hereditary servant caste, chosen young, trained in the stroke until it is as steady as a heartbeat, and they wear rubber aprons and never go below the fourth stair. A good tender's family serves one prince for generations. A tender who falters for a minute kills their master; the punishment is to be weighted and lowered into the master's tank, to breathe what he breathes. None have managed it.

### In Operation

A Lung-line in a water-helm is a thing out of a nightmare walking in daylight: a grey face in a globe of greenish water, hair drifting like weed, eyes huge and black and unblinking, and the mouth opening and closing slowly, like a fish. Bubbles do not rise; the wearer does not breathe out air. Lips read through glass, words written on a slate. The collar leaks a steady drip of cloudy brine that smells of the bottom of a rock pool, and leaves a white crust of salt on the wearer's coat from collar to hem. The pump wheezes, stroke by stroke, behind. When the brine fouls, it fouls fast: the water clouds from the bottom up, the face inside darkens and swells, the eyes bulge against the glass, the hands go to the collar and scrabble, and the tender pumps faster, and faster, and if the bladder is dry, the wearer drowns in air in front of everyone, slowly, standing up, looking out through clouding water at the people who are failing to save them.

[pull] Thirty years I've pumped for His Grace. I hear the stroke in my sleep. If I stop in my sleep, I wake, every time, gasping, because I think it's me drowning. | — a tank-tender of the cadet House of Vasht-Ennor

### Rules

**Wearing a breather.** A character at the Course with Fathom-Body suffers −1 to all rolls for each hour spent in open air beyond the first, cumulative, until they are immersed (this replaces the core rule while the character is in Brinehollow's dry air and not wearing a breather). A water-helm with a tender removes the penalty for half a day before the bladder must be refilled. A lung-bag removes it for one hour. While helmed, the wearer cannot speak aloud, is at −2 to Search by sound, and gains +1 Armor to the head.

**Tending.** A tender must make a Gut + Endure roll at Routine 10 each hour. A failure lets the brine foul: the wearer loses 1 Flesh per round until the tender succeeds or the bladder is changed. Combat, running and fright make the roll Hard 14.

**Foreigners.** A non-Brinehollower who puts on a water-helm drowns, as anyone would. A Brinehollower at the Taste or Appetite who wears one for an hour makes a Caul + Resolve roll at Hard 14; on a failure, they find it comfortable, and gain 1 Regrowth on a Lack.

### Failure Table (d6)

| d6 | The tank fails |
| 1 | The neck-seal fails. Brine floods down the wearer's chest and the globe empties in a minute. The tender must re-seal it with tallow (Hard 14 Hand + Craft) before the wearer drowns in air. |
| 2 | The pump jams. The tender must pump by mouth through the hose. The brine tastes of the wearer. |
| 3 | The globe cracks. It holds for an hour. Then it does not. |
| 4 | The bladder was filled from a basin someone was Called from last night. The wearer is calm, and courteous, and faces west, and asks the tender to stop pumping. |
| 5 | The foul brine is not foul. It is thickening, and darkening, and moving of its own accord around the wearer's face, and the wearer is breathing it with every sign of pleasure. |
| 6 | The tender falters, from exhaustion or malice, and the wearer drowns in front of the court. Dread 2 for all present. The House will want the tender weighted. |

### Hook

**The Prince Wants a Walk.** Prince Aurel Vasht, who has not surfaced in nine days, asks the PCs secretly to take him out of Fathomhouse in a water-helm to see the sea, the real sea, at the Trench edge, once, before he can no longer bear the air at all. His tender, an old woman named Sorrel Cask whose family have pumped for Vashts for nine generations, will come, and will pump. On the road west Aurel stops writing on his slate. In the globe his lips are shaping words in a rhythm the PCs have heard once before, knocked from the bottom of the Headland tower.
