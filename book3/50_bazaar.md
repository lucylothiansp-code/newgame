# The Rim Market

> Everything on the Table has a price. The trick is finding out who is paying it.
>> — painted over the door of the Company counting-house at Toll Nine

[fiction]
The stall stood where the Rim Road bends at Gristmoot, between a man selling anchoring-rope by the fathom and a Seamer's boy crying "Thread, wire, lacquer, fingers!" in a voice that had broken years ago and been stitched back wrong. It was a Fatlands stall. You could tell by the width of the counter, which was built to bear a forearm the size of a bolster, and by the smell, which was gravy, clove, and something under the clove that the clove was there for.
The stall-wife was called Mardy Haunch and she was, by the custom of her country, beautiful: she spilled over her stool on both sides like risen dough over a tin. She was eating while she sold. She was always eating while she sold. She had a sausage in one hand and a knife in the other, and the sausage was a yard long and coiled on the board in front of her like a sleeping dog, and every so often it moved.
"It is not moving," said Mardy Haunch, to the customer. "That is settling."
The customer was from the Vigil. You could tell by the eyes, which were red to the rims and had stopped bothering to blink, and by the long pin she wore through the web of her left hand so she could twist it when the world went soft. She had a nudger with her, a small boy on a lead, whose whole job was to kick her ankle every twenty breaths. He was very good at it. He did not look up from her ankle once.
"Price," said the Vigil woman.
"For the yard? Eleven lacks."
"Eleven." The Vigil woman put three little black tabs on the counter, pressed and stamped with an open eye. "Rouse. Good rouse. Crane's strain, not the Slope stuff. Four hours of waking each."
Mardy Haunch looked at the tabs the way a cat looks at a painted bird. "What am I going to do with four hours of waking, my love? I sleep like a ditch. Coin."
"I have six lacks in coin."
"Then you have six lacks of sausage." Mardy raised the knife and the sausage, quite distinctly, tightened its coil away from it.
"Settling," said Mardy, and put the knife down.
At this point a third party arrived, which is how all real haggling on the Rim Road goes. He was an Oathener, tall and dry and dressed in canyon-red, with a little brass plate at his collar that said PRICE-WITNESS, LICENSED, and he spoke very slowly, the way a man walks across a frozen pond. "If it would be of use," he said, "and if neither party objects, and barring weather, I might, as I now intend, assist."
"Nobody asked you," said Mardy.
"That is so," said the Oathener, which cost him nothing, because it was true. He looked at the sausage. He looked at it for some time. "I would observe, without asserting anything as to its composition, that the casing bears a mark."
It did. A little way from the end there was a faded blue pattern pricked into the skin. Anchors. Two anchors, crossed, and under them, in a sailor's crude letters, the beginning of a name: SAL. The rest of the name was round the other side of the coil.
There was a silence of the kind you get at a market when everyone within six stalls has stopped pretending not to listen. The rope-seller had stopped measuring. The Seamer's boy had stopped crying fingers.
"That," said Mardy Haunch, with tremendous dignity, "is a pig that went to sea."
"I would not say otherwise," said the Oathener, very carefully, "since I cannot."
The Vigil woman leaned in, red eyes unblinking, close enough to read. The boy kicked her ankle. She did not notice. "Turn it over," she said.
"Turn it over yourself. Four lacks, then. For the yard. And the tabs."
"Three lacks and the tabs, and you turn it over."
"Five and I don't."
The Oathener coughed, gently. "If it would help to settle the matter, and as a neutral party, I might say that in my judgment, as I now hold it, the item has a value, and that value is not nothing."
Both women looked at him with loathing. He received it calmly. He was paid a crumb a word, and he had been very economical.
In the end the Vigil woman paid four lacks and two tabs, and Mardy Haunch threw in a heel of toothed bread, which bit the boy, and wrapped the coil in a sheet of oiled paper and tied it with string, and did not turn it over. The Vigil woman walked off up the Rim Road towards the lamp-glow on the eastern horizon, carrying the parcel against her chest like a baby, and three stalls down she stopped, and unwrapped one end, and looked.
Nobody saw what the rest of the name said. But they saw her stand there a long while. And they saw her, at last, wrap it up again and keep walking, because it was a long road and she was hungry and four lacks is four lacks.
"Lack keep away," said Mardy Haunch to the next customer, and smiled, and ate.
[/fiction]

On the Table everyone needs something that only an obscene neighbour can sell. The sleepless need oil, brine and pain. The undying need perfume and thread. The starving heartland needs everything, and hates whoever brings it. The Rim Road is the ring of highway that carries all of it, some two thousand four hundred miles of rutted Oathen stone around the Fast's border, owned by the Rim Road Company, tolled at every post and bridge and lined with inns, wells, shrines, gibbets and markets. Most of what the Guests buy, they buy on the Rim, from someone who is lying to them about where it came from.

The Rim is not a road with markets on it. It is a market with a road through it. Every Company post every twenty miles has a bone-slate of prices by the gate; every waystation has a market square; and at the great junctions, Gristmoot and Fogmouth and the Spur and Knocking Gate, the stalls run out along the verge for miles in both directions, so that a traveller can walk from breakfast to supper without once being out of earshot of somebody crying a price. The noise of the Rim is the noise of haggling, in seven accents, through seven kinds of mouth: Fatlanders talking with their mouths full, Brinehollowers stopping to cough brine into a bucket, Oatheners speaking in conditionals so slow you could shave between the clauses, Kept stall-keepers of the north clicking their jaws, Vigil hawkers shouting to keep themselves awake, Cradlewrack pedlars knocking on the counter before every offer, and the Unfed saying "No, thank you" three times to everything, including money.

[pull] FINGERS. MATCHED TO ORDER. YOURS OR OURS. NO QUESTIONS, FEWER ANSWERS. | — chalked on a Seamer's board at Fogmouth

This chapter is a working market. It sets out the money of the Table and what it is made of; how to find something for sale and how long that takes; how to argue the price down, with an example of play; a day at the greatest market on the Rim; what living costs in each land; and long lists of weapons, armour, clothes, tools, food, drugs, beasts, beds and services. It then turns to the two trades that are not supposed to exist and are the largest on the road: the trade in people, and the trade in the gods themselves. It closes with the merchants who deal in all of it, and the things that happen at market when nobody is looking.

[box: Using This Chapter]
Prices are given in lacks (L) and crumbs (c). Ten crumbs make a lack; twenty lacks make a platter. Prices assume a Rim Road market in a good season. Use the Availability rules to decide whether an item is on the stall at all, the Haggling rules to settle what the Guests actually pay, and the Taint rules whenever the thing they bought was once part of a god.
The Carver should not let shopping become bookkeeping. Track money closely when it is scarce and it matters (in the Fast, in a siege, in debt to an Oathener, in a Bed-Due season in the Vigil). Otherwise let the Guests live at a lifestyle and roll only for the purchases that make a story. A market scene earns its place when somebody learns something they did not want to know about what they are eating, wearing, spending or carrying.
[/box]

## The Coin of the Table

> We pay for bread with bone. Nobody thinks this is strange. Nobody has ever asked whose.
>> — Meek Garrity, from a suppressed lecture

[sigil: rim]

### The Lack

The lack is the one coin honoured everywhere on the Table: a disc of salt-cured bone the width of a thumb-joint, a finger's width thick, pierced at the centre so it can be strung, and stamped on one face with the Plate (a full moon) and on the other with an empty circle. By law and by custom it is worth one day's bread. When the price of bread rises, people do not say that bread is dear. They say the lack is thin.

Nobody minted the first lacks. Two kinds of coin are called by that name, and the trade confuses them on purpose. The Cutwrights' College records that the first bone discs were cut in the seventh year of the Long Lack, for the public kitchens of the Bone Dole, so that the starving could be given a token for a bowl instead of fighting at the door; the College declines to say what bone the Dole had to hand in the seventh year of a famine, and the few Dole lacks that survive are yellower and smoother than any coin since, and people who find one in their change spend it quickly. The older tradition of the Rim holds that the first true lacks were cut in the year after the Gorging, when the peoples of the Table had a great deal of bone lying about from the meal: from Ummer's ribs, from Dromm's vast spine, from the knuckles of Ossel. Those god-lacks, if any survive, are not money. They are relics (see Relics and Godflesh). The coin in your purse is ordinary bone. That is the official position, and it is almost true.

#### How Bone Becomes Coin

The Company's first issues were struck at the old Bonehouse beside the Chute, which is a tannery now. Since 212 A.G. the only lawful mint has been the Company's Cure-House at Saltbridge, on the Rim where the Fatlands road meets the Brinehollow road, three days' haul from the Chute. Every lack struck in the last four centuries passed through it. The process is old, simple and filthy.

- **The bone.** It comes from the Chute, the Fatlands' great export depot, in sealed carts stamped BONE: MIXED. The Renderers' Union sells the Company the long bones of everything slaughtered in the south. Everything slaughtered in the south includes the cattle, the swine, the orchard-pigs, the drover-herds of Sal Trotter, and the Fatlands dead, who are served at their own wakes and whose bones the family is paid a few crumbs to send on. The Company does not sort. Coin-cutters say you can tell a cattle lack from a person lack by the grain; the Company says that is superstition, and fines anyone who says it twice.
- **The boil.** The bones are boiled white in copper vats for three days and three nights. The fat is skimmed and sold back south as tallow. The steam from the Cure-House can be smelled from the next post, sweet and heavy, like a stockpot left on the fire for a week, and the people of Saltbridge are famous for their good skin and bad dreams. Children born in the town are said to be born with the taste of marrow in their mouths. The vat-men work naked to the waist and are scalded pink to the collarbone, and they are the only people on the Rim who can look into a copper of bubbling knees and see nothing but work.
- **The cutting.** Coin-cutters, most of them Scraplings bound to the Company for twenty years (see The Trade in People), saw the long bones into rounds on treadle saws and punch the centre hole. The saw-dust is a fine grey flour that gets into the bread, the bedding and the lungs; old cutters cough bone. A good cutter gets nineteen lacks from a man's thigh, and the trade calls a thigh "a bad day's wage." The cutters keep tally-sticks of their work and are paid in their own product, one lack in fifty.
- **The cure.** The rounds are packed in salt for a full season, and the Company's best issue, the platter-bone, for a full year. The salt is Brinehollow salt, and since the sea retreated, most Brinehollow salt is boiled out of the water Brinehollowers cough up at dawn and dusk, collected in parish buckets and sold by the barrel. Every lack on the Table is cured in somebody's lungs.
- **The stamp.** A hot iron die burns the Plate and the empty circle into the faces. The empty circle has been on the coin since 212; the Company's charter says it represents "the bread to be bought." The Chute Bonehouse stamped a closed mouth on that face instead, and the Rim still calls any coin of the old issue a **shut-mouth**; there are a great many still in purses, and a traveller from the far west who has only ever seen shut-mouths will tell you, with confidence, that the lack bears a closed mouth. Some old coin-cutters say the empty circle is a chair seen from above.

A genuine lack is dense, faintly translucent at the edge when held to a lamp, cool to the touch, and tastes strongly of salt. Merchants bite coins. On the Rim this is not a figure of speech, and every money-changer on the road has a dark crescent worn into the front teeth where ten thousand lacks have been tested.

#### Crumbs

A crumb is a tenth of a lack: a bone bead the size of a pea, pierced and cured like its parent, cut from the knuckles, ribs and offcuts that will not make a full disc. Crumbs are strung in tens on a knotted cord called a loaf. Paying a lack in crumbs is legal but rude, and a merchant may count every bead in front of you while you wait. Children are paid in crumbs, beggars ask for them, and every Oathen price-witness charges by them. A beggar's cry on the Rim is "Crumb for the plate," and the beggars of Fogmouth, who are mostly living relatives of the Kept across the Drop, rattle their loaves like rosaries.

#### Platters

A platter is a sealed Rim Road Company tablet of polished bone, the size of a palm, worth twenty lacks. Each is cut from a single shoulder blade, stamped with the Company's seven-spoked wheel, numbered, and closed with a bead of red wax impressed by the signet of Jessamy Quill, Notary of the Rim Road. The Company has sworn, in Oathen, before Quill, that it will redeem every unbroken platter for twenty lacks at any Company house on the Rim. Because the oath was sworn in Oathen, it cannot be broken without the Company's sworn officers coming apart. This is why the platter is trusted more than any coin, and why the Company guards Jessamy Quill more closely than any king guards a crown. If she dies, every platter in circulation becomes a pretty piece of shoulder.

The wealthy keep platters in flat cedar cases lined with felt, slotted like a cutlery drawer, and the very wealthy have their cases made in sets of seven, after the wheel. Platter-thieves on the Rim are called **blade-men**, after the shoulder, and are hanged at the post like murderers, because the Company regards a stolen platter as a stolen oath. A platter whose wax is cracked is redeemed at fifteen lacks and a sworn statement of how it cracked. A platter whose wax has been lifted and resealed is worth a rope.

#### Weighing and Clipping

A true lack weighs as much as forty grains of untoothed wheat. Money-changers carry hanging scales and a pouch of reference grains, and in the Fatlands, where the wheat has teeth, they carry grains from the Rim and guard them like jewels; a money-changer's reference pouch is the first thing a Gristmoot pickpocket goes for, and a changer robbed of it cannot work until a Company factor certifies a new one. Shaving a lack (clipping) is a hanging offence in every land except Sallowreach, where the offender is shaved in turn, very slowly, by the same amount, and goes on living afterward. Clipped coins are accepted at nine crumbs, or at eight if the clipping shows.

#### Counterfeits

Bone is everywhere, so forging lacks is the commonest crime on the Rim. The common fakes and their tells:
- **Horn and hoof.** Pressed and dyed. Too light; burns with a stink when touched with a hot needle. Detected on a Routine (10) Eye + Reckoning roll.
- **Unsalted bone.** Bone that skipped the season in brine. Tastes of nothing. A merchant who bites coin finds it at once.
- **Kept bone.** Cut from the Kept of Sallowreach, whose bodies never finish rotting. These coins are perfect in every respect but one: they never stop decaying. In a few weeks a Kept lack goes greasy and grey and smells faintly of the fen. In a purse it sweats. A purse of Kept coin attracts flies. Hard (14) to spot when fresh.
- **Wrack bone.** Bone from Cradlewrack Parts. A coin cut from a Part, it is whispered, is still growing; it thickens by a hair a month, and in a year will not fit a string. Hard (14) to spot.
- **Blank bone.** The rarest and most sinister fake: lacks cut from the bones of Blanks or the Unfed. They are said to be the only coins with no god in them at all. Collectors pay twenty times face value. They are almost always proof that somebody was killed for their skeleton.
- **Painted shut-mouths.** New coin with the circle sanded off and a mouth burned on, sold to collectors and to the far west as old issue at a premium. Easily spotted by anyone who has handled a real shut-mouth (Routine 10, Eye + Lore); the real ones have a little crack at the corner of the lips, where the old die was flawed.
- **Brine-soaked chalk.** Chalk cut and soaked in bail-water until it tastes right. It passes a bite and fails a rain. Shopkeepers in Oathen, where it rains once a year, are notoriously easy marks for it.

Passing false coin knowingly is punished by the Company with a fine of ten times the face value and the loss of the passing hand's thumb, and by most lands with worse. The Company's thumb-jars stand on a shelf in every factor's office, labelled by year. In Gristmoot the jar for 640 is nearly full.

[box: Whose Bones?]
The question every player eventually asks is answered differently depending on who is asked. The Company says: cattle. The Renderers' Union says: whatever comes. Mistress Brisket Annalow, if she can be made to say anything, says that since the Reaping doubled the Chute ships more bone than the herds could possibly account for, and that the Company has never once complained about the quality. A character who studies lacks for long (Grim 18 Eye + Lore) can learn to read the grain, and from then on will know which coins in their purse were people. They will still spend them. Everybody does. A character who has learned the grain makes a Dread 1 check the first time they pay for a meal with a coin they know was a person; after that, they stop counting.
[/box]

### Sworn Notes

Oathen does not mint. It does not need to. In Oathen, a written promise to pay, sworn aloud before witnesses and signed, is backed by the only security that cannot run away: the issuer's own body. These are sworn notes, and they are the paper money of the eastern half of the Table.

A sworn note reads something like the one below. The issuer reads it aloud in front of a licensed Clausewright, signs it, and the Clausewright witnesses it with a thumbprint in ink. The note then circulates exactly like coin.

[fiction]
I, Halde Vosk, cooper, of the third gallery of the Sunward Wall in Tacit, as I now intend and barring weather, flood, the closing of the stair by order of the Bench, my own death by causes not my doing, and the late coming of the Rain, will pay to the bearer of this paper twelve lacks of good Company bone, on demand, at my door, between the bell of first light and the bell of last light, on any day but Tablenight; and if I do not, let my left hand answer.
Read aloud by the issuer in the Hall of Conditions, the ninth of Plenty, 640. Witnessed. (thumbprint, brown ink) Sorrel Imm, Clausewright, licence four hundred and six.
On the back, in four different hands: Paid on to Asa the goatman for cheese. Paid on to the widow Tarrant. Paid on to Silas Amend at nine lacks. Demanded, the second of Carving, 641; not paid. And below it, in pencil, in a fifth hand, very small: heard it go from the opposite wall.
[/fiction]

The collateral clause is what gives the note its value. It names what breaks if the issuer defaults: a hand, an eye's sight, the use of a knee, the voice, a set of teeth. The Rule of Oathen does the rest. The moment the issuer refuses or fails to pay a bearer who has made lawful demand, the named part breaks, at once, with a sound, wherever the issuer happens to be. Notes backed by large collateral ("let my heart answer") are not legal in Oathen, since a break is never fatal by itself and a heart that opens like a cupboard and goes on beating is considered bad for trade.

- **Valuing a note.** A note is worth its face value only if the issuer is solvent, whole, and findable. Oathen notes trade at full value in Tacit, at a crumb in the lack discount on the Rim, and at two or three crumbs in the lack in the far west, where the issuer is weeks away. A note by an issuer known to be close to default trades at half. Speculators in Tacit watch issuers' bodies through spyglasses from the opposite canyon wall, and a limp is news.
- **Forsworn paper.** Notes issued by the Forsworn of the Breakdowns are backed by what they have left. A man whose ribs already stand open like a book has little to pledge. Brakk's people issue notes against parts that are already broken; these are worthless and are traded as jokes, souvenirs and insults. "Let my open chest answer" is a Breakdowns way of saying *never*.
- **Sureties.** A merchant who lacks good body can hire a Surety to co-sign: the Surety pledges a part of their own body as second collateral, for a fee of one lack in ten. Ona Fell's notes are the best paper in the canyon. She has two hundred and eleven other oaths in her, and her signature still trades at par. (Sureties, and the market in their bodies, are described under The Trade in People.)
- **Default abroad.** The break happens wherever the issuer is, because the oath is in the issuer. A note sworn in Tacit and defaulted in Lastgate snaps a wrist in Lastgate. Foreign issuers who swear notes in Oathen are bound like natives. A non-Oathener who signs a note outside Oathen is not bound at all, and their notes are not called sworn notes but "dry paper," worth what the issuer's honesty is worth.
- **The new terror.** Writing has begun to bind on its own. Two clerks in Tacit have broken this year over notes they copied but never swore. The Bench of Clauses has said nothing. The discount on Oathen paper has quietly doubled, and the paper-brokers of the Rim have begun to ask, before they buy a note, not only whether the issuer is whole, but who copied it.

[box: Default — What Happens]
When a character who issued a sworn note fails to pay on lawful demand, the named collateral breaks: treat it as an Injury from the Mangling table chosen to match (a hand that will not close; an eye gone milky; a knee reversed), with no roll to avoid it, and a Dread 2 check for anyone who sees it happen. The character is now Forsworn in that matter, and every Oathener who sees them knows it. Paying the debt afterwards does not mend the break. Nothing does. The sound carries: a bearer who demanded payment and was refused hears the break, wherever they are, as a small dry snap at the back of the skull, and knows the paper is spent.
[/box]

### Regional Money

Every land uses the lack, and every land also uses something else, usually something only that land could think of. A traveller who wants to be treated as a fool pays in lacks everywhere. A traveller who wants local prices learns the local money.

#### Sallowreach: Licenses, Minutes and Breaths

The only thing truly scarce in Sallowreach is ending, and so death is the money of the north. The Mortuary Court sells a **death-license**, entitling the bearer to one Closing by a Hand-line Closer, for about four thousand lacks; the price rose by three hundred last year, because the Hushes have made death seem, for the first time, a thing that might be had for free. Few families can raise that, so for three centuries the Court has also issued fractions of a license. A **minute of Closing** is a sixtieth part of a license, a narrow lacquered slip of coffin-wood bearing Regent Grue's signature and the number of the license it belongs to. A **breath** is a twelfth of a minute: a black wax token stamped with a closed eye. Families save minutes for generations, in jars under the floor, towards the day great-great-grandmother can at last be finished. Lend a man a minute and he will thank you. Give a man a full license and he will weep. Minutes are the closest thing in Sallowreach to inheritance; they pass because their owners do not.

The jars are a kind of furniture. In a fen house there is a loose board by the hearth, and under it a stoneware crock with sixty slots cut in the lid, and the crock is the family's whole ambition. When a slot is filled, the family holds a small supper. When the last slot is filled, after eighty or a hundred years of filling, the family takes the crock to the Court and the Court counts the minutes, very slowly, checking every number against the register, and very often finds one forged, and the crock goes home with fifty-nine.

[pull] We are saving for Gran. Gran is saving for her Gran. It is a long line and we are all in it. | — a porter of the Landing, Lastgate

#### The Fatlands: Meat Scrip

The Board of Plenty pays wages, rents and taxes in **meat scrip**: printed slips of greased paper, each redeemable at any Board store or at the Chute for a stated weight of meat. The common denominations are the pound, the stone and the side. Meat scrip is the most honest money on the Table in one sense (it is backed by something you can eat) and the most horrible in every other, because its value tracks the supply of meat, and the supply of meat rises every Carving with the Reaping. In a heavy Reaping year the scrip falls by a third in a month. Pell Hogget's tally-keepers chart the scrip rate alongside their count of the missing, and the two lines match. The Board posts the rate of the pound-scrip on the door of every store; in 641 it stands at seven crumbs and falling. Outside the Fatlands nobody honours the posted rate, and the pound-scrip fetches what a pound of meat fetches on the Rim, about three crumbs, and the merchant will want to know the barrel-mark it was redeemed against.

#### Brinehollow: Bail-Tokens

A **bail-token** is a small disc of cast lead, stamped with a bucket, issued by Brinehollow parishes to anyone who bails the infirm at dawn and dusk. Each token is good for one cup of sweet water at an Admiralty cistern or one bailing done on your behalf. Because both are always wanted, the tokens circulate as small change. Children like Tolly Brack earn more in tokens than their parents do in coin. A **Netwatch tally**, a larger lead token stamped with a hook, is paid to the patrol for every Called person dragged home alive; Netwatch tallies are worth ten bail-tokens and are always suspected of being for bodies that were not, strictly, alive. The Gilt Mile's brokers deal in a third paper, the **pier-note**, a mortgage on a length of the Mile not yet built; they trade at a steep discount on the Rim, where everyone has heard what the Harbormistress has calculated.

#### The Vigil: Rouse-Tabs

The Vigil's small money is a **rouse-tab**: a pressed lozenge of black rouse stamped with an open eye, nominally good for one hour awake and worth three crumbs. Wages on the Slope are paid in tabs. Because the dose required rises every year, the tab is the only currency on the Table that suffers literal inflation of the body: a tab that held an hour in 600 A.G. now holds perhaps twenty minutes, and the Apothecary-General's office restamps the official value each season, and each season the eye on the stamp gets smaller. Tabs cut with Brinehollow brine (see Drugs) are worth more on the Slope, because they work better, and are worth nothing to anyone who knows why.

#### Cradlewrack: Naming-Rights

In Cradlewrack, no child is named until it is a year old and plainly a child. Until then, the right to name it belongs to whoever bore it, and that right can be sold. A **naming-right** is a Tally-office certificate giving the holder the sole right to name a particular birth when the year is out. Rich families buy naming-rights to fill a branch of the family tree; the poor sell them for bread. Because nobody knows what a birth will turn out to be, naming-rights are speculation in the purest form: a right to name a healthy boy is worth a fortune, a right to name a hand is worth nothing, and a right to name something the Attendance does not permit is void. Kest has a naming-exchange, under the Tally hall, where rights are bought and sold by the hour as rumours come down from the birthing-rooms, and where the brokers call their prices in a sing-song like a counting-rhyme. A right bought for two lacks on a Rudge clay-digger's unborn may sell for four hundred if the child is born whole, speaking, and plainly gifted. A right bought for forty on a noble's bastard is void the moment the knife falls.

#### Oathen: Sworn Notes

As above. Small coin in Oathen is still the lack, and the price-witness is still paid in crumbs, but anything over a platter changes hands as paper. In the Breakdowns, where the Forsworn have nothing left to pledge, the currency is tally-sticks, which are held not to bind, and whose price has trebled since handtalk began to break.

#### The Fast: Ration Chits

The Unfed do not touch bone coin. They say the lack is cut from Godeaters and cured in Godeater lungs, and they are right, and they will not have it in their houses. Inside the Fast the only money is the **ration chit**: a square of unbleached cloth stamped by the Pantry with a single empty bowl, entitling the bearer to one day's ration at the Pantry window in Wanting. Chits are issued, never earned; the Abstinent give them out by household, by name, by list. A family left off the list has nothing to trade. The chits cannot lawfully be sold, and are sold constantly. In Lack season, when Mother Stint Avery begins cutting the lists, a chit fetches three lacks from a Crumb-Runner, who will be spat at for buying it.

#### The Rim: Wage-Chits and Bone-Slates

The Company pays its Bonded Hands three lacks a day, and pays most of it in **wage-chits**: thin bone slips stamped with the wheel and a post-number, good for a bed, a meal or a drink at any Company house, and worth a lack apiece to anyone who lives on the road. Off the road they are worth nothing, which is the point. The Company has never once paid a Bonded Hand in full in lacks, and Bonded Hands who retire after forty years on the Rim tend to find that they cannot afford to leave it.

### Barter

Most of the Table does not live on coin. Scrapling villages run on barter, and on the Rim a great deal of trade is goods against goods, with lacks used only to settle the difference.

- **Fair barter.** Goods trade at their listed price if both parties need them. Goods the buyer does not need are taken at half their value. Goods the buyer badly needs (oil in the Vigil, perfume in Lastgate, sweet water in Brinehollow, anything edible in the Fast) are taken at one and a half times or double.
- **Regional goods travel well.** A good carried from its home land to a distant one is worth half again its home price on arrival, before any haggling. This is the whole business of the Rim Road.
- **Bartering with Partaking.** A character may not Partake to haggle with goods unless the god they carry is one the other party fears; the Tooth is felt, not seen, and merchants on the Rim know the look of a customer who is getting help.
- **Barter in the Fast.** The Unfed accept no gift and give none. A barter in the Fast must be declined three times by both parties before it is agreed on the fourth asking. Skipping the ritual voids the trade, and the offended party will not deal again.
- **Barter with the dead.** The Kept of Sallowreach own nearly everything and need almost nothing. They will barter only for what keeps them: thread, lacquer, cedar, vinegar, a reader for an evening, and news. A Kept landlord in Lastgate will take a season's rent in good gossip, provided it is new, and has been known to evict a tenant whose stories repeated.
- **Barter in Oathen.** An exchange of goods is an exchange of promises, and every promise binds. Oatheners barter by setting the goods side by side on a felt cloth, in silence, and adding to or taking from the piles until both parties stop moving their hands. Nobody says "done." A foreigner who says "done" in Tacit has sworn to it, and if a crate is short, something breaks.

## Finding Things

> Anything can be bought on the Rim Road. Not everything can be found.

### Availability

Every item in this chapter has an Availability rating. To find an item that is not simply sitting in front of the Guests, one character makes a Grace Roll: **Eye + Haggle** against the difficulty below, modified by where they are looking. Other characters may help in the usual way. A character may Partake on the roll; the Tooth makes merchants talkative, or makes the searcher notice the stall behind the stall.

| Rating | Difficulty | Search time | Typical goods |
| Common | Routine 10 | an hour | bread, knives, rope, lamp oil, perfume, rouse |
| Uncommon | Hard 14 | half a day | swords, crossbows, mail, Kept-candles, laudanum |
| Scarce | Grim 18 | a day | plate, Brinehollow tonic in the Fatlands, a Tucker |
| Rare | Dire 22 | a week | godflesh, a Sayer's word, Blank parts |
| Unique | special | an adventure | named relics; never found by rolling alone |

- **Success** means the item is for sale, at listed price, in this market. Each **Helping** may be spent to find a better example (one extra quality, or no hidden flaw), to find it cheaper (one step of price in the buyer's favour, see Haggling), or to learn something about the seller.
- **Grace** means the seller is eager, or there is a second one, or the merchant offers something better that they "weren't going to show anyone."
- **Failure** means it is not here. The character may try again after the search time has passed again, at +2 difficulty for every retry in the same market.
- **Lack** means the item is found, and it is wrong: stolen and recognisable, Tainted and undeclared, a fake, or sold by an informer. The Carver chooses, and does not say which until it matters. If it is a fake, roll on Counterfeit Goods, below.
- **Unique** items are never found with a roll. They are the end of a trail, the price of a favour, or the reward of an adventure.

### Black Markets

Every city on the Table has a market that is not on the Company's ledgers. In Lastgate it is the undercity under the lowest pilings, the Sinks and Gutter Lane, where Marrow Jack's boys sell limbs off drying racks and the stalls are lit with fen-glow so that nobody has to see the faces of the goods. In Sated it is behind the rendering yards, where the gutters run warm and the trade is conducted by people standing ankle-deep in it. In Lowmark it is out on the Uncovered at low fog, and under the Mile itself, in the Underpier, where the pilings drip and the buyers come down ladders. In the Vigil it is the Slope, which is a black market with houses on it, and the Pinchmarket, where pain is sold by the pin. In Kest there are no locks, so the black market is simply wherever you are when somebody shuts the door, and the Odd Fold, in the western ravines, where Parts change hands for the price of a winter. In Tacit it is conducted entirely in Handtalk, which is held not to bind, and which lately does; the Breakdowns, below, are louder and cheaper, and lie for sport. In the Fast there is only one black market, and it is the Crumb-Runners' handcarts, and everyone uses it and nobody admits it. On the Rim itself, the black market is behind the stockyards at Gristmoot, after Last Bell, and its stock is mostly people (see The Trade in People).

Finding a black market needs a contact or a Hard (14) Eye + Search roll. Contraband bought there costs one and a half times the listed price, or double if the goods are capital crimes in that land. Selling there pays half the listed price, since the fence carries the risk. A Lack on any black-market roll means the Wakers, the Netwatch, the Bailiff's men or the Echo-Wardens were already watching.

## Haggling

> The price is what you pay. The cost is what it does to you afterward.
>> — Renderers' Union proverb

Listed prices are opening prices. When a character wants to argue, the buyer rolls **Tongue + Haggle** opposed by the seller's Tongue + Haggle (or Threat, for minor merchants). The higher total wins. Ties favour the seller; it is their stall.

Price moves in steps of one-tenth of the listed price. The winner shifts the price one step in their favour, plus one step for each Helping (count Helpings from the loser's total, not from a fixed Difficulty). Price cannot fall below half or rise above double the listed price by haggling alone. A Grace for the buyer adds a small thing thrown in (a heel of bread, a name, a warning); a Lack for the buyer means they have offended the seller, who will not deal again today and will tell the next stall.

- **Regional tempers.** Sallowreach merchants have centuries of patience and are at +2 to resist haste. Fatlanders haggle while eating and are at −2 if the buyer refuses to eat with them, +2 if the buyer finishes the plate. Brinehollowers stop mid-haggle to bail and are not to be hurried. Vigil sellers are short-tempered and hallucinating; a buyer who pinches them first is at +1. Cradlewrack sellers knock on the counter before every offer.
- **Haggling in Oathen.** Every offer in Oathen is a promise. A buyer who says "I'll pay six" has sworn it. Oatheners haggle in conditionals ("If six were mentioned, it would not be refused") and a foreigner who does not is at −2 and is also likely to break something. A buyer who hires a licensed price-witness (1 crumb a word, a good one uses twenty) haggles at +2 and swears nothing. The Rule of Oathen holds only inside Oathen's borders (and on Oatheners abroad who have reached the Course), but Oatheners on the Rim haggle in conditionals anyway, from habit and from fear, and the price-witness's bonus applies wherever one can be hired.
- **Haggling in the Fast.** No price may be accepted until it has been declined three times. A character who forgets is at −4 on any further dealing that day.

[box: Helpings and Price at a Glance]
Opposed Haggle: the winner moves the price one tenth in their favour, and one more tenth per Helping of margin. Floor half, ceiling double. A buyer who Partakes and rolls a 1 on the Tooth has let the god show at the counter: the seller sees it, and the price goes up a step for fear, or down two for awe, as fits the god.
[/box]

### Example of Play: A Crossbow at Gristmoot

What follows is a short shopping scene from a game in progress, showing Availability, opposed Haggling, Partaking at the counter and the Fast's decline. The Carver is running the market at Gristmoot on a Moot-day (see Market Day at Gristmoot, below). There are three players. Jo plays **Bryony Kettle**, a Brinehollow Factor (Eye 3, Tongue 3, Haggle 3, Cut 2, Hunger 2). Sam plays **Refrain Dunn**, an Unfed Crumb-Runner (Cut 0, Tongue 2, Haggle 2). Priya plays **Peregrine Asch**, an Oathen Clausewright (Tongue 4, Haggle 2, Clause 3).

**Carver:** Gristmoot on the full Plate. Three miles of stalls, the smell of gravy and woodsmoke and horse, and somewhere ahead of you the Weighhouse bell has just rung. You've each been given a Stranger's Loaf at the inn door, hot, and you can feel the whole street watching to see if you eat it.

**Jo:** Bryony eats hers. She's practical.

**Carver:** Fatlands bread is toothed wheat, milled or not. Taint 1 Hunger.

**Jo:** Hunger 3, then. I want a crossbow before we go over the Hem tomorrow.

**Carver:** A crossbow is Uncommon, so Hard, 14. Gristmoot's a great Rim market, so minus 2: you need 12. It's not anybody's home land in particular. Eye plus Haggle.

**Jo:** Eye 3, Haggle 3. *(rolls)* Eight and three. Eleven plus six is seventeen.

**Carver:** That beats 12 by five: one Helping. You find a crossbow at the listed 50 lacks. What do you want the Helping for?

**Jo:** To know something about the seller.

**Carver:** He's Abner Wedge, an arms-man on the Foreign Row, broad as a door. A boy at the next stall tells you, for a crumb, that a quiet woman in grey bought six bolts off Abner at dawn and paid in coin she held through her sleeve. Abner has been in a very good mood ever since.

**Priya:** A Reckoner. Lovely.

**Jo:** Fifty is too much. I'll haggle.

**Carver:** Abner's eating, of course. He pushes a plate of brawn in jelly across the counter at you, and the jelly is still faintly moving with somebody's pulse. Fatlanders haggle at minus 2 against a buyer who refuses to eat, plus 2 if the buyer finishes the plate.

**Jo:** Bryony finishes the plate. *(groans from the table)* It's work.

**Carver:** Fatlands meat is Taint 1 Hunger. Mark it.

**Jo:** Hunger 4. And I'll Partake on the haggle as well. I want this crossbow.

**Carver:** Plus 1 Hunger for Partaking, and roll the Tooth with your 2d10.

**Jo:** Hunger 5. Tongue 3, Haggle 3, plus 2 for the plate. *(rolls)* Six and six! And the Tooth is a 1.

**Carver:** Doubles on a success, if it is a success, would be a Grace. The Tooth showing 1 is another point of Hunger: the god bit back. What's your total?

**Jo:** Twelve plus eight is twenty, plus the 1 is twenty-one. Hunger 6.

**Carver:** Abner has Threat 4. *(rolls)* Five and four, nine, thirteen. You win by eight. Count Helpings from his total: two full fours, two Helpings. So the price moves one step, plus two, three steps of five lacks: thirty-five. But the 1 on the Tooth means the god showed at the counter. Describe it.

**Jo:** Brine. She coughs, and it isn't the dawn bail, and it comes up cold and green and runs down her chin, and it smells like a long way down.

**Carver:** Abner stops chewing. Fatlanders don't fear much, but they fear the sea, which is the one thing on the Table that cannot be eaten. Fear, not awe: one step back up. Forty lacks. And your doubles were a success, so a Grace: something thrown in. Abner leans close, mouth full, and says, very low, that the woman in grey asked him which inn the Gut-line wool-buyers sleep at, and he told her, and he'd like someone to know he's sorry. He gives you two bolts for nothing.

**Jo:** Forty, then. Done.

**Sam:** While she's doing that, Refrain has to buy grain. A week's worth. We're going into the Fast and he won't eat what he didn't carry.

**Carver:** Plenty of grain at Gristmoot; it's Common, you find it at once. The grocer is a cheerful Fatlands girl called Dimity who has never met one of the Unfed. She says "Lovely. Two lacks the sack, and have a sweet bun while you wait."

**Sam:** "No, thank you."

**Carver:** "Oh, go on."

**Sam:** "No, thank you."

**Carver:** "It's free, my love. Look at you. You're all bones."

**Sam:** "No, thank you." And then he waits for the fourth offer, because that's manners.

**Carver:** Dimity doesn't make a fourth offer. She's a Fatlander: three refusals of food is the worst insult she's ever received. She looks as if you've slapped her. Roll your haggle, and you're at minus 2 for refusing to eat with her.

**Sam:** Tongue 2, Haggle 2, minus 2. *(rolls)* One and one.

**Table:** Oh no.

**Carver:** Licked Clean. A Lack. Dimity will not deal with you again today, and she tells the next stall, and the next. Gristmoot's a long market and word travels faster than you walk. By noon every grain-seller on the Bakers' Mile knows there is a thin man who refuses bread three times, and some of them have worked out what that means.

**Priya:** Peregrine steps in. I hire a price-witness to buy the grain for us. A crumb a word.

**Carver:** That gets you plus 2 and you're not in Oathen, so nothing you say binds, but Peregrine speaks in conditionals anyway, out of habit. Who are you buying from? Not Dimity.

**Priya:** Two stalls down, before the word reaches them.

**Carver:** Roll it. The new grocer is Threat 3.

## Market Day at Gristmoot

> Come for a week. Everyone does.
>> — the Gristmoot welcome, said at every inn door with the Stranger's Loaf

[sigil: rim]

Gristmoot is the great market town on the Rim Road at the Fatlands' eastern gate, where the road comes down out of the dry red country of Oathen and meets the first green fields. It is a Company town in all but name: inns, warehouses, stockyards, toll-houses, the Weighhouse, and the longest market on the Rim, three miles of stalls along the road where Fatlands meat and bread are sold to every land and the money of every land comes in. It is loud, crowded, mercenary and cheerful. It smells of woodsmoke, sausage, horse and hot fat, and from the east, dust. It is the place where most travellers first taste Fatlands bread, and the place where a great many first stop being able to leave.

The market is open every day but Tablenight. Twice a month, on the full Plate and on the licked-clean Plate, it is a **Moot-day**, and the stalls double: the drovers bring the herds in from the south, the Oathen paper-brokers come down from Mumchance with their spyglasses, the Vigil glass-sellers arrive red-eyed off the plateau road, and the population of the town triples between dawn and noon.

### The Rows

- **The Weighhouse and the Gate.** At the eastern gate stands the Company's toll-house and, beside it, a public scale on a gibbet-frame, where **Toll-Reeve Bastable Crumb** weighs every foreigner who enters and every foreigner who leaves, and chalks the difference on a board outside. It is always a gain.
- **Gravy Row.** The first half-mile: cookshops, sausage-stalls, roast-pits, brawn-sellers and Mardy Haunch's stall, the widest on the Rim. Smoke hangs at head height. The cobbles are slick with drip-fat and the dogs are enormous.
- **The Bakers' Mile.** Toothed bread, clatter-cake for Vigil buyers, kelp-bread for the west, Unfed ration-loaves baked of Rim flour for the Crumb-Runners, who buy at the far end and are not served at the near one.
- **Foreign Row.** Every land's stalls in a long jostling line: Seamers' booths with fingers on cork boards, Brinehollow rope and buckets, Vigil lenses and pins, Cradlewrack wool and knocker's rings, Oathen felt and bits. Arms-men like Abner Wedge keep the middle of it.
- **The Paper Bench.** A long table under an awning where the Oathen brokers sit in silence, buying and selling sworn notes by slate and nod.
- **Cure-Sellers' Lane.** A crooked side street of booths selling "safe" food, cures, charms and relics to frightened foreigners. Almost everything here is false. The rest is worse.
- **The Stockyards.** A mile of pens at the western end, where Sal Trotter's herds and every other drover's come in. The beasts are walked through a stone race and stamped. Some of the herds sing.
- **Behind the Stockyards.** After Last Bell, a second market opens in the yards. Its stock stands in the pens. See The Trade in People.

### The Bells of the Moot

The Company rings the Moot by bells from the Weighhouse tower. **First Bell**, at dawn, opens the gate and the scale. **Grace Bell**, at noon, stops every stall for the space of the grace: three miles of people turn toward the nearest stranger and say "Lack keep away," and then everyone eats, because this is the Fatlands, and the Feasting Laws apply even to foreigners. **Weighing Bell** in mid-afternoon is when the drovers' herds are weighed at the race. **Last Bell**, at dusk, closes the lawful market. Any stall still trading after Last Bell is either a cookshop or behind the stockyards.

## Taint

> Never eat the god twice. Once is history. Twice is a habit.
>> — Purgation catechism

Some goods are made from the gods. Bread from toothed wheat is grown in Ummer's flesh. Bottled brine is the Fathom coughed up by its eaters. A Kept-candle is Ossel's long refusal to finish, rendered into tallow. These goods carry **Taint**: the residue of the god, which goes on working in whoever uses them.

Taint is written as a rating from 1 to 5 and a kind: **Hunger**, **Regrowth**, or **Dread**. An item may carry more than one kind (for example, "Taint 2 Hunger, 1 Dread"). Taint takes hold **on use**: once each time the item is eaten or drunk; once per scene it is wielded in anger, or burned, or opened; once per day it is worn against the skin; and once each time a relic's power is called on.

- **Hunger Taint.** The user gains Hunger equal to the rating, exactly as though they had Partaked. No roll. If this takes Hunger to 10, Regrowth rises as normal.
- **Regrowth Taint.** The user makes a Caul + Resolve roll against the matching difficulty: Taint 1 Routine 10, 2 Hard 14, 3 Grim 18, 4 Dire 22, 5 Impossible 26. On success, they gain 1 Hunger. On failure, they gain 1 Regrowth directly. On a Lack, they also gain the item's land's Want for a day, even if it is not their land.
- **Dread Taint.** The user makes a Dread check at the rating's Dread level, because the thing is horrible to use (a candle that whimpers; a loaf that bites back). Repeated use of the same item lowers Dread Taint by 1 after the first five uses, to a minimum of 1. One gets used to things.

**Foreign gods.** Godflesh from a land not your own carries a god you did not eat. Taint from a foreign god is not reduced by any Gift of your own Regrowth, and on a failed Regrowth Taint roll the gained point of Regrowth is marked as foreign: the Carver may spend a foreign point at any time to give the character that land's Taste-stage tell for a scene. Characters who mix gods in their blood are the reason the trade in cures never ends and never works.

**The Unfed and the Blank.** A character with Cut 0 ignores Hunger Taint; there is nothing in them to be hungry. Regrowth Taint is worse. Each failed Regrowth Taint roll gives a Cut 0 character one mark of **Seasoning**; at three marks, the meat has found what it was looking for, and the character gains Cut 1 in that god's line, permanently. Tobias Wether and the Reckoner called Scour each have three marks.

**Declaring Taint.** In the Fatlands the law requires godflesh to be declared and stamped. Nowhere else does. A seller who knows the Taint will rarely say so; a Hard (14) Eye + Lore roll identifies the kind and rating of an item's Taint, and a Grim (18) roll identifies which god. Rim folk have cruder tests, all of which work about as often as chance: a Tainted loaf is said to be warmer on the side facing the eater, a Tainted coin to turn in the palm toward the land it came from, Tainted meat to cook a little faster on the side away from the fire.

**Purging.** A Purgation emetic (see Drugs) taken within an hour of eating Tainted food removes the Hunger it caused. Nothing removes Regrowth that Taint has given.

## Cost of Living

> The rich smell of cedar. The poor smell of themselves.
>> — Lastgate saying

A character's lifestyle covers food, lodging, clothes and small expenses. Pay it by the day, the week (seven days) or the season (ninety days, at a tenth off).

| Destitute | 1 L | 80 L | a ditch, a crust, the clothes you stand in | no Fray recovery; +1 Hunger each week |

Characters who live below modest for a season lose 1 Flesh maximum until they recover a full season at modest or better. Characters who live lavishly in any land are noticed: by the Cutwrights, who will want to know their Cut, and by the Reckoners, who already do.

What "modest" means changes at every border. A modest life in Lastgate is a rented corner of a house owned by somebody's great-great-grandfather, who sits in the best chair and must be asked before the shutters are opened. In Sated it is five meals a day and a tent-coat let out every season. In Lowmark it is a room on the Middle Mile with a ring in the floor and ten cups of sweet water a day for a household that needs twenty. In the Vigil it is a standing strap in a waking-house and two-fifths of your wage gone on rouse. In Kest it is a bricked room at night and a neck-kit of needle and gut for the morning. In Tacit it is a cistern-share and the silence of the galleries. In Wanting it is a name on the list, which is everything, until it is not.

## Arms and Armour

> There is no gunpowder on the Table. There are a great many hooks.
>> — Netwatch drill-sergeant's opening remark

Damage is listed in brackets. Attack with melee weapons is Hand + Blades (or Brawl for fists, clubs and grapples). Ranged weapons use Hand + Shooting. Weapons marked with a land are Common there and one step rarer everywhere else.

Every land's weapons are tools first. The Table has fought few wars since the Gorging, because the lands that could fight cannot cross borders and the lands that could cross borders have nothing worth taking; its killing is done with the instruments of its trades. A Seamer's awl goes through a skull as neatly as through a Kept cheek. A boathook brings a Called man home or pulls a living one off a pier. A Waker's needle keeps you awake, and keeps you awake, and keeps you awake. Arms-men on the Rim sell by land and by trade, and a buyer who asks for "a weapon" is shown a cleaver.

[pull] NEEDLES. HOLLOW. DIPPED. ONE LACK OFF IF YOU SAY WHO IT'S FOR. | — slate in the Pinchmarket, the Vigil

### Weapon and Armour Qualities

- **Brutal.** Each Helping on a hit adds +3 damage instead of +2.
- **Concealable.** Finding it on a body takes a Hard (14) Eye + Search roll.
- **Entangling.** On a hit with at least one Helping, the target is held instead of taking the Helping's damage: they cannot move away and are at −2 Guard until they spend an action and succeed at a Hard (14) Gut + Athletics roll.
- **Fragile.** On a Lack, the weapon breaks.
- **Heavy.** Needs Gut 3 or more to use without penalty; otherwise −2 Attack. The wielder is at −1 Guard.
- **Hooking.** On a hit with at least one Helping, the attacker may give up the Helping's damage to drag the target one pace, pull them off a ledge or mount, tear away a held item, or knock them prone (the target may resist with a Hard 14 Lung + Athletics roll).
- **Light.** Can be used in the off hand; +1 Attack against a held or prone target.
- **Noisy.** Everyone within a long stone's throw hears it used. In the Vigil, this is considered good manners. In Oathen, where an echo can bind, it is a provocation.
- **Parrying.** +1 Guard against melee attacks while held ready.
- **Piercing.** Ignores 1 point of Armor.
- **Reach.** Strikes from two paces away. A foe closing to arm's length is attacked first.
- **Ripping.** If the hit deals 3 or more damage after Armor, the wound bleeds: the target loses 1 Flesh at the end of each round until someone succeeds at a Routine (10) Hand + Stitching roll. In Sallowreach a Ripping wound never closes without stitching, ever. In Cradlewrack it closes and reopens at the next pang.
- **Silent.** Makes no sound in use; attacks from hiding gain +2.
- **Slow.** Reloading takes a full action.
- **Stunning.** On a hit with a Helping, the target loses their next action unless they succeed at a Hard (14) Gut + Endure roll.
- **Thrown.** Can be thrown up to ten paces with Hand + Shooting.
- **Two-Handed.** Needs both hands.
- **Waking.** A Vigil quality. A creature struck cannot blink, sleep or fall unconscious for the rest of the scene, even at 0 Flesh, and feels everything.
- **Tainted.** The item is made from godflesh and carries Taint as listed (see Taint).

### Addiction

Each time a character uses an addictive drug, they roll Gut + Endure against its Addiction difficulty. On failure, they gain one point of **Need** for that drug. At 1 Need they want it; at 3 Need they are addicted. An addicted character who goes a day without a dose is at −1 to all rolls, and −1 more for each day after, to −4; each day without also adds 1 Fray. Need falls by 1 for each full week without the drug, while suffering. A Lack on the Addiction roll adds 2 Need.

Rouse is special. In the Vigil, nearly everyone is addicted, and the dose rises. A character addicted to rouse must double the dose after each season of use to get the same effect. Dr. Aldous Crane knows where the curve ends. It is eleven years off, and a character who works the arithmetic for themselves (Grim 18, Eye + Reckoning) takes a Dread 2 check.

[box: The Inn at the Crossroads]
In the last three years Company inn-keepers on the Rim have reported finding tables laid in their common rooms at dawn: a white cloth, warm bread, a chair pulled out, a place card. The Company's standing instruction is to burn the table and not read the card. Not all inn-keepers obey. A few have started renting the chair. The going rate at Saltlick Cross is a platter a night, paid in advance, and the renters are mostly Fatlands pilgrims who have been hungry every second of their lives. The inn-keepers do not say what happens to the renters. They do say that the chair is always empty in the morning, and the plate is always clean, and the bill is always settled.

## The Trade in People

> On the Table nobody owns a slave. It is not permitted. We own debts, and bonds, and bodies at interest, and the paper on which a man has sold himself in instalments. That is quite different, and it pays better.
>> — Lysander Coyle, Auditor-General of the Company, in a private letter

[sigil: rim]

Every land on the Table forbids the sale of people, and every land does it. The Rim Road Company's charter forbids it within a bowshot of the road, and the Company's own law punishes the theft of cargo by making the thief cargo. The trade survives every prohibition because it never calls itself by its name. People are sold on the Table in three ways: **whole**, as Blanks, poached Unfed and debtors bound for the dusk shift; **in pieces**, as Blank organs, Kept limbs, Cradlewrack Parts and Surety-flesh; and **in futures**, as bonds, debts, naming-rights and carried oaths, which are promises that a body will be delivered later. Most of it passes along the Rim, because the Rim is where a person from one land can be sold to a buyer from another without either land's law noticing.

[bigquote] They took my kidneys in Sated and my eyes in the Vigil and the rest of me went home to the Fast in a sack marked BONE: MIXED, and that is how I came to be in your purse. | — a Rim Road ghost story, told to children who bite coins

### Blank Flesh

Godsign does not live in Blank flesh. A kidney, liver, eye, stomach, length of gut or pint of blood taken from a Blank or one of the Unfed is, so far as any surgeon can tell, wholly clean, and sewn into a Godeater it is the one part of that body the god does not grow into, for a few months or a few years. The buyers are almost always High Cuts: Laden matriarchs who want to feel, for a season, what it is to be full; lidless Eye-lines who want eyes that close; Unvacated of Sallowreach who want to feel one heartbeat that is not Ossel's. Then the buyer's godsign grows into the new organ, as ivy grows into a wall, and in a few years they need another.

The chain runs the same way every time. A **poacher** crosses into the Fast by night with nets and sedatives, or buys from a body-man like the gravedigger of the Lean House who sells the omitted still warm. The catch is **fed on the road**, generously, because the meat must arrive in condition; Kale Dragoman spoon-feeds his captives honey and warm milk and holds their heads. They are delivered to a **holding-house**, a hired place with no west windows in whatever city the surgeon is working, where they are weighed daily and kept alive between harvests, because organs taken from the living are better. The **surgeon**, most often Master Quillon Barr, takes what is ordered and closes what is left, if there is to be a second order. The **buyer** never sees the donor, and pays through intermediaries who never give a name.

#### The Fourth Asking

The Unfed decline every offer three times and accept on the fourth, and the poachers of the border have learned to use it. In the hungry Lack of 637, a widow of Lammas Bottom whose household had been struck from the Pantry list walked to the border at night, to the place where Kale Dragoman's men camp, and waited to be asked. They asked her three times whether she would come with them, and three times she said "No, thank you," and on the fourth asking she went, on the condition, sworn on nothing because the Unfed swear on nothing, that her price be paid to her children in grain. It was. Two sacks of good Fatlands wheat were left at her door in Lammas Bottom. Her kidneys went to a Laden matriarch in Sated, whose god grew into them inside three years. Her children ate the grain. It was Godeater grain, grown in Ummer's flesh, and the Abstinent, finding the sacks, struck the children's names from the list for eating what they had not carried in themselves, as the law requires. Mercy Thrane has the whole of it in her ledger. She has underlined nothing. She says none of it needs underlining.

### Debt and the Bond

Where there is no Blank to sell, the Table sells its poor, by the length of time they will work and the parts of themselves they will pledge. Every land has its own way of doing it, and every land's way is lawful.

- **The Company Bond.** Coin-cutters, vat-men, porters and Bonded Hands swear to the Company in Oathen for twenty years, or for "as long as the wheel turns." The Bond binds the body anywhere; a bonded cutter who walks away from the Cure-House at Saltbridge breaks at the knees on the road. Theft of Company cargo is punished by being made cargo: the thief is entered on the manifest at the price of what they stole and shipped, by Company wagon, to whoever will buy the debt. The Company never says who buys. The manifests are sworn.
- **The dusk shift.** In the Fatlands a debtor who cannot pay is read out on a Cullmaster's list in Carving, and walks the far field at dusk, and the debt is struck off "in full," up to two hundred lacks an evening. Those who come back are free. Gristmoot moneylenders write loans to foreigners at terms they know will end on a list.
- **Impressment.** In Brinehollow, debtors who cannot pay the Pier Tithe are pressed into the Dry Fleet at a year for every hundred lacks, to stand watches on a ship that will not sail. Their families visit them on the props. The Gilt Mile's brokers buy their debts at a discount and sell their terms to the Raw Mile, where the pier-haulers die.
- **Dead wages and the long debt.** In Sallowreach nobody dies, so nobody's debt dies either. A Kept cooper in Lastgate has been working off a thirty-eight-year debt to a moneylender who is also Kept, and will be for the rest of time. Kept debtors who cannot pay sell themselves by the limb, at auction, and go on working with what is left; a Kept auctioneer of Lastgate has been selling himself by the limb since a debt of 300 A.G., and the bidding is brisk.
- **The Bed-Due.** In the Vigil the family of every Counted sleeper owes a lack a season for ever. Families who fall behind sell what they have, and when they have nothing they sell their children's waking hours as nudgers, by the bell, to the Slope's hiring-masters, who pay in tabs. A nudger who blinks on duty is not paid for that bell. A nudger who sleeps is delivered to the Dormitory steps, and the family is billed a new Bed-Due.
- **Naming and the Companies.** In Cradlewrack the poor sell the naming-rights of children not yet born, and the Crown sells the contracts of the born-grown Foundling soldiers to whoever will hire a regiment, including the Company, which bought Warden-Captain Fallowmere's contract out of a Foundling Company before he was two years old. Paupers who cannot pay the Attendance's birth-fee pay it in laundry at the Coppers, or a year of slot-feeding at the Debt Wall.
- **The list.** In the Fast nothing is sold, and so the trade is in the list itself. A name struck from the Pantry list is a household foreclosed. Families on the list sell their spare chits to Crumb-Runners for three lacks in the Lack season, and families off it buy them back at five, and both are spat upon.

### Sureties: Bodies at Interest

The Surety trade is the only trade in people on the Table that is licensed, honoured and taxed. A Surety swears, before another person's oath is made, "I stand for this word," and from then on, if the principal breaks, the Surety breaks in their place. Sureties are tattooed with the oaths they carry in fine brown script, so densely that the old ones look bruised from collarbone to ankle, and they are among the richest commoners in Oathen and almost all of them are ruins. A Surety in the trade for twenty years has paid on a dozen defaults and carries them all: the fingerless hand of a grain-merchant's default, the wired jaw of an envoy's lie, the hooked spine of a quartermaster's failed delivery.

On the Rim the trade has a second market. Foreign merchants crossing Oathen hire Sureties at Mumchance to stand behind the conditional phrases they will certainly get wrong. Paper-brokers buy the fees of a Surety's future bonds in advance, at a discount, and so own a share in the Surety's body. And a principal who has knowingly defaulted on a Surety is called **sold**, and no Surety in the land will stand for them again; the sold drift west along the Rim, where their word means nothing and nobody can read the bruise-script on the people they broke.

### Behind the Stockyards

After Last Bell at Gristmoot, when the lawful market has closed and the Company's toll-men have gone to supper, lamps are lit in the stockyards at the western end of the town and a second market opens in the pens. It is not on the Company's ledgers. It is a bowshot and a half from the road, which is to say just outside Company law, a distance measured every year by the auctioneer, Tobiah Nethercott, with a knotted rope. Its stock stands in the pens, washed and fed and wearing a numbered tag on a string. Its buyers stand on the rails and bid by raising a crumb-loaf. The lots are always adults, by the auctioneer's rule, which he states before every sale and which nobody has ever seen him break; it is the only rule he has, and he is very proud of it.

What is sold behind the stockyards is never a person, by name. It is a debt, a bond, a manifest, a contract of service, a term of years, a pledge on a body, or "a lot." Sometimes it is a Blank, under a sack, sold as "the meat of a fasting animal." Sometimes a buyer from Lastgate bids on an Unfed for her heart, and a buyer from the Vigil bids against her for the eyes, and Nethercott, who is a fair man, sells the lot to both. Some buyers want company, of the adult and unspoken kind, and Nethercott does not ask what kind and neither does the lot. The crowd is quiet. The bidding is quick. Afterward, the buyers say the grace with the lots they have bought, because they are about to eat together on the road, and it would be the universal insult not to.

[box: Running the Trade in People]
The trade in people is the Rim's deepest horror because it is ordinary: everyone in it is polite, paid, and following a law. Run it as tragedy, never spectacle. Stay with the buyer's ease, the lot's manners, the family at home, the arithmetic.
**Dread.** Watching a sale behind the stockyards is Dread 2. Watching a lot you know being sold, or seeing a holding-house, is Dread 3. Taking part in a sale, as buyer, seller or witness, is a Dread 3 check the first time; on a failure, the Fray gained is marked as **complicity**, and the next time the character sees the lot, or anyone like them, the Carver may call for a Dread 1 check that cannot be passed with a Grace.
**Buying a debt.** A Guest may buy anyone's debt, bond or term behind the stockyards at the debt's face value, Haggled as usual. They may then forgive it, which ends it, or call it, which hands the debtor to the dusk shift, the Dry Fleet, the Bed-Due or the Company's wagons. A forgiven debtor is free, and hated by everyone who could have bought them.
**Being sold.** A Guest who defaults on a Company debt, is made cargo, or is caught on the wrong side of a poacher's net, is a lot. Their price is listed above. Their way out is an adventure.
**Children.** Children are caught in every part of this trade, as nudgers, as Blanks, as names not yet given. Keep their suffering off the block and out of the operating room: show it through the adults who sold them, carry them, miss them and come for them.
[/box]
