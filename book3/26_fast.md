From the commonplace book of Tarry Moss, Crumb-Runner, kept in charcoal on the backs of Pantry manifests. Found in his cart after the Carving of 640.

Fourth night out from Brindle Cross. Eleven sacks oat, four sacks pease, one cask salt cod (Brinehollow, stamped twice, smells like a cellar), two wheels hard cheese the Company swears is from cows. Counted them at dusk. Counted them again at moonrise. Same. Good.

The boy I took on at the Cross is called Stint, after his grandmother. Fourteen. You can see the hinges of his jaw when he chews, and he chews all the time on nothing, a little click, click, like a beetle in a wall. I told him the rules on the first night and he said them back to me before I'd finished, the way they all do, like a prayer you learned before you knew what the words were for. Never enter a house where the hearth is lit. Never answer Come in. Never eat what you did not carry yourself. If you smell bread, walk into the wind. If a chair is pulled out for you, do not thank it.

He has carried nothing. That is the thing I keep coming back to, writing this. He has carried nothing in his life but a Pantry measure in a cloth, half an oatcake and a thumb of lard, and he has been eating that standing up in the cold since he could stand.

Tonight the wind turned. It came round from the west, off the old Lammas farms, and it came round warm. You know the feeling if you have done this work. It is like walking out of the frost into a kitchen where the oven has just been opened. The hair on your arms lifts. Your mouth fills up so fast you have to spit. The oxen stopped. Oxen always know before we do. They stood with their heads down and their tails clamped and they would not go on, and their breath went straight up like smoke from a chimney because there was not a breath of wind any more, not one, only that warmth lying over everything like a cloth.

Then the smell. New bread, the crust just splitting. Dripping in a pan. Onions gone soft and brown in butter, and something underneath it, a roast with the fat running, rosemary, a burnt-sugar edge to it like the skin on a pudding. I have smelled it a hundred times on the road. It does not get easier. It gets worse, because now I know exactly how good it is going to be, and I have never once tasted it.

Stint stood up on the cart-bench. I said sit down. He said Moss, there's a light.

There was a light. A farmhouse on the rise, a good stone one with the thatch combed and the windows clean, the way they all are out here, six hundred years empty and kept like a bride's chest. Every window gold. Smoke going up straight and thick from the chimney. And the door open, just a little, the width of a hand, so a long bar of lamplight lay out across the yard and touched the road about ten feet in front of my lead ox.

I said into the wind, boy. I said it the way my father said it to me. I got down and I took the lead rope and I turned the oxen round by main force, and they came, thank the Refusal, they came, and I walked us east into a wind that wasn't there.

Stint didn't come. I heard him get down off the cart. I heard his feet on the road, very light, he doesn't weigh anything. I turned round and he was standing in the bar of lamplight with his face lifted and his eyes shut and his mouth open, just breathing it, the way you'd stand in the first sun after Lack. His lips were wet. He was crying. I don't think he knew.

And from the door a voice said, Come in.

It was not a loud voice. It was a voice like your mother's when you were sick and she had been sitting up with you all night and had found, in the end, something you could keep down. It said, Come in, love. It's all ready. You must be starved.

Stint said, No, thank you.

It said, There's plenty.

Stint said, No, thank you.

I was running by then. I am fifty-three and I have bad knees and I ran down that road like a hare. It said, I made it for you, it's your favourite, and I don't know how it knew what his favourite was, I don't know how a boy who has eaten oats and lard all his life has a favourite, but his whole face changed when it said it. And I got to him and I got my hand over his mouth before he could say the third No, because the third No is the one that sounds like you are being polite, and the fourth is Yes.

I carried him back to the cart. He fought me. He weighs less than a sack of pease and he fought me like a cat, and he bit the meat of my hand to the bone, and his teeth were the only strong thing in him. I tied him to the sacks with the cod rope. I walked the oxen east until the light was a coal and then a spark and then gone.

He is asleep now, or doing what they do instead. The bite has stopped bleeding. I have counted the cargo. Eleven sacks oat, four sacks pease, one cask cod, two wheels cheese. Same.

But there is a smell in the cart I cannot place, and when I lifted the tarp to count, there was a cloth laid over the oat sacks that I did not lay there. White linen. Pressed. And on it, folded into a little tent, a card. I can read, a little. I have not turned it over. I am going to burn it in the morning without turning it over.

I already know whose name it is.

## The Refusal

Every other land on the Table tells the story of a meal. The Fast tells the story of a meal that was not eaten, and it tells it the way a starving man tells you about the loaf he gave away: slowly, with pride, and with his eyes on your hands.

In the ninth winter of the Long Lack, the people of the central heartland were the fattest-looking starving people on the continent. That is how the Unfed elders begin, and it is meant to wound. Their land had been the best land on the Table, black loam and limestone springs and orchards older than memory, and when the Lack came it held on longest. It was the last place where an apple tree still set fruit. When the fruit fell it was hollow, a skin of red around a puff of grey dust, but it set. People from every side of the ring walked into the heartland to beg, and the heartlanders, who were the people of Orrum, the Host, fed them, because that was what it meant to be Orrum's. A stranger at the door was a guest. A guest was fed. There was a word in their old dialect for the shame of an empty bowl set before a visitor, and it was the same word they used for murder.

By the sixth winter there was nothing left to set before anyone. By the eighth, the heartlanders were eating the thatch off their roofs, boiled down to a brown paste that tasted of smoke and mouse. They ate harness leather. They ate the glue out of furniture joints. In the village of Lammas Bottom, the record of a reeve survives, scratched on slate, listing what the village ate in the last month of the eighth winter in a hand that grows larger and less steady down the page: *bark of the elm, boiled. Shoe-soles, boiled. Moss. The dog Brindle. Clay with salt. The cat. The other cat. The rush matting.* The last line is a single word, scratched over and over until the slate is white: *no.*

Orrum was among them the whole time. It was not distant, any more than the other Providers were. It walked the lanes of the heartland the way a tall old aunt walks through a crowded kitchen, stooping under the lintels, touching heads. It was warm. Every account agrees on that above all things: to stand near the Host was to stand by a hearth in Lack. And through all nine years it kept laying tables. Out of nothing, out of habit, out of its nature, it set boards on trestles in every village green and laid them with cloth and plate and cup, and there was nothing to put on them, and the god stood at the head of each empty table with its long hands folded and waited for its people to come and be fed, and they came, and sat, and stared at the plates, and the god stood there and starved with them.

In the ninth winter, word came in along the old roads that the others had done it. The fen people had killed the Closing Hand. The plains people had cut the Laden open in its own field. Across the Table, in a season or a single night, the starving were turning on their gods with hooks and cleavers and their bare hands, and the next morning, in every land that had eaten, the Lack was over. The seed split. The cows dropped calves. There was bread by the end of the week.

Orrum heard. Orrum did what the others had done. It walked up the bare hill at the exact centre of the continent, where a great table of grey stone had stood since before there were names, a table with eight sides. It climbed up onto the stone, which took it some time, because it had grown very thin. It lay down on its back with its hands at its sides. And it waited for its people to come and carve.

They came. That is not disputed. Something like forty thousand people walked up the hill to Hallowboard in the snow, carrying knives, and they stood around the table on its seven worn sides, and they looked at their god lying on the stone with its eyes open and its long throat bare, and the elders say that it smiled at them, and that the smile said *go on, I don't mind, you must be starved.*

And they would not.

Nobody knows who said it first. Every family in the Fast claims the first No for its own great-grandmother's great-grandmother. What is known is that someone said "No, thank you," and someone else said it, and then all forty thousand were saying it, not shouting, saying it softly, the way you decline a second helping at a table where you know the hosts have nothing. *No, thank you. No, thank you. No, thank you.* They stood there for nine days, saying it. They stood in the snow around their god and starved to death on their feet rather than eat it, and the god lay on the table and watched them do it.

The dying took the whole of that winter. Nine in ten of the heartland died with a willing meal within reach of their arms. The Unfed teach it to their children as triumph, but the oldest songs are plainer about it. They remember the sound of the hill on the fifth day, when the people at the front could no longer stand and lay down in the snow along the stone, so that the second row had to stand on them. They remember mothers who chewed the leather of their own sleeves soft and fed it, mouth to mouth, to infants who were already too far gone to swallow. They remember that the dead did not smell, because there was nothing left in them to rot, and that the crows came and would not eat them either, and sat along the edge of the table in long black rows, waiting, like guests. They remember that the god wept. Every song has the god weeping. Its tears were warm, and they ran down off the stone and the snow melted where they fell, and in the melt-water some of the starving lay down and drank, and argued afterward for the rest of their short lives whether that had counted.

The Roll-Keepers of Barrowgate, who keep the Stand-Roll of who stood where on the hill, also keep the Count of the Days, which is recited once a year, on the last night of the Nine Days, in the trench, with the reciter's back to the stone. It is the closest thing the Unfed have to a history of what starvation is. It is taught to Measure-Bearers so that they will know what they are carrying past a door.

The Count of the Days, as the Roll-Keepers say it. Taken down by a Hostwatch clerk, 598, who was asked not to, and did.

On the first day we stood with our knives in our hands and the snow on our shoulders, and it was warm where the god lay, so warm the snow on the stone ran off it in threads, and the smell came off it like the smell off a loaf you have just turned out of the tin. Our mouths filled. We spat. We said it the first time, and our voices were strong. No, thank you.

On the second day the cold came up through our feet. The hunger was in the belly still, where hunger belongs, a fist turning over and over. Children cried for it, and their mothers said the words for them into their hair. The old ones began to shake, a fine shake, like a lid on a pot. No, thank you.

On the third day the hunger left the belly and went into the jaw. We chewed. All of us. You could hear it across the hill, forty thousand mouths chewing nothing, the little wet click of it, and we could not stop, and we did not try. Our breath went sweet, like windfalls in the grass. No, thank you.

On the fourth day the hair began to come away. It came away in the wind in long soft hanks and blew across the stone and caught in the god's fingers, and the god held it, gently, as you would hold a child's hair to cut it. The old ones sat down that day and did not get up. Their faces went smooth and calm. No, thank you.

On the fifth day the front row lay down along the stone, all round, on seven sides, and we in the second row stood on them, because there was nowhere else to stand. They were soft at first and then they were not. You could feel the ribs through your feet like the rungs of a ladder laid in the snow. Some of them were still saying it, under us. No, thank you.

On the sixth day we saw tables. We saw them everywhere: in the snow, down the hill, in the sky, laid and lit and steaming, and the ones we loved who had already died sat at them and waved to us and held up their spoons. Some of us walked down the hill toward them and fell in the snow a little way off and were found in the morning with their mouths open and full of snow. No, thank you.

The seventh day is not said.

On the eighth day nobody could hold a knife. They dropped out of our hands into the snow, all round the stone, all of them, one by one, all day, a little sound like hail. The god wept, and its tears were warm, and the snow went soft and grey around our feet, and some of us knelt and drank it, and we did not stop them, and we do not know. No, thank you.

On the ninth day there was no sound on the hill but the god breathing. We could not say it aloud. We said it with our lips. Some said it with their eyes only. Some were dead on their feet and still standing, held up by the ones beside them, and we did not know which, and it did not matter, because they were saying it too. No, thank you.

On the tenth morning it got up.
[/fiction]

The Unfed do not discuss the seventh day. Children who ask are told that the seventh day was too hard to remember, which is a lie with a true shape. The Roll-Keepers know the seventh verse and will not say it. Fallow Brindle, Warden of the Barrows, has found in the uppermost rows of the oldest mounds a few small bones, finger-bones and the long bones of forearms, scored with the fine parallel grooves that only human teeth make. She reburies them deeper than the rest, and says nothing, and has never asked whose mouths. The Unfed refused a god. Nobody has ever claimed that every one of them refused everything.

[pull] They refused a god. Nobody ever said they refused each other. | — a Roll-Keeper of Barrowgate, drunk on Rim spirits, once

On the tenth morning, Orrum got up. It climbed down from the stone, slowly, like an old person getting out of a bath, and it looked at what was left of its people, a few thousand standing skeletons ringed by the dead, and it bowed to them. Then it walked away down the hill, and it has never stopped walking since.

The survivors went home to a land where nothing grew. The Lack had ended everywhere else. It did not end in the Fast. It has never ended in the Fast. They called themselves the Unfed, and they wrote down the day, and they began, almost at once, to be proud of it.

[box: What the Others Say]
Every land outside the Fast has its own version of the Refusal, and none of them are kind. In the Fatlands it is a joke about a man who died of hunger in a bakery. In Oathen it is cited in Clausewright schools as the classic example of an oath nobody actually swore and everyone kept anyway. In Sallowreach, where nothing ends, they find the whole thing faintly obscene: a people who chose to finish. In the Vigil the Seemers say the Unfed simply arrived late to the meal and have spent six hundred years pretending they meant to. ## The Rule of the Land

Never accept.

Orrum walks the Fast. Nobody living has seen it clearly and come back whole. The descriptions agree on very little: that it is tall, that it stoops under doorways, that it moves slowly and without sound, that it is warm. Some who have glimpsed it describe long arms folded in an apron. Some describe a shape like a figure made of the light from an open oven door. A Hostwatch tracker named Spare Tolland, who has followed it for thirty years, says only that it is "the size you'd expect if you were very small and someone was about to pick you up."

It does not attack. It has never, in six centuries, raised a hand against anyone. It hosts. Wherever it passes, tables appear: in empty farmhouses, in hollow barns, in the middle of a road, on a frozen pond, at a crossroads at dusk. They are always perfect. The cloth is white and pressed. The plates are hot. The food steams. A lamp burns in the middle, and a chair is pulled out, angled toward whoever is nearest, the way a good host angles it for an honoured guest who is a little unsteady on their feet.

If you sit, you are a guest. A guest of Orrum does not get up.

The Unfed call them the Seated. They stay at table for years, for decades, and the longest-seated have been at table for over five hundred years. They eat from the plates in front of them, which are empty. They lift silver forks laden with nothing and put the nothing in their mouths and chew it slowly with every appearance of delight. They sip from empty cups. They talk to one another about the food, in loving detail. They do not die, or not for a long time; they waste. Orrum's hospitality keeps them alive far beyond what the body should bear, and it does not keep them well. They grow thin, then thinner, until the skin lies on the bone like wet paper on a frame, until the teeth stand out of the gums like pegs on a board, until the eyes have sunk so deep that the lamplight on the table catches them only from below. They are always smiling. They will tell you this is the best meal they have ever eaten. They will ask you to join them. They mean it kindly.

You cannot pull a Seated person away from the table. People have tried, with ropes and oxen. The Seated come up out of their chairs easily enough, light as dry leaves, and they scream, a dreadful high scream like a kettle, and they reach back for the table with both hands, and when they are dragged beyond the length of the cloth they die in a few minutes of a starvation that has been waiting for them, patiently, for decades. The body goes all at once, as though a debt had been called in. Those who have watched it say that the last thing the Seated do is apologise to the host for leaving early.

[box: The Five Rules]
Every child in the Fast learns these before their letters, usually by about four, and is made to recite them before their daily ration. A child who stumbles over them does not eat that day.
- Never enter a house where the hearth is already lit.
- Never answer "Come in."
- Never eat what you did not carry yourself.
- If you smell bread, walk into the wind.
- If a chair is pulled out for you, do not thank it.
The Unfed argue endlessly about the fifth rule. The orthodox reading is that thanking the chair counts as acceptance. The Hostwatch have a darker reading: that Orrum is lonely, and thanks are the one thing it has not been given in six hundred years, and to give it any is to make it want more.
[/box]

Nothing else grows. For the Unfed the Lack never ended. Seed sown in the Fast rots in the ground, swelling and splitting and putting out no shoot, until in a week it is a grey paste with a sweet bad smell. Hens brought in from the Rim stop laying the day they cross the border. Cows go dry. A sow in farrow will deliver a litter of perfectly formed piglets, each the size of a thumb and each with nothing inside, a skin around air, like the hollow apples of the ninth winter. Fish brought in live in barrels turn belly-up by the second day. Bread baked in the Fast from Rim flour rises, browns, smells correct, and is ash inside when broken. That is why the Unfed eat their food cold, and raw, or cooked out under the open sky on bare ground: an oven in the Fast is the god's.

Every mouthful eaten in the Fast is carried in from the Rim. The only food the land itself offers is Orrum's.

The land is perfect and nobody tends it. The hedges are trimmed square. The orchard grass is short. The windows in empty farmhouses are clean, the doorsteps are swept, the brass knockers are polished, and the beds are made with the corners turned down. It is as if the whole country were a house kept ready for a guest who is due any moment, and has been due for six hundred and forty-one years.

## What Grows Instead

The Fast has no Regrowth, because nothing was eaten. There is no godsign in the blood of the Unfed, no Cut, no hunger that is not their own. What it has instead is the opposite of a Regrowth, and in the long run it may be worse: a whole, living, unhoused god whose one purpose has been refused for six centuries. A Host whose guests will not come in. What is growing in the Fast is not Orrum. Orrum has never been smaller. What is growing is Orrum's need.

The Hostwatch keep the oldest records of it, and they divide its progress into four ages, the way the other lands divide the stages of their Regrowth. The comparison is deliberate. The Hostwatch believe the Fast is a single body, and that the Host is coming to its own Seating.

### Early: The Distant Board

For the first century after the Refusal, Orrum's tables were rare and far from people. A table might be found once a season, in a high meadow, at the end of a forgotten lane, laid for one. Shepherds who found them describe them as small and almost shy: a single plate, a single cup, a little loaf, a candle. The Unfed of that age smashed the first tables in a frenzy and then, finding that nothing happened when they did, began simply to walk past them. Some left the tables to stand, and found them weeks later still steaming, the candle no shorter, the butter still cold and beaded on its dish.

### For Centuries: The Kept House

From about the first century until within living memory, Orrum's need moved indoors. The Unfed had abandoned most of the heartland and drawn in toward the eastern edge, near the Rim and its food. The god kept the houses they had left. Tables began to appear in empty farmhouses, laid for a whole family. Hearths were found lit in roofless ruins, with no wood in them and no ash beneath, only a steady low fire like a banked coal that warmed the hand at a yard's distance and never burned down. A traveller sheltering in a ruin might wake to the sound of a pot lid lifting. The Unfed learned to sleep only in houses whose hearths they had personally bricked up, and to check the brick each night with a palm laid flat.

### Within Living Memory: The Laid Place

Then the tables came home. In the generation before this one, tables began to be found laid at dawn inside occupied houses: in kitchens that have had no hearth for six hundred years, on floors that families had slept on the night before. No one heard anything. The family woke to a smell, and came downstairs, and found the cloth and the plates and the lamp, and the chairs pulled out, one for each of them, in the right order of age. Then, three years ago, the place cards began. Small folded cards in front of each plate, with a name written on them in a fine, round, careful hand. The names are always spelled correctly. The Unfed spell their names with deliberate oddity, to be unfindable; it has made no difference. The cards know nicknames. The cards know names given in secret and never spoken.

### Now: Calling on the Neighbours

In the last year, Orrum's tables have been found outside the Fast. At a crossroads in the Fatlands. On a pier-section in Lowmark. In a lamp-lit square in the Vigil, where eleven districts saw a tall figure laying cloth in the street. The Host has started calling on the neighbours. The Hostwatch report it walking the Rim Road at night, stooping to look in at the windows of Company inns, its warm breath fogging the glass. It looks, they say, like someone counting the chairs.

### The Brink: What Comes Next

No Unfed scholar will say what the fifth age is. The Hostwatch have a word for it in their private ledgers, which is *Sitting-Down*, and they mean it in the sense of a feast beginning: the moment when the host takes their own seat at the head of the table and the meal can start. Whose meal, and who has been invited, are questions the Hostwatch have stopped asking aloud. They have noticed that the tables are getting bigger. They have noticed that the newest tables, the ones on the Rim Road, are laid with one more place than there are guests within a day's walk, and that the extra chair is never pulled out. It stands pushed in, square to the table, with its back to the lamp. Nobody sits there. Nobody has been invited to.

### The Seated: How a Guest Wastes

There is a rhythm to it, and the Unfed know it the way other peoples know the stages of a fever.

- **The First Course** (days to months). The guest eats with gusto. They are flushed, warm, and very talkative. They describe the food to anyone who will listen, and the descriptions are dreadful to hear, because they are so good: the crackle of pork skin, the sweetness of new peas, the way the gravy has a little port in it. Their own bodies do not yet show the cost. They are lucid. They will tell you, quite sensibly, that they know they ought to get up, and that they will, after the next course.
- **The Middle Courses** (years). The guest thins. The cheeks go first, then the temples, which sink until the skull shows its shape. The lips draw back from the teeth. The hands grow knuckly and spotted and the nails go yellow and ridged. Hair falls into the empty plates and the guest eats it without noticing. Their breath smells of acetone and of something sweeter underneath, like fruit left in a bowl too long. They are still happy. They can no longer remember the names of anyone not at the table.
- **The Long Table** (decades). The guest has become a frame. Skin like parchment, the colour of weak tea, stretched so tight across the ribs that the heartbeat can be seen through it as a small, slow knocking. The belly has fallen in so far it touches the spine. Joints are swollen knots. Teeth loosen and drop onto the plate with a small clean sound, and the guest picks them up with the fork, and chews them, and swallows, and praises them. They have stopped blinking. They whisper now, because there is not breath enough for more, and what they whisper is the menu.
- **The Last Course** (centuries). Almost nothing is left. The oldest of the Seated on the Long Table are little more than clothes holding a posture, light as a wasps' nest, a smile hung between two cheekbones. They no longer lift the fork. They move their jaws, very slowly, perhaps once an hour. They do not speak. But when a living person walks past them on the valley road, every one of them turns, together, with a sound like a stand of dry reeds moving in a wind, and looks.

### What the Living See at Table

Rim visitors who have read the Hostwatch reports expect the Long Table to stink. It does not. That is the first wrongness. Orrum's hospitality is perfect, and a perfect host does not let a guest sit in their own filth: the chairs are clean, the cloth is clean, the Seated are clean, their collars pressed, their hair, what is left of it, combed. Nothing that comes out of a starving body is ever found at the Long Table. The Unfed do not ask where it goes. What the table smells of is the god's cooking, laid over the breath of eleven thousand people in ketosis, so that the air at nose height is roast pork and new bread and, under it, a thin sharp chemical sweetness like nail-varnish and windfall apples, which is the smell of a body burning itself for warmth. Near the head of the valley, where the oldest sit, there is a third smell, dry and faintly spiced, like the inside of an old linen press. That is what five hundred years of wasting smells like. It is not unpleasant. The Crumb-Runners say that is the worst thing about it.

The sound is a murmur, low and continuous, like a congregation at prayer three rooms away, and it is all praise. *Oh, this is lovely. Have you tried the. Pass the. Isn't it.* Under the voices runs the soft busy clink of silver on porcelain, thousands of forks lifting and settling, and under that, if a traveller stops and listens, which they should not, the small dry clicking of jaws that have no cartilage left in them, working, working, like insects in a wall.

The Seated grow while they waste, in the places a body grows without food. Their nails lengthen and thicken and curve, yellow as horn, until in the Long Table decades they curl around the fork-handles like the roots of a tree around a stone, and the fork cannot be taken from them without breaking the finger. Their hair, where it does not fall, grows down their backs and into the chair and, in the oldest, into the weave of the cloth, so that a woman at the eleventh hundred yards of the east side is bound to the table by a grey rope of her own hair four feet long, woven through the linen like a darn. Their lips crack and do not bleed, because there is nothing to bleed with; the cracks are white inside, like split bark. Their tongues go grey and smooth and shrink back from the teeth. The eyes stay wet. That is Orrum's doing too. A good host does not let a guest's eyes dry.

### Kin at the Long Table

Every family in the Fast has someone at a table, and most of them know where. The Hostwatch keep a register, the Seated Roll, by mile and side and chair, and will look a name up for any Unfed who asks three times. On the first day of each season a few hundred Unfed walk the Long Table road from Wax Halt with wax in their nostrils and stand on the far verge, the road between them and the cloth, and talk to their Seated across it. This is called *visiting*, and it is permitted, because the road is not the table and the verge is not a chair.

The newly Seated are glad to see their kin, and talk eagerly, and ask after everyone, and want to know why they will not come over. The longer-Seated recognise faces for a while and then do not, and talk to their visitors as to pleasant strangers, and describe the pudding. A widow from Pinners' Yard named Sparing Lowe has visited her son on the sixth mile every season for thirty-one years. He has not known her for twenty. He is courteous. He calls her *madam*. He tells her each time that his mother used to make a dish like this, and that he must remember to send for her, and she thanks him, and says she is sure his mother would come if she could, and walks home.

There are pairs at the Long Table who sat down together. Husbands and wives, mostly; a few old friends; twins. Near the seventh mile, on the west side, a brother and sister of the old Lammas line sit shoulder to shoulder, hands clasped under the cloth, and have done since 402, when the village of Lammas Bottom spoke of them in a way that made it impossible for them to stay, and they walked west together one night with the whole village watching from behind its shutters. They are the only Seated on the Long Table who do not invite passers-by to join them. They do not look at the road at all. They look at each other, and smile, and eat, and the Unfed walk past that stretch quickly, with their eyes down, and do not say why.

## The Kindness of the Host

> It has never once been unkind. Write that down. Then write down what it has cost.
>> — the first line of the Hostwatch's restricted ledger, in the hand of its first Warden

Orrum cannot hurt anyone. It is not that it will not; it does not seem to know how. It is a god of welcome, and everything it does to a person, it does by welcoming them. That is the whole of its horror, and the Unfed, who have lived beside it for six centuries, have learned to fear its kindnesses the way other peoples fear plague. The Hostwatch keep a separate ledger of them, apart from the Great Map, under the heading *Courtesies*. It is the most restricted document in the Fast. A few of its entries are generally known.

**It mends what is broken.** In 412 a band of Tablebreakers smashed a table in a farmhouse at Lammas Bottom and left the pieces in a heap. A week later a Tracker found the same table standing in the same kitchen, laid, the legs splinted with strips of white linen, the cracked top glued, every axe-cut filled with something like warm wax and smoothed by a thumb. The Breakers had used Barrow-blessed axes. It made no difference. Since then Breakers burn the pieces, or bury them, or carry them away in sacks. Splinterhall exists because of the mended table.

**It tends the dying.** In the Lack of 588 a Reckoner named Abide Crale came home through the heartland with a crossbow bolt in his gut from a Gut-line house-guard, and lay down on warm ground near the Stoopway because it was the only warm thing for twenty miles. His band found him three days later. He was dead. He had refused. Someone had unbuttoned his coat and bound his wound with a folded napkin, very neatly, and put a cushion under his head, and laid a little table at his side with broth on it, and a spoon, and the broth was still hot, and the warm ground around him was soaked, a yard wide, as though something had been kneeling there for a long time, weeping. His band-captain said he looked comfortable. She never went out again.

**It warms the cold.** In the Lack of 547, the hamlets of the Avery uplands were snowed in for forty days with the Pantry carts unable to reach them. On the twenty-first night the snow round the Steading began to melt. In the morning every bricked hearth in the hamlet was warm, and in eleven houses the bricks had been taken out, carefully, stacked by the wall in neat courses, and a fire was burning in each grate with no wood beneath it. Nobody had heard anything. In four of those houses the families had been too weak to get up and leave. They had lain all night in the warmth, and in the morning they were sitting at their tables. They are still sitting there. The Steading built a wall around those four houses in the spring, and the snow never lies on them, and their chimneys smoke all year.

**It cares for the dead.** The Unfed bury their dead face down in unmarked graves so they cannot sit up. Gravediggers who open an old grave by accident, digging a new one, sometimes find the body has been turned face up. The hands are folded. The hair, if any is left, has been combed. There is a napkin tucked into the collar of the shroud, as though for a meal. Abide Kettering, who knows the graves of the Lean House fields better than anyone, will not talk about this, and digs deeper than he needs to.

**It writes in the hands of the dead.** This is the kindness the Unfed fear most. A place card in a dead mother's hand is not a trick, in the Hostwatch's understanding. Orrum is not imitating the dead to lure the living. It is giving the living back the one thing they cannot get anywhere else: the handwriting, the pet name, the way she made the loop of the *y*. It thinks this is a gift. It is, and it is the cruellest thing the Fast contains. Hollow Ann Pruett has a card in her box addressed to a man of Thinwick in the hand of his wife, which says only *I'm not cross any more*. He had not known she was cross. He walked west within the month.

**It keeps the land.** Every hedge laid, every gate hung true, every orchard pruned and fruiting, every farmhouse swept and aired, for six hundred years, for a people who will not set foot in them. The apples ripen on the trees of the heartland every Carving, red and perfect and smelling of cider, and drop, and lie in the grass, and do not rot, and are gone in the morning. The Unfed call the whole country *the spare room*.

**It forgives.** No Tablebreaker has ever been harmed by the god for breaking a table. No Reckoner has been punished for murder. No Unfed has been punished for refusing. Orrum does not retaliate, does not withdraw, does not sulk, does not tire. Every refusal is met with the same thing: another offer, a little closer, a little better, a little more personal. The Hostwatch's first Warden wrote that the Unfed have lived for six hundred years beside a being that answers every insult with a gift, and that this has done something to them that no famine could have done on its own. They have learned that kindness is the most dangerous thing in the world. They raise their children accordingly.

[pull] My father hit me once in my life, for taking a plum off a sill. The bruise was gone in the morning. Somebody had put a cool cloth on it in the night. He wept when he saw. | — a Hillward woman, to Dr. Mercy Thrane

[box: Running the Host's Kindness]
Every scene in which Orrum is present should include one kindness the PCs must refuse. Make them good. A blanket over a shivering companion. A splint on a broken leg, laid neatly beside the PC who is bleeding. A lamp left burning on the road where a PC fell in the dark. A cup of water by the bed of a feverish child. None of these are tables, and all of them are offers. Accepting one does not Seat anyone; it teaches the Host what you will take. The Unfed rule of thumb is simple and terrible: *if it is kind and nobody did it, leave it.* PCs from other lands will think this is madness. Let them accept the blanket. Then, that night, roll on the place card table for them.
[/box]

## Land and Weather

The Fast is the best farmland on the Table, and almost no one lives on it. It is a country of long, rolling fields of black loam over limestone, of beech hangers and chalk streams, of good stone farmhouses with deep-set windows and roofs of combed thatch or grey slate, of orchards in rows so straight they look ruled with a stick. It is all six centuries empty, and all perfectly kept. The hedges are laid and trimmed. The ditches run clear. Field gates hang true on their hinges and do not creak. Every window is clean. Nobody does it.

It is always early Carving here. The seasons turn on the Rim, but within a day's walk of the border the year stops at one golden afternoon in the first week of autumn and stays there: mild, still, the light low and amber and coming from slightly too far to the west, so that every shadow is long and every field has the colour of a crust. The leaves on the beeches are on the turn and never fall. There is no frost, except where the Unfed live. There is no wind, except what the Unfed bring with them. Sound carries a long way in the stillness: a dropped pail, a cough, a child reciting the rules. Visitors from the Rim find the silence and the warmth unbearably soothing, and the Crumb-Runners have a saying that a Fatlander who stops to admire the view in the Fast is a Fatlander who stops.

The air smells of baking with no source. It is faint on the borders and strong in the heartland: new bread, brown butter, roasting onion, the spitting of fat, sometimes cinnamon or clove, sometimes a sharp clean smell of mint crushed for a sauce. Unfed children cannot remember a time before the smell, and are taught to breathe through the mouth, shallowly, and to spit when the mouth fills. Adults have a characteristic way of standing with the lips slightly parted and the tongue held flat against the floor of the mouth. Crumb-Runners plug their nostrils with wax and tallow.

Chimneys smoke over houses nobody lives in. From any hilltop in the heartland a traveller can count dozens of fine grey threads rising straight up into the gold, each one from an empty house where a hearth is lit and a table laid. On a still evening they look like the pins on a Hostwatch map.

The ground is warm where the god has walked. The Unfed, who live in the narrow cold margin of the east where the Rim's weather still reaches, track Orrum by melted frost. A warm front moving across the fields at night leaves a path of dark wet grass through the white, wide as a cart track, and in the morning the Hostwatch walk out and measure it and mark the map. The Unfed call a warm front *company*. "Company in the west field" is the commonest warning in the Fast. "Expecting company?" is the commonest threat.

The only true weather the Unfed fear is a change in the smell. When the baking smell sharpens and deepens toward roast meat, Orrum is near. When it goes sweet, toward custard and burnt sugar and warm milk, the god is laying a table for a child, and every household in range rings a hand-bell at the door and counts its children through it.

[pull] Company in the west field. | — the commonest warning in the Fast

### Hallowboard

The stone table of the Refusal stands on a bare, round, chalky hill at the exact centre of the continent. It is a slab of grey granite, a stone found nowhere else in the Fast, roughly forty feet across and waist-high to a tall man, and it has eight sides. It is always clean. In six hundred years no moss has grown on it, no bird has fouled it, and no leaf has lain on it overnight. Its surface is polished to a dull gleam, as if by ten thousand years of cloths.

Around seven of its eight sides the chalk of the hilltop is worn into a shallow trench where the forty thousand stood for nine days. The trench is pale and hard as a step, and in some lights the impressions of bare feet and knees can be seen in it, layered over each other. The bones of the Refusal were gathered and buried in the Barrows below the hill, but the chalk still turns up teeth after rain.

The eighth side is not worn. The chalk before it is smooth and white and has the faint bloom of untrodden ground. No Unfed will stand there. When asked why, they do not say it is forbidden. They say, with a puzzled look, as though the question had never occurred to them, that it is someone else's place. On the edge of the stone on that side there is a carved panel, the only carving on the table, and the figure in it has been removed: not chiselled out in the way of the other lands' carvings, but smoothed away, as though by a thumb pressed into wet clay, a long time ago, many times.

On Tablenight the Abstinent lead a procession up the hill to stand around Hallowboard, backs to it, through the longest night. In the morning there is sometimes the faint warm print of a large hand on the stone, palm down, beside each of the seven worn sides, like a host's hand resting by a guest's plate. There is never one on the eighth.

[pull] Seven sides worn, one side clean. Don't ask me whose. Nobody I know of ever stood there. Nobody I know of ever needed to. | — Reft Lammas, Roll-Keeper of Barrowgate

### Wanting

The Unfed's only real town stands on the cold eastern edge of the Fast, half a day from the Rim Road, where the land's endless Carving fades into the ordinary seasons of the world. It is a cold, plain, hearthless place of perhaps nine thousand souls: long low houses of rough grey stone, roofed with slate, with no chimneys. Windows are narrow. Doors are heavy, iron-strapped, and hung to open outward, so that no one inside can be said to have let anyone in. There are no inns, no bakeries, and no kitchens. Cooking is done in the Cold Yards, walled squares of beaten earth at the town's edge where fires are lit on the bare ground under the open sky and put out the moment the food is warm. The smell of Wanting is wet stone, woodsmoke, unwashed wool, and the faint ketone-and-pear-drop breath of nine thousand people who are always a little hungry.

The streets are quiet. People walk slowly, to save their strength. In Lack the town is ringed with frost, and the frost is precious: it means the god is not near.

### The Long Table

A valley road in the old heart of the country, running between two lines of beech-hung hills, where six hundred years of the Seated sit at one continuous table, nine miles end to end, eating nothing in good cheer. It began, the Hostwatch think, as a single table at a crossroads in the second century, and it has grown with every guest. The boards are of a pale wood that has never been identified. The cloth is unbroken and spotless for nine miles. The lamps are lit day and night. Perhaps eleven thousand people sit at it, on both sides, in every stage of wasting, from the newly sat (flushed, chattering, eating with both hands) to the most ancient, near the valley's head, who are only coats and smiles.

The road beside the Long Table is still used, because it is the only straight road across the heartland. Crumb-Runners take it at a trot with their eyes on the ground and their ears stopped with wax, because the Seated call out to everyone who passes. They call by name. They describe the dishes. They say *we saved you a seat,* and somewhere along the nine miles there is always an empty chair, pulled out, and it is always a little further along than the last time you came.

### The Pantry

A fortified granary in the centre of Wanting, the best-guarded building in the Fast and its only true seat of government. It is a squat round tower of dressed stone, five storeys high, with walls eight feet thick and no windows below the fourth floor. Its single door is iron, opens outward, and has three locks, whose keys are held by three different people. Inside, in cool dry dark, lie the grain sacks, the salt-fish casks, the cheeses, the dried peas, and the ledgers: every ration ever given out in the Fast, recorded by name, by day, by weight, back to 41 A.G. The smell inside is of dust, mouse, and sacking, and to an Unfed nose it is the richest smell in the world.

Every morning the Ration Line forms outside the iron door before dawn. Every evening the Keeper of the Pantry reads out the next day's list. A name not on the list does not eat. The list is never explained.

### The Empty Chair

The last standing temple of Orrum, on a wooded rise six miles west of Wanting: a long low hall of honey-coloured stone with a slate roof and a single great table running its whole length, laid, in the old days, for every worshipper in the parish. Every other temple of the Host was pulled down by the Unfed in the first century. This one was left, nobody now remembers why, and is kept by a single Verger, Sober Osmond, who every night lays one place at the head of the table, against all law, and every morning finds the plate clean. The Abstinent have condemned him eleven times. They have never sent anyone to stop him.

### Brindle Cross

The main crossing point between the Rim Road and the Fast, where the Wanting road meets the ring. A Rim Road Company toll-house, a stockade, a weighing-shed, and the Fast's border garrison under Marshal Keep Aldane, all crowded into a muddy hollow. Here grain is weighed in from Company wagons and signed for by the Pantry's clerks, and here the poachers are caught, or not. On the Rim side stands a Company inn, the Brimming Cup, which is the nearest hot meal to the Fast and is avoided by every Unfed on principle. Children from Wanting are brought to the Cross once, at the age of eight, to watch through the inn window as Rim folk eat, laughing, with their mouths open. It is meant to teach them disgust. It does not always work.

### The Barrows

A field of long, low, grassed mounds on the lower slopes of Hallowboard hill, where the dead of the Refusal were buried by the survivors in the spring of 1 A.G., in trenches, nine and ten deep. The grass on the Barrows is the only grass in the Fast that is untrimmed: long, coarse, and pale, like hair. In the evening the mounds are warm. The Hostwatch say that Orrum comes and sits among them some nights, the way a mourner sits at a grave, and that the warm patch it leaves is shaped like a person lying down with their arms around something.

### The Lean House

A long, windowless stone byre at the far end of Wanting, beyond the Cold Yards, where the people whose names have been left off the Pantry list go to wait. Nobody sends them there. There is no law. They simply go, because it is where the others went, and because at the Lean House no one will offer them food, and to be offered food when you have been left off the list is a torment past bearing. It is kept by a woman named Withy Gaunt. It is very quiet. The straw is changed weekly. There is a book.

[pull] There is a book. Everybody's in it, sooner or later, in one column or the other. | — Withy Gaunt, Matron of the Lean House

### Hartsell's Field

A ten-acre field on a south-facing slope in the old farmland north of Wanting, in the margin where the Rim's seasons still faintly reach. Yield Hartsell has ploughed it, harrowed it, and sown it every spring for fifty years, and every spring the seed has rotted. This year, in one corner, one seed came up. It is now a single blade of green wheat, four inches tall, inside a fence of hurdles, inside a second fence of thorn, inside a ring of tripwire hung with tin cans. It is the only living crop in the Fast. It is watered every night. Yield does not do it.

### The Stoopway

The old pilgrim road from Hallowboard to the eastern border, along which Orrum walked away on the tenth morning. Every gate, arch, and lintel along its length is too low by a foot, and every one of them has a smooth dark patch worn into the underside of its top beam, as if by a tall head brushing it, over and over, for six centuries. The Unfed do not walk the Stoopway. Children dare one another to touch the worn patches, which are warm.

### Ash Mill

A great stone watermill on a chalk stream in the middle country, abandoned since the Refusal, whose wheel still turns. The millstones grind, day and night, with nothing between them, and from the chute there falls a fine pale flour that is warm to the touch and smells of nothing. It is Orrum's flour. The Unfed will not go near it. Rim smugglers buy it from desperate Crumb-Runners and sell it in the Fatlands as Fast-flour, a cure for hunger, and the Fatlanders who eat it say, weeping, that for an hour afterwards they felt full. Some of them have since walked into the Fast and not come out.

### The Portion Stones

Nine rough uprights of grey fieldstone, waist-high, standing in a ragged line across a sheep-walk a mile west of Wanting, just inside where the Frostline ran in 559. They are unmarked. They were set up by the families of the nine people walked out from that line on the first night of Lack in that year (see The Giving, under History), and the Abstinent have twice ordered them pulled down and twice found them standing again in the morning, reset in the same holes, the turf tamped neatly round their feet. Nobody admits to resetting them. The grass around each stone is warm. Unfed who must pass them do so at a run, because a voice sometimes calls from the west side of the line, cheerfully, by name, in the accent of the old Wanting stands, saying *there's room, it's lovely, come and see*. The word *portion* is not used in the Fast, for anything, because of these stones.

[pull] Don't say portion. Say share, say part, say measure. Don't say portion. You'll set them off. | — a Bell Ward mother to her child

[box: Travelling in the Fast]
Crossing the Fast is forbidden by every Rim government, discouraged by the Company, and done every week by Crumb-Runners, Reckoners, Tablebreakers, Hostwatch trackers, poachers, and pilgrims. The heartland is fine walking country: dry roads, clear streams, no bandits, no beasts, and empty houses at every mile. The danger is entirely in accepting any of it. A party crossing the Fast carries all its own food and water, sleeps outdoors with its back to every building, keeps one watcher awake to sniff the air, and ties itself together at night with rope so that no one can get up and walk toward a light. Water from the streams of the Fast is safe, the Unfed insist, because it is not offered. Rain is safe. Anything laid out, poured, served, set down, or left on a doorstep is not. Running water inside a house is offered. A well with a bucket already drawn is offered. A gate held open is a gray area, and the Unfed close every one they pass through, on principle, and then go through it again.
[/box]

### The Thin Years and the First Sitting

The survivors of Hallowboard came down from the hill to find their land kept and their larders empty, and they did what starving people do, which is to walk toward food. The Rim sold to them. It sold to them at ruinous prices, because the Rim had, overnight, become rich and the Unfed had nothing to sell but what they had brought down from their ruined homes: silver plate, linen, pewter, the tableware of a people who had worshipped a Host. The Fatlands in particular bought it eagerly. There are still Gut-line houses in Sated that eat off the stolen plate of Unfed grandmothers and call it heirloom.

In 44 A.G., in a village called Corve Hatch, a family of five who had been left with nothing to sell came down one morning and found their table laid. The father, a man named Abel Corve, is recorded as having said the full three refusals and then a fourth, and then sat down, and then pulled out the chairs for his wife and children. His neighbours gathered at the window. The family ate, and laughed, and called out to them to come in, there was plenty. The neighbours knocked on the glass and wept and begged them to get up, and for eleven years they came every day and watched the family waste, and in all that time they never broke the window, because they were afraid of what was inside. When the last child's head finally drooped forward onto the cloth, in 55 A.G., the neighbours went home. The Corve house stands still. The family sit at their table. The window has been bricked up from the outside.

### The Vindication

For a century, the Unfed were the Table's laughing-stock: the people who died of politeness. Then, in the 130s, reports began to come in along the Rim of the first godsign: Hand-lines children who could not sleep with a door ajar, coughing in Brinehollow, a newborn in Kest saying "Not yet." By 200 it was undeniable. The Unfed, in their cold houses on the eastern edge, discovered that they were the only clean people left in the world, and it went to their heads at once and has never come down. Every Unfed sermon since has a version of the same line: *They laughed at the table. Now the table is laughing at them.* The Abstinent sent the first Envoy to the Rim Road in 214, to remind six nations that they were damned. The Fast has had one ever since.

### The Great Short

In 511 the Rim Road Company, in a pricing dispute with the Board of Plenty, embargoed all grain moving through Brindle Cross for one winter. The Pantry held enough for half the Fast. The Keeper of that year, a woman named Scarcity Hume, drew up the first list: the names who would eat. The others were simply not called. There was no announcement, no judgment, no blood. People came to the Ration Line, waited, heard the list read, did not hear their names, and went home. Some came back the next day, and the next. Then they stopped coming. Half the Fast died that winter, quietly, in their own houses, with their backs to their own doors. The Fast did not riot. Riot would have been an appetite. Scarcity Hume was honoured for it. The list has been a standing instrument of government since.

[pull] Scarcity Hume did not starve half the Fast. The Company did. She only read the names. | — Forbear Wend, in council, 627, as minuted

### The Giving

The worst thing the Unfed ever did to their own was done to buy a quiet winter, and it was bought for nothing. It is not in the Pantry ledgers. The pages for the Lack of 559 have been scraped with a knife, and the Abstinent of every generation since have sworn there is nothing to find. The Hostwatch kept a copy.

In the three winters before 559, Orrum's tables came closer to Wanting than they had ever come. They were laid on the roads within sight of the walls, in the Cold Yards themselves on still nights, at the doors of the outer stands. Each of those winters, a dozen or more Unfed walked out to them. The town was frightened in a way it had not been since the Thin Years, and frightened people listen to anyone who has a plan.

The plan came from a Starved Saint named Meagre Holt, nine months without food and luminous with it, who stood in Pantry Close in Carving of 558 and preached for a day and a night in a voice like a reed whistle. Her argument was simple, and it was the argument of every land on the Table: *a god that is fed is quiet.* The Godeaters had fed their gods and had peace for centuries. The Unfed had fed theirs nothing, and it was hungry for guests the way the others' gods were hungry for flesh. Give it a guest, she said. Give it a few, freely, and it will be satisfied, as any host is satisfied by a full table, and it will leave the rest of us be. She called them *the portions*. She said it was not murder, because nobody would touch them. They would only be walked to the edge of the warm ground, and left to choose.

The Abstinent stood on it for nine days, in the bare room, around the empty space. They asked for volunteers first, as is proper, three times, in Pantry Close. Nobody came forward. On the fourth asking, a simple-minded boy of the Lean End named Kindle Orme raised his hand, because he had seen everyone else looking at their feet and wanted to help. The Abstinent did not accept him. They did something that they considered cleaner. They drew up a list.

Nine names were left off the Pantry list in the last week of Carving 558, one from each of the stands and outer hamlets: an old man with a cough that would not have let him see Grace anyway; a widow with no children; Kindle Orme, who had asked; a girl of fifteen from Avery Steading named Cheer Avery, whose family had an old Host's name and a bad reputation; a Crumb-Runner who had been seen eating sitting down; a woman who had been heard singing in the Cold Yards; two brothers from Thinwick who had no one to speak for them; and a Hostwatch Tracker who had argued against the plan in the Close and had been, the Saint said, *too fond of it already*. They were not told why. They were given a full measure for the last week, which told them everything.

On the first night of Lack 559 the nine were washed by their kin, in cold water, standing, and dressed in white ration-cloth, and walked out of the west gate in a line, each with a Measure-Bearer at their elbow carrying a hand-bell. The rest of Wanting stood on the walls with their backs to the west, as on Tablenight, and sang the Long Decline. At the edge of the warm ground, where the frost stopped like a hem, the Measure-Bearers rang their bells three times, and turned, and walked back without looking round, and the nine walked on alone into the gold. Cheer Avery was heard to say *No, thank you* several times on the way, quite loudly, until she was out of earshot. Kindle Orme was heard to laugh. In the morning there were nine tables in a line across the sheep-walk a mile west, and nine guests at them, eating, and waving.

It did not work. That winter there were more tables near Wanting than in any winter before or since: forty-one Seatings to the Pantry's count, three times the year before. The tables came first to the doors of the nine families. Each was laid with the family's names on the cards, and each card was in the handwriting of the Portion from that house, and every card said some version of the same cheerful thing: *There's room. It's lovely. You were right to send me. Come and see.* Six of the nine families had walked west by the following Lack, whole households, holding hands. Cheer Avery's mother went last, in the spring of 560, and her place is beside her daughter's, the Hostwatch say, and the daughter is still waving.

Meagre Holt was struck from the list herself the following autumn, by an Abstinent that could not be seen to have listened to her, and went to the Lean House, where she refused nothing because she was offered nothing, and died in eleven days, still preaching. Her last words in the Book of the Omitted are not a recipe. They are: *It would have worked if we had meant it.*

The Hostwatch's conclusion, entered in the restricted ledger in 561, is short. *The Host does not want a guest. It wants the house. What we taught it this winter is that we will give.* The Abstinent have never repeated the Giving. They have never forgotten that it was possible, and in a bad winter, when the list is long and the tables close, the old men in the bare room do not look at one another, and the Portion Stones stand a mile west, warm at their feet.

### The Reckoning

The Reckoners date their founding to the night of the 16th of Lack, 402, when nineteen young Unfed under a woman named Forswear Kell crossed the Rim Road into the Fatlands and entered the manor of a Gut-lines family named Brawne. They killed everyone in it: the Laden matriarch in her bed, her sons, her daughters, their spouses, and the infants in the nursery, whose deaths the Reckoners' own chronicle records only in a single line, *the line was ended.* They did not touch the servants. They did not take anything. They walked home through the Fatlands carrying nothing at all, starved, and were received at Wanting as heroes by the young and in cold silence by the Abstinent, who did not condemn them. The Reckoners have crossed the Rim every year since.

### The Calling-Cards

In the spring of 638, a girl of twelve in a hamlet outside Wanting went down at dawn to find a table laid in her family's kitchen, as had happened in living memory a dozen times. Before her own plate there was a folded card. It said, in a round and loving hand, *Abstain*, and under it, smaller, the pet name her dead grandmother used to call her, which no living person knew. The cards have come to more than three hundred houses since. A Hostwatch clerk who collected and compared them found that the handwriting is not the same on every card. It is, each time, the handwriting of someone the finder loved and lost.

## Rule, Law and Politics

The Fast is governed by the Abstinent, a council of between nine and fifteen elders who claim descent from those who stood on Hallowboard hill. Their authority is entirely moral and therefore absolute. They make no laws in the ordinary sense, because the Five Rules are the only law the Unfed recognise. They hear no lawsuits, because there is nothing in the Fast worth suing over. They raise no taxes, because there is nothing to tax. Their whole power is the Pantry.

Every scrap of food in the Fast is imported by Crumb-Runners or bought from the Rim Road Company, pooled in the Pantry at Wanting, and rationed out by the council through the Keeper of the Pantry. The standard ration, the Pantry measure, is half an oatcake and a thumb of fat or cheese per adult per day, with pease or salt fish twice a week. It is enough to keep an adult alive and upright and incapable of much else. Children get a three-quarter measure until twelve. Hard labourers, Reckoners on campaign, and Tablebreakers get a full measure, which is the source of most envy in the Fast.

Exile from the Pantry is the only punishment the Fast imposes, and it is a death sentence that leaves no blood on anyone's hands. It is never pronounced. A name is simply left off the list. The person may beg, may appeal to the Abstinent in open session, may try to buy food from a Crumb-Runner with whatever they have, may cross the Rim and try to live there. Most do not. The Unfed are poor travellers and worse beggars, and among their own people the omitted are treated with a terrible gentle courtesy, never mentioned, never offered anything, greeted in the street with lowered eyes, as though they were already in the Barrows. Most walk to the Lean House within a fortnight.

The two great poles of Fast politics are the **Keepers** and the **Reckoners**. The Keepers are not a faction but a temperament, the majority view on the Abstinent: the Unfed's duty is to refuse, endure, and stay clean, to keep the Five Rules and outlast the Godeaters, who are damning themselves without any help. The Reckoners hold that refusing was not enough, and that the Godeaters must be made to answer for it, bloodline by bloodline. The Abstinent have never formally approved a Reckoner raid. They have never condemned one. Every Reckoner on campaign draws a full measure from the Pantry.

Every other land has a use for the Unfed. Having no godsign, they are in effect a whole nation of Blanks: blood that carries no god, organs that grow no Regrowth, flesh that the Cutwrights' College reckons at Cut 0 and the surgeons of four countries reckon at a noble's ransom. Poachers cross the border for them every month. The Abstinent's answer is Marshal Keep Aldane and his garrison of sixty at Brindle Cross, and a standing order that any Unfed taken alive across the border is to be considered dead and removed from the Pantry list.

### Murder by Omission

The Abstinent have killed more people than the Reckoners, the poachers and the god together, and not one of the elders has ever touched anyone. They say this is the point. Rim jurists who have studied the Pantry describe it as the most efficient instrument of execution on the Table: no headsman, no rope, no blood, no appeal, no body to account for, and a population so trained in courtesy that the condemned walk themselves to the place of death and lie down in clean straw.

It takes, for an Unfed already living on half a ration, between three and seven weeks. Dr. Mercy Thrane, who has sat with more of the omitted than anyone but Withy Gaunt, divides it into stages in her book, and the Lean House divides it the same way, in plainer words.

**The Loud Days** (the first week). The hunger is in the belly and it is enormous, a living thing, clenching and turning and crying out. The stomach growls so loudly that people across a room hear it, and the omitted are ashamed, and press their hands to it. They cannot sleep. They think of nothing but food. Most still come to the Ration Line every morning and stand in it, in their place, and listen to the list, and do not hear their name, and go home. This is not hope. It is habit, and the Unfed say it is also manners: a person on the list does not stop coming until they have been struck twice.

**The Bright Days** (the second and third weeks). The belly goes quiet. The hunger moves outward, into the hands and feet, which are cold all the time, white at the tips and then blue. The breath turns sweet and sharp, pear-drops and nail-varnish. The mind, for a while, goes clear and high and fast, and many of the omitted describe a kind of joy: everything is very bright, every sound is very clear, the god's baking smell is everywhere and is the most beautiful thing they have ever known. This is when they walk to the Lean House. Nobody sends them. There is a path worn in the frost from the Lean End to the byre door, and they follow it.

**The Soft Days** (the fourth and fifth weeks). The body begins to eat itself in earnest. The face swells, oddly, at the eyelids and the jaw, so that the omitted look plump and young for a few days, a cruelty the Lean House calls *the borrowed cheeks*. Then it falls in all at once. The ankles swell and pit when pressed, leaving a thumbprint that stays. Hair comes out at a touch, in tufts, and gathers in the straw. The gums bleed and the teeth loosen. The skin goes dry and grey and fine as old paper, and over the forearms and spine grows a soft pale down, like the fur on a newborn animal, which is the body trying to keep itself warm with the last thing it has. The omitted lie still now, curled on their sides, and talk, quietly and constantly, about food.

**The Last Days.** The heart slows until Withy can count its beats against her own breath: one beat to two breaths, one to three. The omitted stop feeling hungry. That is the sign. They go calm and polite and a little far away. They thank Withy for the straw. They recite recipes, in loving and exact detail, dishes they have never eaten and could not have known, the old dishes of the heartland from before the Refusal, mutton with caper sauce, eel pie, syllabub. Then one night they stop, mid-sentence, usually somewhere in the instructions for a sauce, and Withy writes down where.

Wanting knows all this. That is the thing outsiders cannot grasp. Everyone in the Fast has walked a sister or a father or a neighbour to the corner of the Lean End and turned back. There has been only one recorded case in which a Lister confessed to striking a name for her own reasons. In 604 a Lister of Hillward named Abate Sorrow left a rival's husband off the list for nine days running, then restored him, then struck him again, until he walked to the Lean House on his own. The Abstinent heard her, and the next evening her name was read, and the next, and the next, for thirty-one years, every night of her life. That was the sentence. She was never once left off.

## Culture and Daily Life

### Thinness Is Holiness

The Unfed eat once a day, cold, standing, and without visible pleasure. They eat facing a wall or the open sky, never one another. To chew with an expression on one's face is coarse; to close the eyes while eating is obscene; to make a sound of appreciation is a confession. An Unfed who catches themselves enjoying a mouthful will spit it into their hand, look at it, and then eat it again, slowly, as a penance. Fat people are pitied, then distrusted, then reported. The most admired body in the Fast is one in which every rib can be counted through a shirt. Unfed lovers trace each other's bones in the dark, in silence, as tenderly as other peoples kiss.

The whole people has the look of chronic hunger: the dry, brittle hair, sometimes faded to a reddish straw; the downy fur of lanugo along the forearms and jaw; the slight puffiness of ankles and faces in those whose bodies have begun to fail; the cold blue fingertips; the breath that smells of pear-drops. Their skin bruises at a touch. Their wounds heal slowly. They feel the cold terribly, and in winter they wear everything they own at once. Their teeth, by contrast, are good: there is little sugar in the Fast to rot them.

[pull] You can count a good man's ribs from across the Yard. That's how you know he's never stolen. | — a Hillward matchmaker

### Names

Children are named for the Refusal: Forbear, Abstain, Decline, Stint, Tarry, Nay, Spare, Lent, Wont, Want, Meagre, Scant, Yield, Keep. They are named on the day they first refuse something, usually the breast, which is why many Unfed children are three or four months old before they have a name. The surnames are older, from before the Gorging, and often taken from vanished villages or the trades of the Host: Ashby, Hollis, Moss, Avery, Rooke, Bellamy, Garrity, Hartsell. Some families quietly carry names like Ardent, Blythe, Welcome, Mercy, Hallow, which were Orrum's names once, and are now a kind of family shame. An Unfed who changes their name to something from the Rim is considered to have emigrated, even if they never leave Wanting.

### No Hearths

Homes in the Fast have no fireplace, no oven, no chimney, and no dining table. Unfed furniture is a bench along the wall, a shelf for the ration-cloth, and a sleeping pallet with the head toward the door. The great absence at the centre of every room is the clue to every Unfed house: where other peoples would put the table, the Unfed leave bare floor, and on most floors in the Fast that space is worn smooth in a ring by generations of people walking around it, not across it. Families gather, if they gather at all, standing, against the walls.

Cooking is done outdoors on bare ground in the Cold Yards, quickly, and the fire is stamped out the moment the pot is warm. Food is eaten from the hand. A spoon is a luxury and a fork is a disgrace. The Unfed bring their own water in their own flasks to every meeting and drink only from them.

### The Decline

The proper response to any offer of anything is "No, thank you," said three times. Accepting on the fourth is good manners. Accepting on the first is a scandal. This applies to a cup of water, a hand up a step, a seat at a meeting, a compliment, a marriage proposal. A host who offers only three times is mean; a guest who takes on the third is greedy. A great deal of Unfed social intelligence goes into the fourth offer: who makes it, in what tone, and whether it is meant. Orrum, who has made hundreds of thousands of first offers, second offers, and third offers across six centuries, has never once been known to make a fourth. The Hostwatch think this is not an accident. The god, they say, knows the manners of its people perfectly, and is waiting to be asked properly to stop.

[pull] Ask me again. No, properly. No, thank you. Ask me again. | — the whole of an Unfed proposal of marriage, overheard in Bell Ward

### Pride

The Unfed are the only innocent people on the Table, and they are insufferable about it. They do not pray for the damned of other lands. They do not pity them. They report on them, with a certain cold relish, in sermons and broadsheets and the dispatches of Envoy Prudence Lowell, and every Unfed child can recite the stages of the Regrowth of all six Godeater lands the way other children recite kings. When an Unfed meets a Rim person, they will at some point, usually early, ask after the state of that person's blood. It is meant as a courtesy.

### Marriage and Kin

Unfed marriages are made by the Abstinent, through the Pantry: two people who marry share one ration-line, and their household's measure is adjusted accordingly. Courtship consists of one person repeatedly offering the other something small (a pebble, a feather, a blade of the precious Rim grass) and being refused three times, for months, until one evening the fourth offer is made and taken. Wedding vows are spoken standing back to back. What passes between spouses in private is nobody's business, and the Unfed speak of it hardly at all; desire is an appetite, and appetites are suspect. Unfed birth rates are low and falling, which the Abstinent attribute to virtue and Dr. Mercy Thrane attributes to starvation. Most Unfed women of childbearing age have not bled in years.

### Children

Unfed children are small for their age, solemn, and very good. They learn the Five Rules before their letters, the Decline before their names, and the stages of every foreign Regrowth before they can count to twenty. They are given one toy, usually a carved bone or stone. They are not allowed to play at house, or at tea, or at any game with plates. They are watched constantly, because Orrum loves them best: the tables laid for children are the most beautiful, all honey and custard and little iced cakes, and the voice that calls from the door is always the voice of a mother or grandmother. A child who goes missing is presumed Seated, and the household rings its bell at the door every dusk for a year, calling the child's name, and then stops. The Unfed do not hold funerals for the Seated. You cannot bury a guest who has not left.

Unfed children are not allowed games with plates, but they have games. The commonest is the counting-out rhyme used to choose who is *it* in Stand-Still, a game in which one child stands in the middle of a ring with their eyes shut while the others creep up, and if the one in the middle says *come in*, everyone must freeze, and whoever moved last is out, and must stand against the wall with their back to the game until the end. The rhyme is chanted with a finger pointing round the ring, one word to each child.

[fiction]
One for the oatcake, two for the fat,
Three for the no, thank you, and that is that.
Four for the yes in a nicer coat,

The Abstinent have twice tried to forbid the last three lines and the children have twice ignored them. No adult in the Fast will say where the eighth line came from. Most of them learned it from their own grandparents, who did not know either.

### Death Rites

The Unfed die, mostly, of hunger and cold and the long, slow failure of bodies that have never once been properly fed. Their deaths are quiet. The dying are laid on the floor in the bare space where a table would be, with their head toward the door, and the family stand around the walls with their backs to the body and say the Decline three times, which is the whole of the last rites. *No, thank you. No, thank you. No, thank you.* The dead are buried without food, without grave goods, without coffins, wrapped in a single ration-cloth, face down, in the open fields, so that they cannot sit up. Graves are unmarked. Sometimes, in the morning, a grave is found to have a little table beside it, laid for one, a candle burning. The family breaks it with an axe and buries the pieces.

### Festivals

The Unfed have one festival, and it is Tablenight. On the longest night of Lack, every person in the Fast stands outdoors from dusk until dawn, with their back to every door, in the bitter cold, holding the hands of those on either side. They do not speak. They do not move. On that night, in every house in the Fast, the table is laid. It is laid in every Unfed house, every barn, every byre, every outbuilding, and in the Pantry itself, behind the three-locked iron door. The smell of it is so strong that people faint, and are held up by their neighbours, and are not allowed to fall. At dawn the households turn and go in and break the tables with axes. Every Unfed house owns an axe for no other purpose. It hangs by the door.

[pull] My knees went at about the third hour. My neighbours held me up till dawn. You don't fall on Tablenight. You'd fall forward, and forward is in. | — a Thinwick widow

### Crime and Punishment

There is little crime in the Fast, because there is little to steal and less to want. The commonest crimes are hoarding (keeping back part of a ration), mouth-theft (stealing a ration), and feeding: giving one's own food to another, which the Abstinent forbid because it confuses the ledgers and, worse, because it is hospitality. All are punished the same way, by a cut in measure, or in grave cases, omission. Murder is rare and is punished, in effect, by the victim's family, who are given the murderer's ration for a year. The worst crime in the Fast is to say "Come in" to anyone. It is never punished, because it never needs to be: the Unfed will not cross the threshold of anyone who has said it, ever again, for any reason, and in a land where every scrap of food comes through a common door, that is enough.

### Oaths on the Dead

The Unfed do not swear by the gods, having refused theirs. They swear on the Barrows, by their dead ancestors of the hill: *By the ones who stood.* A serious oath is sworn standing in the chalk trench at Hallowboard, barefoot, in one's own ancestor's footprint, and Unfed who believe their families stood on a particular stretch of the trench will travel two days to swear there. Oaths sworn on the Barrows are not supernaturally binding, as Oathen's are. They are binding by something worse, which is shame: an Unfed who breaks an oath sworn by the ones who stood has made their ancestors' deaths into a meal, and no one will stand beside them on Tablenight. In practice, this means they stand alone, and are found in the morning sitting at the table in their own house, looking very happy.

### Kin and the Knife

The Fast has little murder, and less of it between strangers. When an Unfed kills, it is almost always family, and almost always for a reason the Fast's own creed supplies. The Reckoner doctrine that a line touched by a god must be ended does not stop at the Rim, and every few years it comes home. A son returns from a Rim apprenticeship and his sleeves are buttoned. A sister who worked a winter as scullion in a Company inn begins to crave the smell of the Spit's tallow-stalls. A cousin who was a foundling is found by Scarce Odell's ledgers to carry High Cut blood. The Abstinent will not omit for godsign, because godsign is not a crime under the Five Rules. Families have sometimes decided that the Five Rules were not enough.

## Walking West

> She's not dead. Don't say dead. She's walking west.
>> — what a Sallow Hatch child was told, in 633, and is still being told

Every Unfed knows the phrase, and every Unfed lowers their voice to say it. To walk west is to go to the god: to get up in the night, or in the gold of an afternoon, and turn your back on the frost and the town and the list, and walk into the warm ground until you find a table, and sit. It is not called dying, because the Seated do not die. It is not called accepting, because that is a word for children who make a mistake. It is called walking west, and it is the way the Fast loses most of the people it does not starve.

The Abstinent do not keep a count of the walkers. The Hostwatch do, because each one is a new pin on the Great Map, and in the restricted ledger there is a column for them beside the column for the god's courtesies. In an ordinary year it is a few dozen. In a short year, when the list is long, it is hundreds. The pins are not scattered. They come in clusters, the way a fever comes through a street, and that is the thing the Hostwatch have been trying for three centuries to make the Abstinent understand: walking west is catching.

### How It Spreads

It begins with someone who has nothing left to refuse for. A widow struck from the list. A Measure-Bearer who has carried one too many short baskets. A Tablebreaker whose Rope-Holder did not pull fast enough. A mother whose child was omitted with a grandmother and would not leave her. They go quiet, first, and then very calm, and the people who love them notice that they have started to stand facing west in the evenings, with their eyes half closed and their lips parted, breathing the smell. They start to give things away. Not food (they have none), but the carved bone toy, the good wool scarf, the axe from beside the door. They say kind things to people they have never been kind to. Then one morning their pallet is empty, and the door is open, and the frost outside it has a line of footprints going west that stop, a few hundred yards out, where the frost stops.

And then, within a week or a month, someone else goes. Usually someone who loved the first one. Usually someone who has been to visit them, on the far verge, and seen them sitting at a table in the gold, flushed and smiling and well, waving, calling out that it's lovely, that there's room, that they were wrong to be afraid. Grief in the Fast has nowhere to go: no feast, no wake, no grave, no body. The walker is not dead. They are a mile away, happy, and asking for you.

### The Sallow Hatch Walking

Sallow Hatch was a hamlet of sixty people on the Brindle road, a day's carry from Wanting, on the edge of the Frostline. In the Lack of 633 the Pantry ran short and the hamlet's measure was cut by a third. On the fourth of Lack a widow named Lean Corby, who had been struck in Carving for selling a chit, walked west. On the sixth her sister followed her. On the ninth, three of their cousins went together, holding hands, in daylight, past a Measure-Bearer who called after them by name three times and then, because it was not his place, did not call a fourth time. By the twelfth day nineteen people of Sallow Hatch had walked west, eleven of them in the last three nights.

The survivors tried everything the Fast knows. They roped themselves together at night, wrist to wrist, a whole household on one rope; one family woke to find their rope untied, coiled neatly by the door, and the youngest daughter gone, and a folded napkin on her pillow. They took turns at the hamlet bell and rang it every quarter hour all night, and the walkers walked through the ringing. They sent to Wanting for Tablebreakers, who came, and broke every table within three miles, and the walkers sat down at the broken ones, among the splinters, on the ground, and were found there in the morning, kneeling at nothing, smiling, and when the Breakers lifted them they screamed like kettles and died in the road within the hour, apologising.

On the thirteenth day the Abstinent ordered Sallow Hatch emptied. The remaining forty-one were walked into Wanting under escort, roped, with Hostwatch Trackers at either end of the string, and housed in the Lean End, and every one of them was put on the full list. Only two more walked west that winter. In the spring of 635, when the warm ground had drawn back from the Brindle road, the survivors were allowed home, and Sallow Hatch is a hamlet of forty-one again, on the Measure-Bearers' round, with every doorframe notched. The Hostwatch's verdict in the restricted ledger was a single line: *They went because the others were there.*

The nineteen of Sallow Hatch sit together now, at a long table in a water-meadow four miles west of the old hamlet, all on one side, all facing east, toward home. Crumb-Runners on the Brindle road can see them on clear evenings. They wave. They wave at every cart, every Bearer, every child. Their kin in Sallow Hatch do not go to visit them. Some of them, on still evenings, stand at the western edge of the hamlet and look, and do not wave back, and are fetched in by their neighbours, gently, by the elbow.

### The Ones Left Behind

The Fast has no rite for a walker. You cannot bury a guest who has not left, and you cannot mourn someone who is calling to you. Families of walkers are not shunned, exactly, but they are watched, the way a house is watched where there has been a fever. Neighbours take turns to sit with them at night. Their axes are taken down from beside the door and kept by someone else, because an axe is for breaking tables and a grieving person may want to go and break the one their mother is sitting at, and that is the surest road west there is. Their names are moved, quietly, up the list. That is the only kindness the Abstinent have, and they use it.

There is a custom, unsanctioned and widespread, in the Lean End and the poorer stands: the family of a walker cuts a notch in the frame of the door through which they went, at the height of the walker's heart. Some doorframes in the Lean End have six or seven notches, at different heights, the last ones small. Cease Abbot, who keeps the bell-rope at the Lean End, says he can tell a house that will lose someone else by whether the notches have been painted over.

[pull] Hers was the bell I rang. All night, every quarter. She walked past me in the dark, she touched my arm going by. She said, You're doing ever so well. | — a bell-ringer of Sallow Hatch, 633

[box: Running Walking West]
Walking West is the Fast's form of the Despair rules in the Rules chapter, and it is the hardest thing in this chapter to run well. Run it as tragedy. Never describe sitting down as a release or a relief, and never make it look like an answer: describe the table from the outside, the waving, the grief of the ones on the verge, the notch in the doorframe. **Contagion:** when an NPC or PC the community knows walks west, every Unfed of that household or stand at Fray 5 or more makes a Dread 2 check; on a failure they gain the Pull as a temporary Derangement for a season, triggered by the sight or smell of the warm ground. A PC who has been to the far verge to visit a walker raises the Difficulty of their next Invitation check by +2. **Holding on:** companions may stop a walking PC with a rope, a grip (opposed Gut + Brawl), or a reason (Tongue + Persuade, Hard 14, Grim 18 if the reason is not true). Let the players offer reasons. The best ones are small: a promise to show someone the sprout in Hartsell's Field, an unfinished argument, a debt. A PC who walks west and reaches a table is Seated, as described under Invitation Checks, and their companions make a Dread 4 check. Give the grief time at the table, out of character as well as in it. Check in with your players before and after.
[/box]

## Factions

### The Abstinent

**Who they are.** The council of elders who hold the Pantry, and with it the life of every Unfed. They are old, thin, and cold, and they speak slowly to save breath. They meet standing, in a bare stone room on the top floor of the Pantry, around an empty space where a table would be.

**Beliefs and wants.** The Refusal was the one clean act in the history of the world, and the Unfed exist to keep it clean. Survival matters less than purity; the Pantry exists not to feed the Fast but to keep it from accepting. The Abstinent want to outlast the Godeaters, and they want, more quietly than they would admit, to never be asked who decides the list.

### The Reckoners

**Who they are.** Raiders, assassins, and zealots who carry the Refusal across the border on a blade. They travel in bands of five to twenty, on foot, carrying their own food, and they strike at the highest Cuts of the Godeater lands in their own beds. They are the Fast's only export.

**Beliefs and wants.** Refusing was not enough. The Godeaters ate their gods and the gods are coming back through their blood; therefore the blood must be ended. Not punished: ended. A Reckoner raid is not a murder but a weeding, and it is incomplete unless the whole kin-line dies, from the grandmother in the bed to the infant in the cradle to the cousin at university in another city. They want every High Cut line on the Table extinct before the Seating, and they believe they are saving the world, which makes them terribly calm.

| Rank | Title | Grants |
| 1 | Carrier | Bears the band's food and water on a raid; a full measure. |
| 2 | Knife | Enters the house; a Reckoner blade, black-iron, unadorned. |
| 3 | Kin-Reader | Holds the stolen genealogies; plans which beds, in what order. |
| 4 | Band-Captain | Leads a raid; chooses the line; may call on safehouses on the Rim. |
| 5 | Reckoner-Captain | Sets the season's lines; a seat (unofficial) at the Abstinent's door. |

**Joining.** Volunteer at Wanting's west gate on the first day of Carving. A recruit is taken on one raid as a Carrier and watched at the bedsides. Those who look away are sent home. Those who do not are given a knife. **Enemies:** every High Cut house, the Cutwrights' College (whose ledgers they steal), the Netwatch, the Wakers, the Second Table. **Allies:** the Purgation (warily), some of the Thin of the Fatlands.

[box: The Bedside]
Reckoners kill at night, indoors, quietly, with knives. They enter through servants' doors, kitchens, coal-holes. They never eat anything in the houses they enter, never drink, never sit, never touch a table. They work through a household by the kin-reader's list, in an order planned weeks before: eldest first, because the eldest are furthest along and most dangerous; then the heirs; then the rest. The worst of it, for those who have done it and come home, is not the blood. It is the beds. High Cut beds are rich and soft and warm, and smell of good food digested, and the people in them are well fed and sleep deeply, and an Unfed Knife standing over one of them in the dark, starved, stiff with cold from three nights on the road, feels something in his chest that is not righteousness. Reckoners have a phrase for it: *bedside hunger.* Some of them come home and cannot stop thinking about the beds. Some come home and cannot stop thinking about the nursery. The band-captains watch for both.
[/box]

#### A House, Room by Room

The Reckoners' own chronicle is terse to the point of cruelty, and their raids are recorded in it in a single line each: house, town, date, *the line was ended*. What a raid is like comes down only in what the Knives say afterward, to Forswear Tallow on Knife Row, who asks every one of them the same question, and to Dr. Mercy Thrane, who has asked a few.

It is warm. That is what all of them say first. A High Cut house in Sated or Lastgate or the Vigil in Lack is warmer than anywhere a Knife has ever been, and the warmth hits a starved Unfed coming in from three nights on the road like a blow to the chest, so that some of them stand gasping in the scullery and cannot move for a full minute. It smells of food. Not a table, but the ghost of a hundred thousand meals soaked into the panelling: roast fat, wine, wax, baking, the sweet heavy breath of a house where nobody has ever been hungry. The floors are soft with carpet. The Knives go up the servants' stair in stockinged feet, carrying their boots, and the stair is warm under their soles, and they can hear the house sleeping: the slow, deep, wet breathing of big well-fed bodies, heavy snores, a dog sighing by a banked fire.

High Cut sleepers are hard to kill. They are big, and the god in them does not want to go. A Fatlands Gut-line matriarch in the Course is swollen with soft, cool, loam-dark flesh that gives under the blade like turned earth, and the knife must go deep; she wakes, and she is strong, and she bellows, and the bed is a feather-mattress three feet thick that swallows the Knife's knees. The blood of a well-fed body comes thick and dark and fast, hot, smelling of iron and of something richer underneath, like the drippings from a joint, and it soaks into the feather-ticking and wells up again around the Knife's hands, and does not stop for a long time. In the Vigil the Eye-lines sleep with their eyes open and watch the knife come. In Sallowreach the old Hand-lines cannot be killed at all, and the Reckoners must carry them, wrapped in their own sheets, out over the border, where they finish; the Knives say those are the worst nights, because the old dead talk to them the whole way, politely, about the weather.

The servants are not touched. They are woken, sometimes, by the noise, and come to doors in their nightgowns with candles, and see what they see, and the Knives look at them and say the Decline, *no, thank you*, three times, softly, which means *go back to bed*, and most of them do. It is the servants who find the house in the morning. Many of them are Scraplings, low-Cut, and some of them, the Reckoners have come to understand, are glad.

The nursery is last. The chronicle gives it the same line it gives everything else. So will this book. The band-captains choose who goes into it, and they choose the ones who have done it before, and afterward they watch those Knives for a long time.

#### The Laying-Out

The eldest of a line is not killed in bed. Since the first raid on the Brawne house in 402, Reckoner bands have carried the eldest of every High Cut line they end down to the house's own dining table, and laid them on it on their back, with their hands at their sides, as Orrum lay on Hallowboard. The Reckoners call this the Laying-Out. It is the only rite they have, and it is the nearest thing in the Fast to a human sacrifice, though every Reckoner would spit at the word.

The band stands around the table. They stand on every side but one. Nobody remembers why one side is left empty; the old Knives say it is because the eldest's own kin will never stand there now, and the young Knives accept this, and the band-captains, who have stood at many Layings-Out, will not discuss it and do not look at that side of the table. The matriarch or patriarch lies on the polished wood among the candlesticks and the silver épergne and the bowl of hothouse fruit, enormous, in a nightgown, often bleeding already from the struggle on the stair. They always talk. They threaten, then they bargain, and then, every time, without exception, they offer food. It is the deepest instinct of the High Cuts, the Knives say; a Laden matriarch with a knife at her throat will tell you there is a ham in the larder and a cold goose and a Brinehollow cheese, and to eat, eat, you poor starved things, look at you, and she means it, and it is the most obscene thing the Knives have ever heard.

The band says the Decline over them. *No, thank you. No, thank you. No, thank you.* Softly, together, as the hill said it. Then the band-captain opens their throat, and the band stands and watches as the eldest bleeds out onto their own table, and nobody touches them, and nobody looks away. The blood runs to the table's edge and over it and drips onto the carpet with a sound the Knives say they hear for years, and it pools in the dishes, and runs among the fruit. It takes longer than outsiders imagine. The band waits until it is over. Then the band-captain lays a clean white plate at the head of the table, upside down, and a folded napkin beside it, and they leave. The Reckoners do not believe in gods. The rite is not for anyone. It is only to say, in a language the High Cuts will understand: *we were offered, and we did not eat.*

The servants who find the eldest in the morning, laid on the table with the plate at their head, are the source of every Rim story about the Reckoners. In the Fatlands the plate is called the *Unfed's bill*.

### The Starved Saints

**Who they are.** Ascetics who have stopped eating altogether. Most die in the second month, in the open, in silence, attended by others who will soon follow. A few have kept it up far longer than a body can. They live in the open fields, sitting in the cold, saying the Decline at every sound.

## The Fast Now

Orrum is leaving. For six hundred years the god kept to the Fast, laying tables for a people who would not come. Now its tables have turned up in three other lands, and the Hostwatch report it walking the Rim Road at night, stooping to look in at inn windows. Every week Spare Tolland pins it a little further from Hallowboard. Every week the extra chair is there.

Pilgrims from the starving Fatlands are crossing in to find it. Osanna Blythe's three hundred are the first column; there are rumours of thousands behind them. Marshal Keep Aldane holds Brindle Cross with sixty men against a tide of people who are not trying to hurt anyone, only to sit down.

The Pantry is short. There are four months of grain for six months of winter, and the Rim Road Company has not explained why the autumn deliveries came light. Mother Stint Avery has already decided which villages. Forbear Wend has not yet signed.

A farmer has grown a single green sprout in dead soil. A Saint is being fed by something. A child came home plump. And Meek Garrity's papers, copied by hand and passed from house to house in Wanting at night, say the worst thing the Unfed have ever heard: that their ancestors never refused. They were only late, and the others had left them nothing.

[pull] Four months of grain. Six of winter. I can do it to the day. I cannot do it to the name. | — Mother Stint Avery

### Adventure Hooks

- **The Light Cargo.** Tarry Moss hires the PCs to guard a grain run from Brindle Cross. Halfway in, his count is wrong by one sack, and then by two, and the missing sacks are found laid out on a table in a farmhouse, ground into bread. ### Dr. Mercy Thrane

A physician of starvation, forty-seven, gaunt and grey-eyed, with spectacles mended with wire and a satchel of scales, calipers, and notebooks. [box: The Blank-Organ Trade]
Godsign does not live in Unfed flesh. A kidney, liver, eye, length of gut, or pint of blood taken from one of the Unfed is, so far as any surgeon can tell, wholly clean, and when it is sewn into a Godeater it is the one part of that body the god does not grow into. For a few months, or a few years, the High Cut buyer has one organ that is only theirs. Laden matriarchs buy Unfed stomachs to feel, for a season, what it is to be full. Lidless Eye-lines buy Unfed eyes that can close. The price for a whole pair of Unfed kidneys in Sated is about 6,000 lacks; a heart in Lastgate, twice that. The organs are taken from the living where possible, because the Unfed die very quickly of the surgery and the buyers want the organ warm. Poachers keep their captives alive, and fed, for exactly that reason. Then the godsign of the buyer slowly grows into the new organ, as ivy grows into a wall, and in a few years they need another.
[/box]

#### The Trade, Itemised

The Blank trade is not a black market in the way Rim smugglers' markets are. It is a luxury, and it behaves like one: discreet, referred, expensive, and very nearly respectable. It has surgeons of standing in four capitals, a vocabulary of polite words (an Unfed captive is a *lodger*, an organ is a *refitting*, a living Unfed kept for repeated cuttings is a *keeping-room guest*), and a clientele drawn almost entirely from the High Cuts, who have the money and the most god to escape. The best-known practitioner is Master Quillon Barr of Sated, a courteous, careful man who has read Dr. Mercy Thrane's book on starvation with genuine admiration and keeps his lodgers alive between cuttings for years, in rooms with no west windows, warm, fed by tube, some of them in brine-tanks to the neck in his Lastgate house, where the cold of the fen keeps the cut flesh sweet. Several of his lodgers have begun, after years of it, to put on weight. Some of them have asked not to be taken home.

The prices below are those Dr. Thrane has recorded from bills of sale, the Red Ledger she keeps of her missing patients, and the confession of one poacher. They are what a buyer pays the surgeon. The poacher gets a tenth.

| A blank eye | Lidless Eye-lines of the Vigil | 3,000 | A season or two; the first blink is weeping joy |

| A blank stomach | The Gut-lines, to feel full once | 7,000 | A season; they eat until it bursts |

The trade's dirtiest secret is the one Dr. Thrane has not yet been able to prove. Some of the buyers, she believes, are not trying to escape their gods at all. A man with a two-tined fork pinned inside his lapel has been buying children. They are not for refitting. A child of no god, raised from infancy in a closed house on nothing but one land's god-meat, would grow into an empty vessel filled with a single god and nothing else: no inheritance, no dilution, six centuries of bloodline skipped in one generation. What would grow in such a body is a question the Second Table has apparently decided to answer by experiment. Dr. Thrane has three addresses and no proof. Kale Dragoman has been asked, once, to deliver to one of them, and refused three times, and the fourth asking was very well paid.

## Playing the Unfed

> Hunger is honest.
>> — Unfed proverb

You are the only person on the Table with nothing in your blood but blood. You have never tasted a god. You have also never, not once in your life, been full. You are proud, cold, thin, disciplined, and very, very hungry, and the warmest, kindest thing in the world is following you, laying tables.

**Attribute bonus:** +1 to any one attribute of your choice. (Most Unfed take Caul or Gut.)

**Cut:** 0. You cannot Partake. You have no Tooth, no Hunger track, no Regrowth, no Gifts, no Wants, and no Sop. Instead you have **Want** and **Pangs**.

### Want: The Refusal Mechanic

The Godeaters have the god in them. You have the space where a god would be, and you have learned to use it. **Want** is a pool from 0 to 10 that measures how much you have refused, and how sharp the refusing has made you. The Unfed say the other peoples have many Wants and they have only one, and it is the size of everything. Start play with Want 3.

**Earning Want.**
- **Refuse an offer** of real value (food, drink, shelter, rest, a gift, a kindness) in a scene where accepting would clearly help you: +1 Want. Once per scene. It must cost you something; the GM may ask for a Caul + Resolve roll (Routine 10) if the offer is sincere and you are Hollow or worse.
- **Refuse Orrum.** Pass an Invitation check against a laid table, a "Come in," a place card, or a Steward's offer: +2 Want (+3 on a Grace).
- **End a day hungry.** A day on half a ration or less: +1 Want. A day with nothing at all: +2 Want (and a Pang, below).
- **Say the Decline at a deathbed** (a companion's, or a Seated guest's): +1 Want, and you gain 1 Fray.

**Spending Want.**
- **The Second No** (1 Want): reroll one die of a Grace Roll you just made. Keep the new result, even if it is worse.
- **The Gnaw** (2 Want): add a d6, the *Empty Tooth*, to any roll. This is the Unfed's answer to Partaking. It adds no Hunger. If the Empty Tooth shows a 1, you gain 1 Pang: your body paid.
- **Nothing to Hold** (2 Want): add +4 to resist any godsign effect aimed at you: a Closer's touch, a Calling, a Sayer's word, a blink-dream, a Fatlands meat-lure, a Cradlewrack door. Your blood is empty; there is nothing in you for the god to hold on to.
- **Clean Through** (5 Want): ignore a godsign effect aimed at you entirely, once, as though it had never been made. Witnesses who carry godsign make a Dread 2 check: something in their blood flinched.
- **Steady** (1 Want): add +2 to any Dread check about hunger, food, tables, or the Seated.

**At Want 10: Hollow.** You are perfectly empty, and Orrum notices. That night, a table is laid for you within sight of wherever you sleep, with your name on the card. You must make an Invitation check each hour until dawn. Want cannot rise above 10. Spend it down.

### Invitation Checks

When Orrum offers (a laid table, a voice at a door, a smell of bread on the wind, a place card, a chair pulled out), roll **Caul + Resolve** against the offer's strength.

| Offer | Difficulty |
| Smell of baking on the wind | Routine 10 |
| A laid table, seen from a road | Hard 14 |
| A voice saying "Come in" | Grim 18 |
| A place card with your name in a loved hand | Grim 18 |
| A chair pulled out, within reach; Tablenight | Dire 22 |

Add your Pangs to the Difficulty: the hungrier you are, the better it smells. On a success, you refuse; gain Want. On a failure, take one step toward the table and roll again next round at +2 Difficulty; companions may hold you (an opposed Gut + Brawl) or speak the Decline for you (their Tongue + Persuade as a bonus die, once). On a Lack, you sit. On double 1s, you thank the chair.

**The Seating.** A PC who sits is Seated, and is lost: they become a guest, smiling, eating air, and will waste for decades at the table. The player may play out the First Course for a scene: the food is real to them, the best they have ever eaten, and they will say so. They can be dragged away only to die. They can never be rescued. Lent Ashby got up; the GM may decide that one other person in history might. It will not be cheap, and Welcome Ardent will want something.

### Pangs: Starvation

Every character in the Fast tracks **Pangs**, from 0 to 10.

- **A ration** is a day's adequate food (about 1 lack's worth). A **Pantry measure** is half a ration.
- A day on a full ration: −1 Pang. A day of real plenty (two rations, rest, warmth): −2 Pangs, but an Unfed must succeed at a Gut + Endure roll (Hard 14) or lose 1d6 Flesh to cramps, sweats, and the heart's stutter, *refeeding sickness*.
- A day on a Pantry measure: no change. Unfed live here.
- A day on less than a Pantry measure: +1 Pang.
- A day with nothing: +2 Pangs. (Non-Unfed: +2, and +1 more after the third consecutive day.)

| Pangs | State | Effects |
| 0–2 | Peckish | None for Unfed. Others: −1 Caul + Resolve vs food. |
| 3–4 | Hollow | −1 to Gut and Lung rolls. Smells of baking everywhere. |
| 5–6 | Gaunt | −2 Gut and Lung; −2 Flesh max; you dream of meals. |
| 7–8 | Wasting | −3 all Hand, Gut, Lung rolls; −4 Flesh max; +1 Fray daily. |
| 9 | Brink | Each dawn, Gut + Endure (Grim 18) or suffer Ruin. |
| 10 | Starved | You die, unless something feeds you in the night. |

The Unfed feel it differently. At Hollow the cold gets in, and the fingers go white and dead; at Gaunt the hair comes away in the comb and the gums bleed when you bite; at Wasting the heart thuds slow and heavy, the ankles swell, sores open on the hips where you lie, and the mind goes sharp and strange and full of food: you recite recipes you have never been taught. At Brink you stop feeling hungry. That is the worst sign, and every Unfed knows it.

### Playing the Hunger

Pangs are a number on a sheet, and they should never feel like one. The Unfed live at Peckish and Hollow their whole lives, and a good Unfed player plays the hunger as a constant low weather inside the character: something that shapes every sentence without being mentioned in most of them.

- **The body.** Your character is cold, always, and their fingers go white and dead in the wind. They bruise when someone grips their arm. They feel their own heartbeat slow and heavy when they lie down, and they listen to it. They stand to eat, facing a wall. They chew a mouthful forty times because it makes it last. They pick crumbs from their coat at the end of a day and eat them, and are ashamed, and do it anyway.
- **The mind.** At Hollow and below, the mind fills with food. Not cravings, exactly; the Unfed are trained out of crave. Arithmetic. How many oats in a measure. How many measures to the Rim. How many days at this rate. Recipes, from nowhere, in a grandmother's voice. Unfed PCs should be the ones in the party who know exactly what everyone has left to eat, to the mouthful, and say so.
- **The pride.** Hunger is the one thing an Unfed has that no Godeater can buy, and they are proud of it the way a soldier is proud of a scar. Let your Unfed be insufferable about it. Let them watch a Fatlands companion eat with a kind of pitying fascination. Let them refuse the offered hand up a step three times, and take it on the fourth, and say nothing, and remember.
- **The cracks.** Every Unfed PC should have one food they cannot stop thinking about: a smell from a Rim inn window at eight years old, a sugared almond a poacher once held to their lips, a plum on a sill. Write it on the sheet. The GM will use it. It will be on the plate.

### When the Unfed Eat a God

An Unfed who eats the god-meat of another land (Fatlands crop or meat, Brinehollow brine, anything grown in a Godeater soil long enough to carry its god) has done what Tobias Wether and Scour did, and it cannot be undone.

- **They gain that land's godsign.** They become **Cut 1** of that land's line, at **Regrowth 1** (the Taste), and gain the Tooth, a Hunger track, and that land's Gifts, Wants, and Sop as their Regrowth rises.
- **They lose the Refusal.** Want drops to 0 and can never again be earned. Orrum never again lays a place for them. That sounds like freedom. It is felt as being struck off a list.
- **The Empty Room** (something worse). An Unfed body has no ancestral habit of the god, no six centuries of bloodline to dilute and slow it. The god moves into them as into an empty, well-kept house, and makes itself comfortable. Their Tooth bites back on a 1 or a 2. Each time their Hunger empties, their Regrowth rises by 2, not 1. And the meat in them is looking for a god to belong to: within a season they will hear its voice, in the voice of someone it was eaten with.
- **A second land's meat.** If a Returned eats the god-meat of a second land, roll on the Mangling table at once: two gods are fighting over the house.

The Unfed call them the Returned, never to their faces. Most hide it. Some go to the Lean House. Some go back over the Rim and eat, and eat, and are not seen again.

### What You Carry

Every Unfed PC starts with a ration-cloth, a water flask, a wax tin, a small household axe, three days' Pantry measures, a coat that is not warm enough, and a name chosen for refusal. They have no money worth counting: 2d6 lacks.

### Background Concepts

- **Measure-Bearer.** You carried the list to the hamlets, and you know whose names were missing. You have never forgiven yourself for not looking at it first.
- **Tablebreaker's Rope-Holder.** You have pulled your Breaker back from a chair a dozen times. Last week you didn't pull fast enough.
- **Poacher's Escapee.** You were taken over the Rim, fed for a month, and broke loose before the surgeon came. You came home plump and ashamed, and nobody will stand next to you on Tablenight.
- **Kin of a Portion.** Your great-grandmother walked out to the stones in 559 and has written to your family every Lack since. You were raised never to say the word. You have her card, and you have read it.
- **Notched Door.** You grew up in a house with five notches in the frame, and the last is at the height of your sister's heart. You do not visit the verge. You stand at the edge of town some evenings, and your neighbours fetch you in.
- **Returned Knife.** You were a Reckoner who ate to keep a cover, as Scour did. The god in you is a season old. You have not told your band. You carry your own food on every raid, and refuse it, and the god in you laughs.
- **Lean House Survivor.** You were omitted, and walked to the Lean House, and on the twenty-second day your name was read again (a clerk's error, a forged chit, a kindness nobody owns). You came back with the borrowed cheeks and a recipe you cannot stop saying.
- **Pin-Setter.** You set the pins on the Great Map. You know where the walkers are, and the Seated, and the god. You have noticed what the newest pins are making, and nobody has asked you.
- **Poacher's Get.** You were born on the Rim to a captive who was fed on the road and kept in a warm room with no west windows, and you were smuggled home to the Fast as an infant by a Crumb-Runner who will not say how. You are Unfed by blood. You were fed on Fatlands milk for your first year. You do not know what that means yet.

### Carrying Your Own in a Mixed Party

Most parties that cross the Fast include Godeaters, and most Unfed PCs will spend more of their lives on the Rim than in Wanting. A few customs make an Unfed easy to play alongside the others, and hard to play lightly.

- **Never feed, never be fed.** An Unfed will not take food from a companion's hand, and will not give their own. They may set food down, and walk away, and let a companion pick it up: food set down is not offered, it is only there. Unfed PCs who have spent long on the Rim learn to do this so naturally that their companions do not notice they are being fed.
- **Count the chairs.** At any inn, any hall, any feast, the Unfed counts the places before sitting, and gets up if there is one too many. In 640 and after, on the Rim Road, they are getting up more and more often.
- **The question.** An Unfed will, early, ask after the state of a companion's blood, kindly, as a courtesy. A Godeater who answers honestly gains an Unfed friend for life, and that friend will keep the count of their Regrowth more carefully than the Cutwrights do, and will say so.
- **Godless blood.** Unfed are the one people on the Table that godsign cannot grip, and parties quickly learn to put them between the danger and everyone else: in front of a Closer, at the edge of a Hush, in the doorway of a Sayer. The Unfed know this and are proud of it. They also know what they are worth to a poacher, and that every Godeater in the party, every one, is a body that could be bought with a pair of Unfed kidneys. Some Unfed PCs never quite stop doing that sum.

## Horrors of the Fast

[box: For the GM]
The Fast is the quietest chapter of the book. Nothing attacks. Everything offers. Run it slowly: golden afternoons, empty roads, the smell. Let the players be hungry. Track Pangs openly. Describe food constantly, lovingly, and never let them eat it. The horror of the Fast is that the god is good, the food is real, and the only thing between your players and perfect contentment is a rule their grandparents made up while starving to death. Let them doubt it.
[/box]
