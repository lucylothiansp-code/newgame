# Things That Eat and Are Eaten

> Everything on the Table is either sitting at it or lying on it. Learn which you are before the knives come out.
>> — Tablebreaker saying, Wanting

Weather: salt rain from the second bell, fog off the Uncovered from the third. Pressure rising. Two of my hookmen bled from the ears before midnight and were sent to bail.
At the fourth bell the count came up short in Ropewalk Row. Widow Carse called her neighbour's name through the wall three times and got no answer, and then, she says, got an answer from the wrong side, from the floor. We broke the door. Bed empty, anchor-rope sawn through from underneath. Not cut. Sawn, as if by something with a very small and patient mouth. The cellar was full to the fourth stair with water that had no business being there at that hour and smelled of a harbour I have not smelled since I was a girl. Weed on the stairs, still moving. I wrote the time.
We took the shore road at the run. Found the Called at the end of the old breakwater, which ends now in nine miles of dry seabed, and which she was walking down as if the water were still there to receive her. Woman of forty, nightdress, barefoot, feet cut to ribbons on the shell and not caring. Hookman Dabb put the boathook through the back of her collar as taught and she did not fall. She kept walking and dragged him. A woman of nine stone dragged a man of fourteen stone forty yards on his knees across the shingle. When I got the second rope on her she turned her head and looked at me, and I am obliged to record that her face was perfectly calm and she was weeping out of her nose. Brine. A good deal of it.
We netted her and sat on her. She bailed in the net, unasked, a bucket and a half, and some of what came up was alive and went scuttling under the planking. Dabb stamped on two. He says the third had a face. Dabb has been on the Watch four years and I do not think he is a man for faces, so I have written it down.
At the fifth bell, while we were roping her for the cart, the Uncovered sang. I have heard it before. Everyone on the Watch has, and none of us mention it at muster. It comes up out of the dark where the sea used to be, low, a sound like a hull grinding on a bar, and it has a tune if you let it. Hookman Persey let it. He put his hook down very carefully, the way you put down something you mean to come back for, and he started walking west. We got the rope on him at the tideline and he fought us harder than the woman had. He was crying and saying he was sorry, he was so sorry, they had been waiting such a long time.
On the walk back we passed something standing upright in the mud about a furlong out. In the lamp it looked like a wreck's keel with the ribs still on. It was not a keel. It had more ribs than a keel and they were opening and closing. We did not stop. I note for the Clerk that it was nearer the pier at the sixth bell than at the fifth, and that the Watch's standing orders do not cover it, and request that they be made to.
Called recovered: two, one of them my own man. Called lost: none tonight. Injuries: Dabb, bitten on the ankle by something under the planks, wound weeping salt, he swears it is nothing. The Table is not short of monsters. It is short of anything else. Every land ate a god, and every land is now host to the slow return of what it swallowed, and that return does not confine itself to the bloodlines of the nobility. It gets into the soil, the weather, the drowned, the sleeping, the unborn and the overheard. It makes things. Some of those things were people a week ago. Some were never people at all, and some are people still, and are the worst of the lot.

This chapter is the GM's larder. It gives rules for running adversaries in the terms of the Grace Roll, then walks the seven lands one by one, presenting the horrors native to each with full statistics, a witness's word on each, and the long, unpleasant detail that makes them worth meeting: what they smell like, what they sound like in the dark, and what they leave behind for the family to find. After the lands come the human adversaries, who outnumber the monsters and do more harm, among them the cult-shepherds and the patient questioners whose work leaves no mark a Seamer can stitch. Then come the rules for the god-ridden, the people whose god has taken the reins for an hour or a year; the Seated Gods, the endings of campaigns, and a frank discussion of why nobody at the table should expect to win against one; a chapter of lairs; and, at the last, random encounters for every land and for the Rim Road between them.

## Running Adversaries

### Threat and the Quick Line

Most adversaries do not need a full stat block. A Netwatch hookman, a field of toothed wheat or a mob of the Forsworn can be run from a single **quick line**, which gives everything needed at the table: **Threat**, **Flesh**, **Guard**, **Attack**, **Armor** and **Dread**. Threat is a flat bonus the adversary adds to every 2d10 roll that is not an attack: a Search to find a hidden player character, a Stealth to creep up on the camp, a Resolve against a Hush-walker's silence. Threat 1 is a frightened farmhand. Threat 3 is a trained professional. Threat 5 is the best in the land at their work. Threat 7 and above belongs to things that are no longer working at anything human.

Attack is the full bonus added to 2d10 against the target's Guard. Damage follows the core rules: the listed weapon damage, plus 2 for each Helping (every full 4 points the roll beats Guard), minus the target's Armor. Doubles on an adversary's roll produce a Grace or a Lack exactly as for player characters, and the GM should describe both with relish. A Netwatch hook that rolls a Grace has hooked a player character's collar and is dragging them. A Seated guest that rolls a Lack has knocked over the salt, and the whole table turns to look at who did it.

### Full Stat Blocks

Important adversaries, monsters with Gifts and anything with a name receive a full stat block. These use the same six attributes as player characters (Hand, Gut, Lung, Eye, Tongue, Caul) and the same skills. Humans rarely exceed 5 in an attribute. Monsters, the Laden, the Tenanted and the deep Regrowth may run to 6, 8 or 10, and a Seated fragment of a god ignores the scale entirely. Flesh, Guard and Attack are calculated by the core formulas where the creature is anything like a body; where it is not (a field, a tide, an echo), the block says so and gives what is needed instead.

### Dread Ratings

Every adversary carries a **Dread** rating from 0 to 5. The first time in a scene that a character perceives the thing clearly, they make a Dread check: Caul + Resolve against the difficulty the rating sets. Failure adds Fray equal to the rating; success adds 1; a Grace adds nothing. Many entries specify what, exactly, sets off the check, since a Tenanted judge in a courtroom is Dread 0 until her lower voice finishes her sentence before her upper one does.

| Dread | Difficulty | Typical horror |
| 0 | none | An armed man, a thin dog, an honest threat |
| 1 | Routine 10 | A Kept beggar, a toothed ear of wheat, a corpse |
| 2 | Hard 14 | An Unstitched Kept, a Forsworn's opened ribs, a blink-murder |
| 3 | Grim 18 | The Sump-thing rising, a Tenanted loved one, a table set for you |
| 4 | Dire 22 | A Laden hill speaking, a crowning opening, a god's attention |
| 5 | Impossible 26 | A Seating, the Host's face, any Seated god |

Some horrors carry a **rising Dread**, written as "Dread 2/4". The first number applies on sight; the second applies when the thing does what it does (a Hush-walker's silence reaching the character, a Seeming turning to look at the one who imagined it). Others carry **Contagion**: anyone who sees a character Break in the creature's presence must make the Dread check again, and on failure takes on the same Derangement the Breaking character did. The Table's madness travels best in crowds.

[pull] You don't get used to it. You get used to the smell, and you mistake the one for the other. | — a Seamer's apprentice, Lastgate

### Godsign-Powered Creatures

Anything with the god in it can **Partake**. Creatures with a Cut are listed with it, and with a Hunger track exactly like a player character's. Before any roll, the GM may Partake for the creature, adding one Tooth (a d6) or, for Cut 3+, two Teeth. Each Partake adds 1 Hunger, and every Tooth that shows a 1 adds 1 more. When a creature's Hunger reaches 10, it empties and the creature's **Regrowth rises by one**. For a monster this is not bookkeeping. It is escalation: the Sump-thing pulls three more drowned bodies into itself and gains Flesh, the Laden hill's mouth opens wider, the Waker gone mad stops blinking. Each entry describes what a Regrowth rise does to that creature. Players learn quickly that a monster which gambles on its Teeth is a monster that gets worse, and that sometimes the best tactic is to make it Partake.

Godsign creatures also have **Sops**. A creature that performs its Sop removes 1d6 Hunger, exactly as a player character does, and the Sop is always something the players would rather it did not do: close a child's eyes, eat the farmhand, take one more of the Called. Interrupting a Sop in progress is often the only way to break a creature's momentum.

### Monster Special Abilities

The following abilities recur through the chapter. They are written in the core rules' terms and can be lifted onto any creature the GM builds.

- **Unending.** The creature does not die from Ruin. At 0 Flesh it is Undone: it stops acting for 1d6 rounds, then recovers 1d6 Flesh and resumes, unless it has been dismembered, sunk, Closed, or moved out of Sallowreach. Every Kept thing has this ability within Sallowreach's borders.
- **Swarm.** The creature is many. Blades and Brawl attacks deal only 1 damage regardless of Helpings; fire, smoke, flood and area effects deal double. A swarm reduced below half Flesh disperses and re-forms elsewhere next scene.
- **Hooking / Grapple (X).** On a hit with one or more Helpings, the target is held. Escaping is a Gut + Athletics or Lung + Athletics roll against difficulty X. A held target cannot move and suffers −2 to all rolls.
- **Engulf.** Against a held target, the creature's next hit pulls the victim inside it. Engulfed characters take the creature's damage automatically each round, ignoring Armor, and make a Dread check each round they remain.
- **Fray Aura (N).** At the start of each round within the creature's reach, every character must pass a Caul + Resolve roll against difficulty N or take 1 Fray. This is in addition to the first sight Dread check.
- **Pull (N).** The creature calls. Characters with the relevant Cut must roll Caul + Resolve against difficulty N each round or spend their action moving toward it. Resisting is not free: every success adds 1 Hunger, because the god in the blood wants to go.
- **Rooted.** The creature cannot move, but cannot be pushed, knocked down or held. It usually has enormous Flesh and a reach measured in fields.
- **Tide (N).** The creature's influence rises and falls. On the GM's turn, roll a d10: on N or less it acts twice this round. Things in Brinehollow and the Vigil have this.
- **Binding.** The creature's words count. When it speaks a sentence about a character and that character fails a Tongue + Clause roll (difficulty set in the entry), the sentence becomes physically true. Found only in Oathen and in very bad places elsewhere.
- **Hosting.** The creature offers. Any character who accepts anything it offers (food, a seat, shelter, "Come in") must roll Caul + Resolve against the listed difficulty or stay. Refusing three times grants +2 to the roll; thanking it imposes −4.
- **Known Voice (N).** The creature speaks in the voice of someone the listener loves, living or dead. Each time it does, the listener rolls Caul + Resolve against N or takes one step toward it, and a character who has sworn an Oath on the Dead in that person's name rolls at −2.
- **Leftovers.** Destroying the creature does not end it entirely. Some part (a hand, a face, a wick, a word) goes on, and the entry says what it does and who will pay to have it.
- **Riding.** The creature is a god wearing a person. It uses the rules for the god-ridden (see *The God-Ridden*, later in this chapter), and the person inside it can sometimes be called back by name.
- **Armor (type).** Some armor is not leather. Waxed flesh, bone lattice, compressed water and layered fat all count. Where an armor ignores a damage type (fat ignores blunt, water ignores blades), the entry says.
- **Helping Effects.** Many creatures list what their Helpings do in place of or alongside extra damage. A Flyfront that scores two Helpings does not deal +4 damage. It gets into the mouth.
- **Grace and Lack Effects.** Some entries list special results when the creature's own roll comes up doubles, or when a character's roll against it does. A character who rolls a Lack against a Hush-walker hears, for one awful second, nothing at all.

### Putting Things Down

Killing is not the same in every land. Outside Sallowreach, a creature at 0 Flesh suffers Ruin and further damage kills it, as for any character. Inside Sallowreach nothing dies, and the only ways to finish anything are a Hand-line's Closing touch, a Hush, or carrying the thing across the border, where it drops at once. Many creatures in this chapter have a **Weakness** or an **End** entry, which describes the one way the people of that land have found to stop them. Some of those Ends are worse than the monster. Some are not ends at all, only delays, and the entry will say so.

Every creature in this chapter is built to be survivable at first sight. The Dread check, not the attack roll, is where most of them do their damage. Use that. Let the players see the Sump bubble in patterns for a full session before anything comes up.
The best monster fights on the Table end without a death and with a cost: a player character's Hunger maxed out to win a single roll, a companion who Broke and is now certain their sister has been replaced, a field that now knows their name. Track those costs and bring them back.
When a creature's Hunger fills and its Regrowth rises mid-fight, describe the change before you describe its next action. The moment of transformation is the scene.
Smell first, sound second, sight last. The players should know what is in the cellar a full minute before the lamp shows them.

## Sallowreach: What Will Not Finish

[sigil: sallowreach]

Every monster in the north began as a person or a thing that tried to end and was refused. They are not malicious, mostly. They are in pain, and they have been in pain for a very long time, and pain that long becomes a kind of weather. Remember that within Sallowreach all of these have **Unending** unless noted, and that nothing they do to a victim is ever finished either: the people they hurt are still somewhere, still feeling it, and some of them can be found.

[pull] May you finish. And if you can't, may you at least be shelved somewhere dry. | — Lastgate leave-taking among the poor

### The Sump-Thing

Weighted sacks go into the Sump by the hundred each year: the Kept too far gone for stitching, the unclaimed, the inconvenient. The sacks rot. The weights do not. Over decades, in the black peat-water forty feet down, the bodies find each other. Rotting flesh that cannot finish rotting goes soft as dough, and soft things press together under the fen's weight until there is no telling where one grandfather ends and the next begins. A Sump-thing is what happens when a few hundred of them agree to come up.

It surfaces first as bubbles in patterns: rings, then spirals, then the shape of a hand spread flat under the water. Then the smell, which is not the sweet Sallowreach rot but something older, sulphurous and vinegary, a pickle-jar opened after a century, with a bottom note of wet wool and the copper of old coins. Then the mound breaks the surface, a slick dark heap twenty feet across, hair and sackcloth and peat-tanned leather stretched over a lattice of knitted bone, and out of it, everywhere, faces. Pressed flat. Mouths working. Some still have the twine of their labels around their necks, the ink long run, a name and a date of shelving. The sound it makes is a choir of drowned people trying to say their names through a lungful of fen, which comes out as a long wet gargle with words in it. Under the gargle, if one listens, is a smaller sound, like teeth chattering in the cold, which is exactly what it is.

It wants to be *finished*, and it has learned that it cannot be, so it has settled for company. It drags the living down. Victims are not eaten. They are added. The process takes a week in the dark, and boatmen who have dragged the Sump for a lost brother on the third day have brought up something that was half him and half the mound, joined at the hip and the cheek, still breathing through both mouths. A week later a new face surfaces in the mound, and it is still screaming, and it will scream for centuries. Families who lose someone to a Sump-thing sometimes row out to listen for them, and sometimes hear.

Tactics: it rises under boats and walkways, grapples with dozens of arms, and sinks. It is slow on land but can drag itself up a bank like a slug of wet meat, leaving a furrow of black slime that smokes faintly in the cold. It does not flee. It has nowhere else to be.

### Hush-Walkers

Edda Thrush walked out of Dunmere Hush. She was the first. She is not the last. A Hush-walker is a person who entered a Hush and did not finish, or a Kept body from inside a Hush that was somehow refused even there and stood up. They come out of the silence carrying it with them. A Hush-walker has no smell at all, which in Sallowreach is the most alarming thing about it: one notices a gap in the stink of the world, a person-shaped absence of rot, before one notices the person. Their skin is clean and pale and perfectly still, unrotted, every fly that lands on them dropping dead and unmoving. Their clothes do not stir in the wind. Their hair does not lift. They walk slowly. Their feet make no noise on boards. Their mouths move.

Within ten paces of one, sound stops. Not quiet: stopped. A scream comes out as breath. A drawn blade makes no ring. A heartbeat cannot be heard in one's own ears, and this is what breaks people: the body insists the heart has stopped. Swallowing makes no sound, and people who have stood in the Silence report that they kept swallowing, over and over, to be sure the throat still worked. In that silence something is listening, the way Edda Thrush insists something listens through her. Victims who stay in the silence too long simply end. They sit down, close their eyes, and are properly, completely dead, with no rot afterward and no flies, and their families tend to envy them. They are found sitting upright in the road, faces calm, mouths a little open, as if they had heard the end of a song. Hush-walkers wander toward Hand-line estates. Nobody is sure if they are drawn there or sent.

[pull] My Da went into the quiet after her. He's sitting by the Glovehall road with his eyes shut and his face clean. Six weeks now. Not a fly on him. I go and sit with him. It's the best he's ever looked. | — a reed-cutter's daughter, to the Heirs

### The Flyfront

Flies are Sallowreach's weather, and a Black Noon is its storm. A Flyfront is a mass of carrion-flies thick enough to put out lamps, rolling across the fen at walking pace with a sound like frying fat. It is not a creature in the ordinary sense, but it behaves as one: it hunts. Everything in Sallowreach rots and never finishes, so the flies never finish breeding, and the oldest fronts are centuries old, and have a direction. They come down on the living because the living are warm and wet and still have their soft parts. Within a minute a person is black with them, flies in the eyes, the ears, the nostrils, the throat, a thick crawling paste that has to be scraped off with a knife. The taste is the worst of it, survivors say: bitter, buttery, and moving. The Kept caught in a front come out the other side with their faces gone to lace, lips and eyelids eaten to a fringe, the teeth showing in a permanent grin. The living come out choking up maggots for a week, and some of them, in the second week, find eggs hatching in the corners of their eyes. Justice Ever Lathe's colony is said to have come from a front, which raises questions about what fronts want.

[pull] The Black Noon came over the Sinks at the fourth bell and when it lifted my neighbour was sitting on his step with his hands in his lap, and the flies had taken his face off so neatly you could see where he'd been smiling. | — a lamp-trimmer of the Sinks

### The Unstitched

The Seamers' Guild holds Sallowreach together with gut-thread, wire, lacquer and resin. When a Kept body's stitching fails all at once, from neglect or a lapsed fee or a Seamers' slowdown, what results is an Unstitched: a person who has come apart along every seam, held together only by habit and old tendon. They lurch. Their arms swing loose from shoulders that are only hooked on. Their jaws hang. Their bellies, sewn shut a dozen times, open like purses with every step and have to be held closed with both hands, so their arms are never free, so they use their teeth. The old thread trails from them in grey loops, and the wire, rusted, rings faintly against their bones as they walk, which is how the poor of the Sinks know one is coming up the stair. They smell of everything Sallowreach smells of, all at once, concentrated: sweet rot, sour lacquer, vinegar gone off, the rancid tallow the very poor use instead of resin. Most Unstitched are not violent, only desperate: they come at the living begging for thread, for a needle, for someone to hold their face on while they get the wire through. Some, after enough decades of it, have stopped asking. Marrow Jack's victims are often Unstitched, and come looking for their parts.

### The Undone Closer

The Hand-lines sell death by touch, and the touch grows. Osric Vane in his lead mittens is the famous case. An Undone Closer is the case nobody contained: a Hand-line noble deep in the Course whose mittens came off and who went out into the fen. The hands are the thing. Grey, cold, damp like the underside of a stone, the fingers lengthened to a span and a half and jointed nine and ten times, so that they fold and unfold like a spider settling. The nails are long and yellow and clean. The hands move independently of the face above them, which is often weeping, and which has usually not eaten in weeks, because the hands will not hold a spoon without closing what is in it. Everything the hands touch finishes: a candle gutters and will not relight, a conversation stops mid-word and cannot be restarted, a person sighs and slumps and is done. The Closer cannot stop touching. It is the Want, gone total. Victims are found laid out tidily, eyes shut, hands folded, and the village around them strangely silent, birdless, every door closed, every book shut, every pot lidded, every shutter latched. In one hamlet near Glovehall a Closer passed in the night and in the morning even the well had a lid on it that nobody had made. Hunting one is the Mortuary Court's most secret duty, because every Undone Closer is a cousin.

### The Tallow-Saint

Wick Tallow renders the unclaimed into candles that feel themselves burn. A Tallow-Saint is a candle that did not want to be one. ### New Horror: The Whispering Aisle

The Lofts hold the Set-Aside: nine storeys of shelving, forty thousand Kept too far gone to stand, wrapped, labeled, shelved, and still aware. The whispering is audible from the street. Most of it is harmless, the same murmur one hears in any ward: complaints, prayers, the names of grandchildren, the endless recitation of the last book someone read aloud to them. A Whispering Aisle is what an aisle becomes when it has not been visited in a generation. Nobody comes on feast days. Nobody reads. The shelved have only each other, and over the decades their whispering has fallen into step, the way the breathing of the Counted falls into step in the Vigil, until two hundred wrapped mouths are saying one thing at one time.

The air in such an aisle is thick, close and sweet, linen gone brown with seepage, the cedar-dust the Wardens scatter long since settled into a crust. The shrouds have stuck to the shelves. Underneath them the bodies have shifted, sagged and spread, so that a shelf is no longer a row of separate parcels but one long lumpen bolt of stained cloth with heads in it. As a visitor walks the aisle, the heads turn. The whisper rises. It is, nearly always, a request: *read to us*. It is said very sweetly. A visitor who stops to listen begins to hear their own name in it, and then the ends of their own sentences before they have spoken them, and then the thing they have never told anyone. The wrapped hands come through the linen then, grey and soft as wet bread, and take hold of sleeves and hair and ankles, very gently, the way the old take the hand of someone who has come to visit, and do not let go.

Bettany Shroud keeps a list of the aisles that have begun to whisper in unison. Lately they all whisper the same word, and it is not *read*.

### New Horror: The Sewn Gentleman

Marrow Jack takes limbs from the Kept while they shelve or sleep and sells them to back-alley seamers. The buyers are rarely poor. A Sewn Gentleman is what that trade looks like at the top of the market: an Unvacated elder, rich enough to buy and old enough to have rotted, who has replaced himself part by part over two or three centuries until almost nothing of him is his own. A young sailor's forearms. A Laundry-girl's hands, small and chapped. A ploughman's back, grafted in a single great flap and wired at the shoulders. A singer's throat. Eyes, two of them, not quite the same colour, behind tinted glass. He dresses beautifully, in high collars that hide the joins. He smells of cedar, pomade and the cloves the seamers pack into the seams, and beneath that, faintly and always, of other people's sweat.

The parts remember. That is the horror and the hunt. Marrow Jack's victims wake up able to feel what their missing hands are being used for, and the Sewn Gentleman's original donors, Kept and shelved and Unstitched across the north, feel all of him: his dinners, his correspondence, his razor, his nights. ### New Horror: The Hive-Kept

Justice Ever Lathe is a judge rotted down to bone and a resident colony, and nobody is sure any longer which of them is deciding. He is famous. The Hive-Kept are the ones nobody has noticed. When a Flyfront passes over the Sinks, it leaves behind more than lace-faced dead. It leaves eggs, in the soft places of Kept bodies that were already far gone, and over the years the colony that hatches grows into the hollow where the person used to keep themselves. One day the person is a slow old Kept fishwife who sits in the doorway. Then for a season she is quieter. Then she stands up and goes about her business more briskly than she has in a century, and her business is no longer quite hers.

A Hive-Kept is recognizable by the sound before anything else: a thick low hum from inside the chest, steady as a hearth-cat's purr, that rises when it is angry. Its skin is drum-tight and faintly translucent over the swarm, and in good light one can see the shadows moving under it. Flies come and go from its nose, its ears and the corners of its eyes, unhurried, like bees from a hive. It speaks in the buzzing, very fluently, and the words are arranged by many small minds voting. It is clever, cooperative and patient, and it wants more bodies: it moves its family into them as they rot, one by one, and the street it lives on grows quieter and more orderly every year. The worst of it, the Seamers say, is how pleased the neighbours are.

## The Fatlands: The Crop That Stands Up

[sigil: fatlands]

In the Fatlands the monsters are agricultural. Nothing there is anything but crop, and that includes what eats the crop. Fatlands horrors are warm, wet, slow, enormous and hungry, and the smell that precedes most of them is gravy. What they leave behind is rarely a body. It is a better yield.

### The Laden Hill with a Mouth

Many of the best fields in the south are somebody's great-grandmother. Most Seated Laden are quiet hills, warm to the touch, generous with barley. Some keep a mouth. Old Mother Rind is benign, more or less. Not all are. A hungry hill is a low green mound a hundred paces across, its turf rising and falling with slow breath, and in its side, at about the height of a cart, a mouth: lips of soft red loam, a tongue the size of a ploughshare, and teeth that were once teeth and are now stones, old roots, and the skulls of what it has eaten, pushed out through the gums like new growth. Its breath is hot and smells of ripe silage and stomach, and on cold mornings it steams like a midden. Its gullet can be heard working from the lane, a slow wet peristalsis under the turf like a cow chewing cud in the next field. When its family stops coming, it calls out to passersby by the names of grandchildren. The ground around it is soft for a long way. The roots under that ground move. ### The Toothed Field

Wheat with teeth in the ear has been a superior strain since 204 A.G., by ruling of the Board. A toothed field in its late Plenty is tall as a man, gold and red, and every ear is a small jaw of white milk-teeth set in a husk that clicks open and shut in the wind. On a still day it is quiet. On a windy one, a toothed field sounds like a crowd chewing. Harvesters wear leather to the shoulder. Anyone who falls in a toothed field in late Plenty is in serious trouble: the ears turn toward warmth like flowers to the sun, and every ear takes a nip, and there are millions of ears. Victims come out lacework, bleeding from a thousand pinpricks, skin hanging in fine pink threads, and the wheat they fell in is the best of the crop that year, heavy and pink in the grain. The loaves from it are said to taste faintly of the person. Granny Cracknel says the teeth in a field that has fed well are adult teeth.

### Two-Legged Cattle

### Rootkin

Tansy Lard dug up a root with her mother's birthmark on it. She was not the first. ### The Reaping

The Reaping is not a monster. That is the Board's official position, and it is technically correct: no single thing takes the Fatlands' missing. The land does. On dusk in Carving, out in the far fields, the soil softens. The furrows deepen, slowly, so slowly a farmhand will not notice until her boots are below the surface. The rows of wheat lean in. The air thickens with the smell of fresh bread and fat. The earth makes a sound, low and continuous, like a stomach in the next room. And then the ground opens, wetly, the way a mouth opens around a spoon, and closes again, and the furrow is smooth, and the crop in that furrow will be very good next year. Sometimes a hand stays above the soil until morning, still clutching a hoe.

### New Horror: The Uneaten

The Fatlands have no graves. The dead are served at the wake, by the family, with the best plates, and to be buried uneaten would be the deepest insult to a life. Sometimes a wake fails. The family is too small, or too poor in appetite, or too grief-struck; a Thin son refuses his portion; a plague makes the meat suspect; the Board's Bailiff comes too late with his funnel. Something is left on the platter overnight. The leftovers are buried, quietly, in a corner of the field, and the family tells nobody. A season later, the Uneaten comes home.

It is what remained of the dead: the joints that were not carved, the ribs that were picked half clean, the head that nobody could bring themselves to serve, drawn back together by the soil into the rough shape of the person and clothed in turf and root-hair and a skin of yellow fat. It walks with a gnawed limp. Its face is half a face, the other half carved down to the cheekbone in neat slices. It smells of a cold kitchen the morning after a feast: congealed dripping, stale gravy, and under it, sweet and terrible, the meat itself. It comes to the family table at the hour of the evening meal, sits down in its old chair, and waits to be finished. It will not leave until every scrap of it is eaten by its own kin. If they refuse, it begins to serve itself to them, and they find they cannot refuse that.

## Brinehollow: What the Sea Left Behind

[sigil: brinehollow]

Brinehollow's horrors are patient, heavy and courteous. They press. Many have **Tide**, and all of them smell of a harbour at low water: weed, salt, rot and cold iron. Their victims are rarely torn. They are found wrung, folded, drowned on dry floors, or not found at all, and the family keeps the bail-bucket by the door for a year in case.

[pull] It's not the sea going out. It's the sea coming in. Into us. You can hear it slosh when the children run. | — a Lowmark bucket-seller

### The Tenanted

A Tenanted is not hostile. That is what makes it so hard. It walks out of the surf weeks after its Calling, heavier, calm, with the same face and the same memories, and someone else looking out. It knows your name, your nickname, the way you take your tea. It is kinder than the person was. Its skin is cool and faintly grey, its hands are always damp, and its breath, if you are close enough, smells of deep water, a clean mineral cold. Its weight is wrong: floorboards creak under a slight Tenanted girl as if under a cart, and a Tenanted man who sits on a bed leaves a dent that holds water. It does not sweat; it weeps salt from the pores in warm rooms, and its pillow is crusted white each morning. When it is angered, which is rare, it does not raise its voice. It presses. The air in the room thickens, ears pop, windows bow, and a man who has crossed a Tenanted judge has been found with his ribs gently folded inward, as if the sea had leaned on him, his eyes pushed a little way out of his face and his mouth full of pink froth.

[fiction]
A Wreckwives' advice-sheet, passed hand to hand on the Mile. "He will be gentle. Let him. Do not ask where he went. Do not wake him by shaking; he does not wake, he surfaces, and you do not want to be leaning over the bed when he does. Count the children every morning, aloud, and make him count them with you, and watch his mouth."
[/fiction]

### Ribwalkers

On the Uncovered, among the leather weed-forests and the wrecks standing upright, there are things with too many ribs. Salvagers call them Ribwalkers. A Ribwalker is a cage of long curved bones, thirty or forty pairs, opening and closing like a slow bellows, mounted on legs that might once have been the ribs of something else. There is no head. There is meat inside the cage, a grey sack that pulses, and there is usually a salvager's boot or two, and buttons, and once a wedding band on a finger bone. They move toward water, any water, at a creaking stalk, with a sound like a ship's timbers working in a swell, and they open their ribs wide over anything wet and alive and close them, and squeeze, and drink. The squeezed come out flat, dry as leather, with their bail-buckets still on their belts and their faces pressed into the shape of a scream that made no sound, because there was no breath left to make it. Some salvagers swear the Ribwalkers are what the Admiralty's fallen sailors become. Hask Tarn will not say.

[pull] Keep your water-barrel a hundred paces from the camp and your bucket on your belt and never, never bail after dark. They smell it. | — Uncovered salvage-rule, first of nine

### The Dowager Below

Lady Ondine Vasht lives in the flooded cellars of the palace in Lowmark, and the cellars have had to be extended twice. She is far into the Course and has not been seen whole in years. What can be seen of her, through the black water by lamplight, is a vast grey shape folded into the vaults, skin like a drowned sail, hair that drifts across the floor of three cellars, and eyes the size of serving platters, dark and wide and wet, which open one at a time in different rooms. The cellars smell of cold stone, bladderwrack and a great animal's breath, and the water is warmer than it should be, blood-warm near the walls where she lies against them. She is still a grandmother. She still has opinions about the prince's marriage. She speaks through the water: the surface of every cistern in the palace trembles with her words. She would like her grandson to come down. She has been asking for years. The servants who go down to feed her return with less of their breath every time, and some do not return. The ones that come up again sometimes come up years later, through a palace drain, pale and swollen and polite, asking whether the Dowager is receiving.

[pull] Her ladyship takes the mutton on Tuesdays and a footman on the first of the month. That is not written down anywhere, and you will not write it down either. | — the palace steward, to a new under-butler

### The Indoor Tide

In every cellar in Brinehollow, water rises twice a day and drains away. An Indoor Tide is when it does not stop. It comes up the stairs at night, black and cold, bringing kelp and silt and small crabs and the smell of an open harbor. It rises through the kitchen, through the parlor, up the bedroom stairs to where the family sleeps anchored, and it does not care that the house is a mile from the shore. Furniture lifts and bumps along the ceiling. The family portraits float face down. It has a current. It pulls toward the west wall. People found after an Indoor Tide are found tidily, laid face down on the west side of rooms, lungs full, hands reaching, fingernails broken against the plaster. Treat it as a hazard: each round the water rises a step, Lung + Athletics rolls to move against the current start at Hard and rise to Dire; anchored characters are safe until it reaches their mouths. Lung-line characters feel the Pull 14 every round. The Tide has Dread 2 as it rises and Dread 3 when someone sees a shape swimming through the drowned parlor that was not there when the water came in.

[pull] The water went down at dawn like it always does. It left the weed on the stairs and the crabs in the sugar and the Tolleys face down by the west wall, all four, holding hands. | — a neighbour, Saltwick

### The Called Procession

The Deepening walks in willingly, in procession, with lanterns. Sometimes the Called do too, without the lanterns: twenty, forty, a hundred people in nightclothes walking west at night in perfect silence, eyes open, bleeding brine from the nose, singing very low the same tune the Uncovered sings. Their bare feet slap the planks in step. Children walk among them holding the hands of strangers. They are not hostile. They will not stop. Anyone who stands in their way is walked over. Anyone with a Lung-line Cut who watches them pass must roll against the Pull. In the morning the shingle beyond the last pier is printed with hundreds of footprints going one way, each filling slowly with water from below.

### New Horror: Coughlings

What comes up in the bail-bucket is mostly water, and sometimes weed, and sometimes, more often every year, something alive. Dr. Isolde Reave found a shell of no known species in a drowned chest. Hookman Dabb stamped on two in the net and swore the third had a face. These are Coughlings: small soft-bodied things the size of a thumb-joint to a fist, brought up from the lungs of Brinehollowers at the turn of the body's tide, pale grey and slick, with too many legs and a little mouth-ring of needle teeth. They scuttle. They nest in the dark under planking, in drains, in the cold ashes of hearths, in the bottoms of buckets left standing, and they find each other. A nest of a few hundred under a pier-house makes a sound at night like rain beginning, and smells of tide-wrack and spoiled milk.

Coughlings want to go back in. They creep up the bed-ropes of the anchored sleeping and into open mouths and noses, one after another, and the sleeper's next bailing is a bloody one. Those who have hosted a nest cough for a month, and sometimes they cough something larger, and sometimes it looks at them. And the faces: the older and larger the Coughling, the more its pale front resembles a face, and the more it resembles the face of the person who coughed it up.

[pull] Mam says leave the bucket face-down at night or the little ones get back in. I asked where they go. She said home. | — Tolly Brack, the Bailer Boy, aged eleven

[quick] Coughling Nest — Threat 2 · Flesh 18 · Guard 13 · Attack +5 (needle-mouths 1, against everyone in reach; on two Helpings a Coughling gets into the mouth: the character is Choked next round and must bail at the next tide with a Gut + Endure 14 roll or lose 3 Flesh) · Armor 0 · Dread 1 / 3 when one has the character's own face · Swarm; Tide 3; fire and fresh water deal double; drawn to a character who has just bailed.

### New Horror: The Cistern-Kept

At the Course, the Brinehollower's body adapts to depth: grey skin, wide dark eyes, a chest that no longer needs to rise. The sufferer is uncomfortable in open air and sleeps in a cistern. Families with money build a private tank; Sennet Gull keeps her husband in one. The poor share. Under the Mile and in the old towns of the coast there are public cisterns, vaulted, dripping, black, where the Course-struck who have no family go down to lie in the cold and do not come up. They are the Cistern-Kept. Over the years they grow. Their eyes widen until there is no white. Their skin goes slack and pleated, like a drowned sail. Their fingers web. They lie in the dark at the bottom, perfectly still, a dozen to a cistern, and they listen to the buckets coming down on the rope.

When a bucket comes down they take hold of it. Most of the time they give it back, full, with a polite tug. Some nights they do not, and the drawer leans over the edge to see what has caught. Bodies taken from a public cistern come up weeks later through the drain-grates of the street, white and puffed, with their ribs folded in and their fingers webbed, and the street drinks the water anyway, because there is nowhere else to get it.

## The Vigil: What Happens When You Close Your Eyes

[sigil: vigil]

The Vigil's horrors arrive in blinks. Many of them happen, or are seen, in the second between one waking moment and the next. All of them are worse for an exhausted character: a character who has not taken rouse in the last hour suffers −2 on Dread checks in this section. And in a city with no darkness, there is nowhere for them to hide and no need for them to: everything here happens under ten thousand lamps, in plain view, in front of witnesses who are not sure whether they saw it.

[pull] Pinch me. No, harder. Harder. Thank you. I love you. | — the whole of a Vigil courtship, overheard on the Nodding Stair

### The Blink-Killer

Inspector Hale Grimmer has forty cases. The method is the same in each. A blink-killer is an ordinary citizen of the Vigil (a cook, a nudger, a clerk) who blinks for two seconds, mid-sentence, standing at a market stall, and in those two seconds goes to the long room with the eye, and something there uses them. They come back holding a knife they did not pick up, standing behind a stranger, and the stranger is open from hip to collarbone in one perfectly straight line, and the blink-killer does not remember any of it. The cut is the same each time: the line a midwife or a butcher would use to open a body for inspection. The steam comes off the opened body in the lamplight, and the smell, hot and coppery and faintly fecal, reaches the stall-holders before the scream does. The killer is usually still talking, finishing the sentence they began. Blink-killers are not monsters. They are anyone. That is the horror. The stat below is for the second in which they are something else.

[fiction]
Blink-Matter Thirty-Two, statement of the accused, a pastry-cook. "I was telling her the price of the honey-twists. Three crumbs, I said. I blinked. I said, or two for five. And she was lying on the twists, and I had my peel-knife, and it was clean. Inspector, it was clean. I have never cleaned a knife that well in my life."
[/fiction]

### A Seeming Made Solid

After enough waking, everyone hallucinates, and when a whole street sees the same thing, it is entered as news. When a whole street sees the same thing for long enough, it becomes true. A Seeming made solid is a hallucination that has been agreed on by too many exhausted minds: a horse with a man's face walking down the Slope, a staircase that goes on past the roof, a tall figure laying a table in the street. It has weight, smell and heat. It does what the people imagining it fear it will do. It is precisely as strong as their certainty and it smells like rouse, burnt and bitter, because it is made of their attention. What it kills stays killed. The bodies are real, and the Registrar of the Seeming enters them with a cause of death nobody else on the Table would accept: *imagined by eleven households*.

[pull] Eleven districts, separately, the same figure: very tall, stooping, laying a cloth in the road. I entered it as news. Then I went home and set an extra place, and I do not know why. | — Mirela Sconce, the Seemer

### Sleepwalking Counted

Sixty thousand beds, numbered, in rows, completely silent. The Counted breathe in time. In the last year, some have begun to get up. A sleepwalking Counted rises with its eyes closed, its lips moving, and walks in the direction the eye in the dream faces. It does not wake. It cannot be woken. It is warm, unaged since the night it lay down, nightclothes faded to cobweb, hair grown to the floor and dragging, and the soles of its feet as soft and unmarked as an infant's, so that they bleed within a street and it does not notice. It smells of clean linen and old breath. Wherever it walks people around it begin to yawn, then to nod, then to blink. Matron Dorm sends them back to their beds with an orderly on each arm. Odile Farthing's son, in bed 40,112, has grown to a man's size and is smiling. When he walks, he walks with his arms open.

[pull] We put her back in 9,301 three times this week. Each time the sheet's warmer. Each time her lips are further along the same word. | — a Dormitory orderly, to the Matron

### The Waker Gone Mad

The Wakers' job is to hurt people for their own good, and they are skilled after three centuries. A Waker gone mad has decided that nobody is safe, ever, unless they are in pain. He is usually an old Waker, on triple rouse, lidless or near it from long effort, his eyes gone red and cracked and leaking, his teeth long since rotted to brown pegs by the drug. His breath stinks of burnt rouse and gum-rot. He carries a rattle and a sheaf of long steel needles. He has stopped distinguishing between a citizen who is nodding and a citizen who might nod later. He goes door to door at the third bell. He pins people's eyelids open with fish-hooks. He drives needles under nails so that no one in the house can doze. In his cellar there are people he has been keeping awake for weeks, tied in standing frames, weeping and hallucinating, their eyelids torn and crusted, and he feeds them rouse by the spoon and sings to them. They thank him. That is the sound the Wakers who find his cellar never forget: a row of people in frames, whispering *thank you, thank you* in time.

### The Yawn

On Yawn days, the Wakers double their patrols. The Yawn is not a creature; it is contagion. Lukas Marre once put down a market square. When a yawn is spreading through a crowd it moves like a rumor, mouth to mouth, jaws cracking, eyes streaming, and the sound of it, a hundred sighing breaths, is the most frightening sound in the city. Every person who catches it must roll Caul + Resolve 14 or blink. In a crowd of the exhausted the first blink becomes ten, the ten become a hundred, and the hundred do not all come back. The ones who do not are carted to the Dormitory steps before the next bell, still warm, faces soft and young with rest, and their families walk beside the cart pinching each other. Treat a Yawn as a hazard with Dread 1 that becomes Dread 3 the moment a character sees someone fall and not rise. The Glass House exists because of it.

### New Horror: The Tether-Catch

The Dream-Divers go under on a tether and are dragged up by force. Most manage three dives. The tether is a braided silk line fixed to an iron collar, and the hauling crew reel it in at the count of sixty with a windlass, whatever the diver is doing in the long room. Usually what comes up is the diver. Sometimes it is the diver and something else. A Tether-Catch is a thing from the long room that took hold of the line on the way up, or took hold of the diver, or got in.

It surfaces on the hauling-floor of the Tether Hall as a second figure tangled in the silk, or as a shape lying across the diver's chest, or as nothing at all, only a diver who wakes and sits up and looks around the room as if counting the beds. Those that come up with bodies of their own are pale, hairless, soft, and very long, with closed eyes that are only shut skin, no lids at all, and they move by stretching like a yawn. They smell of sleep: warm sheets, sour breath, the bitterness of a mouth that has been open all night. They are drawn to wakefulness the way moths are drawn to lamps, and they put it out. A Tether-Catch that gets loose in the city moves from nudger-house to rouse-den laying its long cool hands over eyes, and behind it the streets go quiet, and people lie down in doorways with their shoes neatly beside them.

### New Horror: The Made Bed

Beds were burned in the squares in 342 A.G., and there has not been a bed, couch or cushion in the city since. In the sealed buildings of the old Slope and in the lamp-starved back rooms the Lamplighters cannot reach, a bed sometimes makes itself. Nobody carries it in. It is simply there one morning, in a room whose lamp has been out a week: a broad low bed with a carved headboard in the old Ludmere style, a feather mattress, linen white as milk, the covers turned down, and a dent in the pillow as if someone has only just risen. The room is dark, warm and still, and it smells of lavender and clean sleep. There is a faint sound, sometimes, of someone breathing in the next room, slowly.

People find their way to a Made Bed the way water finds a drain: a tired clerk takes a wrong turn; a child is sent for a lost coat; a nudger follows a rumour of a quiet place. The family finds the shoes outside the door, side by side, the coat folded on top. Inside, the sleeper lies on their back with a smile, warm, unwakeable, and the covers drawn up to the chin by a hand that was not theirs. The bed does not let go. Cut the linen away and the sleeper is found grown into the mattress, the feathers rooted in their back, the headboard's carving continued in a fine pattern across their scalp.

[pull] A pair of shoes on a doorstep, a folded coat on top, the door open on a dark warm room. We call it Lain-Down. We do not call it anything else, and we close the door, and we do not go in. | — Vigil custom, as explained to an envoy

## Cradlewrack: What Comes Through

[sigil: cradlewrack]

Nothing in Cradlewrack is born right, and it is all healthy. Cradlewrack horrors are new, wet and determined. Many are drawn toward each other, toward the Sill or toward the Dilation, and they are getting bigger. They smell of milk, iron and warm clay, and they arrive, always, through something that was supposed to stay shut.

[pull] First we ask what it is. Then we ask whether it stays. Then, God help us, we ask whether it's ours. | — the Two Questions, with the third that midwives add under their breath

### Wandering Parts

A single living hand. A warm ear. A length of spine that crawls. The Parts are born healthy and drawn toward each other, and when they are not caught by the Attendance's knife or the Assemblers' sacks, they wander. They move in the night, at the speed of a crawling infant, through doors that drift open: a hand walking on its fingertips, a pair of lungs that inflate and deflate to roll themselves along, a jaw that pulls itself forward by its teeth. They are warm and blood-pink and smell of milk and iron, and they leave a faint sticky trail, like a snail's, that dries to a crust the colour of rust. They seek each other, and when two fit, they join with a wet click and a seam that heals in an hour. A cluster that has found six or seven partners is called a Gathered, and it is the size of a dog and the shape of no animal, and it is hungry, and it wants the parts it is missing. It will take them from whoever has them. Its victims are found with a single neat absence: a hand gone at the wrist, an ear, a kneecap, the wound already skinned over, as if they had been born without it.

[pull] Wee hand came in under the door and took hold of my finger, gentle as you like. I let it. What was I to do. It held on all night. In the morning it had my little finger, and I had a stump, and it was gone. | — a shepherd of Rudge

### The Refused

The Attendance's knife is called the Second Opinion, and there is no appeal from it. Except sometimes, as Sabeth Thorne found, the knife hesitates. A Refused is a born-grown thing that was meant to be cut and was not, and that has lived since in hiding, and has grown up knowing what it was supposed to be. It may look like a tall woman with a seam across her throat, or a man of thirty born mid-stride with a second face asleep in his chest, its breath warm through his shirt. It knows it was judged. It remembers the midwife's face, the lamp, the smell of the apron. Refused are clever, careful, and very often kind, until they meet a midwife. Then they settle the account. Midwives killed by the Refused are found in their own aprons, opened with their own knife along the line they were taught, and laid with care on the birthing-stool.

### Fenn's Get

Josiah Fenn, a shepherd, labored for three days and delivered something that stood up, looked at him, and walked out of the door. He has followed its tracks for eight years, and they are getting larger. Fenn's Get is twelve feet tall now. It walks the high red hills on legs too long, with a lamb's soft white face on a man's long skull, and its wool is matted with blood from the flocks it lies down among. It is not cruel. It is newborn and enormous, and it does not understand why things break when it holds them. The flocks it has lain with are found in a flattened circle in the heather, every sheep pressed into the turf, their wool combed smooth, as if a child had tucked them in. It has a shepherd's crook it has made from a whole ash tree. It has been seen, at dusk, sitting at a distance from Fenn's camp, watching its father, and making the bleating sound that lambs make for their mother.

### Crownings

The earth dilates, slowly, over weeks, into a perfect round opening, and people gather at the rim to see what comes up. A crowning is a hazard, a festival and a monster's birth in one. The rim is soft, red, wet, warm as flesh, and it gives underfoot like a lip; the hole is perfectly round and descends into darkness that pulses. The air above it is humid and smells of iron and birth-water, and every pang the ground around it groans, a long low sound the watchers feel in their own bellies. Every pang, the crowning widens a hand's breadth. Then, at the end, something comes up. Roll on the table below when it does, and run the crowd. The rim itself is Dread 2 on sight; the moment of emergence is Dread 4.

### Doorborn

A locked chest once gave birth. So did a well. Doorborn are the offspring of thresholds: thin grey things with jointed limbs like hinges, born from any door, gate or lid that has been shut too long. They come out at night into a sleeping house, unfolding from a cupboard or a bricked room with the creak of an unoiled hinge and a smell of dust, damp plaster and something like oiled iron, and they open things: windows, cradles, mouths, eyes, wounds, chests. They do it with care, with long fingers like keys, and they hum while they work, the same tuneless hum of a locksmith at his bench. Victims are found opened neatly along every seam a midwife knows, still alive, and very surprised. Agnes Latch's doors keep them in for up to nineteen days.

### New Horror: Gravebirth

Locks, seals and graves fail in Cradlewrack; nothing stays shut. Most highland dead are buried in round clay pits capped with a stone, and the cap is checked each pang. A woman four days dead once gave birth; a Gravebirth is what that looks like a year in. The cap is found pushed aside from below. The earth of the grave is heaved and split, like a loaf that has risen and burst its crust, and wet, and warm. There is a small round opening at the centre, the width of a fist, and the clay around it is stained dark. Inside, the dead person lies as they were laid, except that they are open, and empty, and something has come out.

What comes out is small, wet and quick, about the size of a hare, grey-pink and soft-skulled, and it wears the dead person's face, in miniature, perfectly: the mole, the scar, the expression they died with. It goes home. It knows the way. It climbs in through the window that is always ajar and curls up in its old place: the chair by the fire, the left side of the bed, the cradle that was theirs a lifetime ago. It is cold, and it wants warmth, and it gets it from the living, slowly, by lying against them in the night. The family wakes grey and shivering, and weaker each morning, and some of them cannot bring themselves to put it out, because it has their mother's face.

[/fiction]

### New Horror: A Loose Cord

The Uncut walk through life still attached to their mothers by yards of living cord. Sister Maeve Cord's mother has been dead six years, and the cord has not noticed. Sometimes a cord does notice. When an Uncut dies, or when a cord is severed in a brawl or by the Attendance's order, the length that is left does not lie down. It is thick as a wrist, glossy and blue-grey, warm, twisted on itself like a rope, and it pulses, slowly, with a heartbeat that belongs to neither end. It goes looking for a navel. It moves like a snake through the grass and under doors, and the hiss of it on floorboards is soft and wet. It wants to be joined. It wraps a sleeper's waist and fixes itself, mouthless, with a slow seeping heat, and begins to feed in both directions: whatever it was joined to before, it is joined to now, and the sleeper wakes to find themselves bound by a living tether to a grave, a stranger, a hill, or something in the dark at the bottom of the Dilation.
