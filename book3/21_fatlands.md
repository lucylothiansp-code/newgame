The fat was the first thing. Mam always said so, and Mam was right about the fat: you start a man at the fat, because the fat remembers the fire best.
We laid Da on the big board at noon, the day after he went, with the shutters wide so the neighbours could see we had nothing to hide and no meanness in us. The board had been scrubbed with salt and hot water at dawn until the grain stood up white, and Mam had laid the good linen under him, the linen she was married on, so that he would not be lying on bare wood like a pig at market. Hob Gristle came out from Sated to do the jointing, because Da had been a weighty man and a deacon of the Weighhouse and you do not let your cousins hack at a deacon. Hob kissed the knife. He kissed Da's brow. He said, "Sorry, Wilm. Sorry, old lad. Sorry," every time the blade went in, under his breath, like a man saying a grace he did not believe but could not stop.
Da came apart beautifully. That is what everyone said after. The skin lifted off the shoulder in one sheet, gold underneath, the fat as thick as three fingers laid flat and white as a new cheese, marbled with pink threads like a map of rivers. There was a smell from him even raw, a sweet one, grass and warm butter and the inside of a barn in Plenty, because Da had eaten Long Acre his whole life and Long Acre is famous. You could see the field in him. Everyone said so. They pressed in at the windows and said so. Old Ma Tibbet from three doors down put her hand flat on the sill and breathed in through her nose with her eyes shut, the way you breathe at a bakery door, and said, "Oh, Wilm. Oh, you lovely man."
We rendered the belly first. It went into the big copper over the yard fire and it sighed when it hit the heat, and the yard filled up with the smell of him, rich and brown and bottomless, and the dogs all lay down flat in the lane and whined. The fat went clear in the copper by degrees, the white going glassy at the edges and then gold, and little bubbles came up through it slow as a man thinking, and on the top a scum of something pinkish gathered that Mam said was his temper, and skimmed off, and gave to the dogs, because you do not eat a man's temper. My sister Petty skimmed the dripping with Mam's wedding ladle. She cried the whole time and kept tasting it. You could not blame her. Nobody could have blamed her.
The legs went on the spit. Hob had scored the skin in diamonds and rubbed salt and crushed sage into the cuts, and as they turned they spat and crackled and the skin blistered up into golden bubbles that popped when you pressed them and ran clear grease down your wrist. The hands went into the pie, with onions and the little purple turnips that grow along Granny Hessop's Rise, because that is the custom for a man who worked with his hands. Mam made the crust herself. She crimped the edge with her thumb, all the way round, and put a pastry wheatsheaf on the lid, and then she stood and looked at it for a long time with her floury hands held up in front of her like a surgeon's, and then she put it in the oven. The heart was mine to cook, as eldest. I seared it in its own fat until the outside cracked like toffee and the inside was the colour of a plum, and I sliced it thin and laid the slices out on the blue plate that came from Mam's mother, overlapping, like scales, the way he would have wanted. When I cut it, it was still warm in the middle. When I put the first slice in my mouth it was so good that I sat down on the floor.
I am telling you the truth. It was the best thing I have ever eaten. It tasted of being twelve and being lifted onto his shoulders at the Weighing. It tasted of his pipe. It tasted of the time he beat me with the strap for losing the heifer and then sat up all night on the end of my bed, saying nothing, with his big hand on my ankle. It ran down my chin and I did not wipe it.
By dusk there were sixty at the board and the yard was slick with drip and the copper had been refilled twice. The Bailiff came round to see the Feasting Laws were kept and there was nothing for him to do, so he sat and ate a rib of Da with tears on his big soft face and said Wilm Callock was the finest-marbled man in the parish, and he would know. Petty had stopped crying and was eating with both hands. Mam was eating with both hands. I was eating with both hands. The children were under the board, sucking the knuckle-bones, which is what the knuckle-bones are for. Somebody started the wake-song and everyone took it up, *remember, he was soft, remember, he was sweet*, with their mouths full, so that it came out thick and wet, like a hymn sung under water.
And I was hungry. That is the thing nobody tells you before your first wake, though they all know it. I ate my father from noon until the Plate came up, and every mouthful was the best mouthful of my life, and I was exactly as hungry at the end as I was at the start. Hungrier. Because now I knew what he tasted like and there was no more of him.
Late on, when the board was down to bones, I went out to Long Acre on my own. The field was sweating in the dark. I knelt and put my face to the soil and it was warm as a cheek and it moved, slowly, up and down, like something sleeping. I said, "Thank you, Long Acre." I said, "We are giving him back to you, we are, what's left, I promise." The furrow under my mouth opened a little. Just a little. Just enough for me to feel the teeth in it, small ones, wheat-teeth, rows and rows of them, all along the ground, all through the dark, as far as the Plate could light. They did not bite. They only touched my lips, very lightly, all together, the way a dog touches your hand with its mouth to say it knows you.
Then I went home and had the marrow, because the marrow had been saved for me.

## The Meal

Every land on the Table remembers its Gorging as something done slowly, in shame, with stops for vomiting and prayer. The Fatlands remember theirs as a party.

That is the first thing outsiders find unbearable, and it is true. In the ninth winter of the Long Lack the southern plains were a grey crust of dead clay from the Brawn to the sea. The people had eaten their seed, then their oxen, then their boots, then the thatch, and by the last Tablenight they were eating the clay itself, baked into flat cakes that cracked teeth and stopped the bowels. The chronicles keep a list of what was eaten in that winter, set out like a bill of fare with the dates beside it, and it is the most frightening document in the Board's archive: *bark of elm, boiled; harness, cut in strips; the glue of bookbindings; candles; the hair of the dead, in broth; the dog; the other dog; clay with salt; clay.* Women's milk failed; infants were nursed on blood let from their mothers' arms. In the village that later became Dimble, the chronicle notes that the people "did draw lots for one another, and kept faith with the lots," and does not say more, and does not need to.

Their god had come down out of the hills to lie among them in the field called the Trencher, because there was nowhere else for it to go. Ummer, the Laden, the Harvest Walker, had once been so vast with plenty that the furrows bent toward it like grass toward a wind. By that winter it was a long grey heap of sagging hide, a hill of skin on a frame of ribs like a collapsed barn, breathing a little. Its belly, which the oldest carvings show swollen with sheaves and calves and fruit, lay empty and folded on the earth like a dropped sack. Its many hands, which had broken bread off its own shoulders for the world, lay open in the clay, palm up. Children were sent to sit in them for warmth. The hands did not close. The people sat around it for forty days, the chronicles say, and the god's breath came slower and slower, and smelled at the end of nothing at all, which was the worst smell anyone had known: a smell of an empty larder, of a cold oven, of a bowl that has been licked so long it has no taste left even of the licking.

[pull] Elm bark. Harness. The glue of books. The dog. The other dog. Clay with salt. Clay. | — the Winter List, as read aloud in the Weighhouse each Tablenight

The Fatland chronicles, which were written down in the Sweet Reigns by people who had been told by people who had been there, insist that nobody gave an order. A girl named Wenna Strake, aged about sixteen, walked out across the Trencher at dusk with a flint sickle, and the crowd watched her go, and she knelt by Ummer's flank and looked into the one great clouded eye that faced her, and said, "Lack keep away." Then she cut. The god did not move. Its eye, every account says, was wet and kind and *pleased*, the way a mother's face is pleased when a child at last takes the spoon.

The first cut opened on fat. It is the one detail every version keeps. Under a god's hide that had been starving for nine years, there was still, impossibly, a layer of gold, a hand deep, as though Ummer had kept one last larder against exactly this night and saved it for them. Wenna Strake put her hand into the cut to the wrist and drew it out glistening and put her fingers in her mouth, and the chronicle says she made a sound "like one who has been struck very gently on the heart," and then she sat down in the clay and began to laugh and could not stop.

They ate it in a night. They ate it fastest of all the peoples. The chronicles linger on this with a horrible, unashamed relish that the College has never managed to edit out. They describe the hide, which peeled away in sheets the size of sails and was found to be lined with a layer of golden fat a hand deep, still faintly warm, smelling of cut hay and new bread and the inside of a dairy. They describe how the people, who had eaten clay for a season, tore at the fat raw and wept as it melted on their tongues, sliding down throats that had forgotten how to swallow anything soft; how the meat beneath was dark and dense and so full of juice that it ran in the furrows, red-brown and steaming, and children lay on their bellies and lapped it from the clay like puppies; how whole families crawled bodily into the cavity of the ribs to get at the organs, and came out painted to the shoulder, hair slicked flat with blood and lymph, grinning, with lumps of liver in both fists; how fires were lit all over the Trencher and the smoke rose so thick and sweet that birds dropped out of it, gorged on the air alone. There was singing. There was dancing in the grease. Old men who had buried every child they had danced with their mouths full.

Some of them died of it. The chronicle, for once, is honest. Bodies that had eaten clay for a season could not hold a god; bellies burst, hearts stopped with the shock of plenty, and an old woman named Gammer Rusk choked on a gobbet of the god's tongue and was found at dawn still smiling around it. The dead were not mourned that night. They were *eaten*, where they fell, by their neighbours, and nobody could afterward say whose meat was whose, which is why the Fatlands still say, of any fortunate death: *she went on the Trencher*.

Ummer's bones were cracked on the field-stones for the marrow before dawn. The marrow was yellow, and sweet as honey, and it is said that those who sucked it never afterward grew old in the face, only in the body. The skull was split with mattocks. The brain was shared out a spoonful each to the elders, grey-pink, trembling on the spoon like a custard, tasting (one elder told the chronicler, in old age) "of every harvest-home there ever was, all at once, so that you could not tell which year you were in." The eyes were last. They were the size of cartwheels, and wept when they were lifted, and were found to be full of a clear sweet jelly that set like aspic in the cold. Nothing was wasted. Not the hooves, not the hide, not the eyes. That is the Fatlands' founding boast, still carved over the doors of the Weighhouse in Sated: NOTHING WAS LEFT.

The families who reached the middle first, and took the stomach, the paunch, the long coils of the entrails, and the great swollen liver, became the Gut-lines. Some say they won those portions by strength. Others say the god's insides *came to them*, sliding out of the opened belly toward particular hands. The chronicles record, without comment, that the stomach was still working when it was eaten. It had been empty for nine years. It was digesting whatever it touched. Those who ate it had blistered mouths for a month, and the women who carried the entrails out in their arms lost the skin of their forearms to the elbow and grew it back, the chronicle says, "thicker, and of a warmer colour." The Gut-lines still bear it. Look at the inside of a great lady's wrist in Sated and you will see the skin there faintly ridged, faintly ruddy, like a rind.

The famine ended the next morning. The clay of the Trencher was black and soft and steaming by sunrise. By noon there were green shoots across the whole plain. Within a week the wheat stood shoulder-high, and that was the Week of Rising, and nobody asked what it was wheat *of*.

One detail is preserved only in a single fragmentary song, sung in the Bleeding Orchards at pruning time, and the Board has twice tried to forbid it. It says that when the feasters had finished, and lay on the Trencher in the grey light, too heavy to move, every one of them woke at the same moment with the same thought. It was not guilt. It was not grief. The song gives it in four words, and the Orchard-keepers sing them very softly: *Is there any more?*

The chronicles say nothing of Wenna Strake after the Meal. She cut first, and was honoured, and vanishes. The Field Church teaches that she was given the first marrow and walked off into the risen wheat and was taken up into the god, a saint of the knife; there is a niche for her in every parish church, empty, with a flint in it. The Board's oldest registers list a *Strake, W., widow* paying Plate-Tithe on an orchard plot east of Sated until 140 A.G., and then nothing. Orchard-keepers at Sapwell will tell a stranger, with their hands, that the Old Grove is where she went, and that the trees there are her. They say it the way people tell a fairy story. They do not let strangers prune in the Old Grove.

[box: Ummer in the Old Carvings]
Before the Gorging, Ummer was carved on every granary lintel on the plains: a broad, stooping, many-handed figure walking ahead of the plough, with sheaves sprouting from its shoulders and a belly like a full cart. In the few carvings that survive whole, it walks in procession with the other Providers. The Gut-lines prefer not to display these. One reason is obvious: Ummer is shown *feeding* the others from its own body, breaking off pieces of its shoulder and handing them along the line. The other reason is less discussed. In every surviving procession, there is a figure walking just behind Ummer, very close, with its hand on the Laden's back. That figure has been chiselled away to the bare stone. In the oldest carving in the Fatlands, over the root-cellar of a ruined granary at Dimble, the chisel slipped, and a little of the hand remains. It is very long, and it is open, palm up, the way you hold out a hand for more.

### The Week of Rising

The plain did not merely recover. It *answered*. On the first morning the Trencher steamed like a pudding lifted from the cloth, and the clay had gone black and soft and greasy to the touch, and when a man named Aldous Crumb put his spade in it to see, the soil bled a little around the blade and closed over the hole in an hour. On the second day green shoots stood an inch high across every field within a day's walk of the Trencher; on the third, they were at the knee, and could be heard growing at night, a sound like many people breathing out through their noses. By the fifth day the wheat was at the shoulder and in ear. On the seventh it was cut, threshed and baked, and the first loaf of the Fatlands came out of a clay oven at Dimble with a crust that crackled like pork skin and a crumb that pulled apart in long, moist, pink-tinged threads, and the woman who baked it, tasting it, began to cry, and nobody could get her to say why.

The cattle came back the same week, though nobody had any cattle left to breed. Herds were found standing in the morning fields, silent, sleek, wet, as though calved from the ground in the night. The chronicle calls them "the gift-beasts." It mentions, in passing, that the first of them had no navels.

## The Rule of the Land

Everything here is meat, and nobody is ever full.

Those nine words are the whole of Fatland law, Fatland faith and Fatland misery, and every child can recite them before it can count. They are sewn on samplers, stamped on the lids of Board flour-barrels, and chalked over the doors of cookshops; children skip to them in the lanes of Sated (*everything here is MEAT, and nobody is EVER full*, a jump on each stress), and the old say them over a finished plate the way other lands say *amen*. What they mean in practice takes longer.

### Everything Is Meat

The soil of the Fatlands is Ummer's flesh, scattered and buried and grown back, and it grows flesh in turn. Nothing that comes out of the ground here is entirely vegetable. Wheat stands in the field in golden ranks and looks, from the road, like any wheat on the Table; split an ear and you find each grain sheathed in a little moist pink gum, and in the gum, a tooth. Toothed wheat has been the only wheat for four centuries. Milled, it makes a heavy, sweet, faintly salty flour that bakes into bread with a crust like crackling and a crumb that tears in long fibres, like pulled shoulder. The teeth are sieved out at the mill. Most of them are milk teeth. Not all.

A Fatland loaf deserves a page to itself, because nothing else on the Table is like it. The dough, kneaded, is warm before it is proved; it is the colour of a baby's palm and it *resists*, a little, under the heel of the hand, the way muscle resists, and bakers say a good dough "pushes back like a sleeping dog." It rises without yeast. Left in its bowl under a cloth it swells and subsides, swells and subsides, slowly, and a careful baker times the oven by counting the breaths. In the heat it does not so much brown as *sear*: the crust goes deep amber and blisters and cracks into plates that shatter in the teeth with a sound like stepping on frost, and the smell coming out of a Fatland bakery at dawn is not the smell of bread at all but of roasting, of a joint basted in its own fat, with something underneath like warm milk and clean scalp. Broken open, the crumb steams. It is faintly pink at the heart, and moist, and threaded with long pale fibres that pull apart in the fingers like slow-cooked shoulder; and it tastes of salt and butter and something deep and savoury that coats the back of the tongue and will not go away, so that a foreigner eating Fatland bread for the first time typically stops after the first mouthful, puts the loaf down, stares at it, and then eats the whole of it without speaking.

[pull] A good dough pushes back like a sleeping dog. A *great* dough growls. | — sampler in a bakery on Ladle Lanes

Orchard trees bleed when they are pruned: a slow, dark, clotting sap that scabs over in a day. Their fruit has a skin, a layer of soft white fat under it, firm pink flesh beneath that, and at the core a pit that is unmistakably bone, ridged and porous and full of a sweet grey marrow children suck out with straws. A ripe Orchard pear, bitten, gives under the teeth in three stages, like a cut of good belly: the thin crisp skin, then the slippery cool yield of the fat, then the dense sweet meat with its grain running lengthwise, and the juice is pink and runs to the elbow and smells of rose and of a butcher's apron. Root vegetables have veins. A beet pulled from the ground pulses faintly in the hand for a minute or two before it stops, and is warm, and the cut face of it glistens and beads with a dark bright juice that will not wash out of linen. Onions have a membrane between every layer, and if you cut them too slowly they flinch. Cabbages are warm in the middle, and the inmost leaves are slick and folded tight over one another like the lids of a sleeping eye. Potatoes, the poor's food, grow in clusters joined by gristle, and when the clusters are pulled apart the cut ends sweat clear lymph. Turnips scream very faintly when boiled; cooks put a lid on.

Dig a well and you strike something that flinches. Every Fatland well is lined with salt-glazed brick, and the water drawn from it is warm, slightly cloudy, and tastes of broth. Drive a fencepost too deep and the ground around it will be bruised purple by morning. Ploughs do not so much turn the soil as *open* it, and the furrow behind the share is red-brown and glistening and smells, in the heat, of a butcher's block. Ploughmen walk barefoot behind the share by preference, because the furrow is warm and soft and closes gently around the ankle like a hand; their feet are stained to the shin a deep oxblood that never comes off, and in Low Tilth a girl will look at a lad's ankles before she looks at his face.

The livestock is the strangest of all, because livestock should be the one thing that was meat already. The cattle of the Fatlands are enormous, sleek, docile and silent. They do not low. They watch. Drovers say a Fatland steer will follow a man anywhere if he walks ahead of it eating, and the Renderers' Union does not employ anyone who has spent too long looking a steer in the eye. Pigs are hairless and pale, with long lashes, and they lie in their sties in rows on their sides like sleepers in a dormitory and breathe in unison. Chickens lay eggs with a heartbeat; a Fatland breakfast egg, cracked into the pan, twitches once in the fat before it sets. Nobody asks what the cattle eat in the far pastures when they are out of sight, because the answer is grass, and the grass here has teeth.

And all of it, every mouthful, is delicious. This must be understood or nothing about the Fatlands will make sense. There is no bad food in the south. The poorest gristle-potato boiled in well-water is better than a lord's feast in Oathen. Fatland bread, broken hot, is so good that foreign travellers have been known to weep at the first bite and refuse to leave the inn. The Fatlands are the breadbasket and the meat-locker of half the Table, and every land that buys from them knows, at some level it does not visit, that it is buying the Laden god by the pound.

### Things in the Wheat

The teeth are the least of it. Every harvest the threshers, the gleaners and the millers' sieves turn up other things, and every family on the plain has a jar on a shelf of oddments the field gave back. Most are thrown away. Some are kept, wrapped in a clean cloth, and brought out at wakes. A few are carried to the Long Count, or to Granny Cracknel, or to the Bailiff, depending on who the finder trusts.

### Nobody Is Ever Full

A Fatlander can eat until the belly splits, and some do, and still feel the hunger exactly as before.

Hunger here has no connection to the stomach. It is not a signal that the body needs food; it is a *presence*, the god's own nine-year famine, inherited and shared out through ten thousand bloodlines like the meat itself. It sits beneath the breastbone and gnaws. It wakes children in the night. It interrupts weddings and funerals and sermons and acts of love. Eating soothes it the way scratching soothes an itch: wonderfully, for exactly as long as you are doing it, and then not at all.

Fatlanders describe it with the precision of people who have had nothing else to compare. It is *a hand in the chest, open*. It is *a mouth under the ribs that can taste what my mouth tastes, and wants more of it*. It is *like missing someone, but in the belly*. The Field Church calls it *the Godward Ache* and preaches that it is Ummer's love, longing to be fed back. The Thin call it the god's starvation, which we stole, and which we owe. Children call it *the Grumble* and give it a face in their drawings: a round, wide-mouthed, smiling thing that sits inside a stick-figure's tummy with its arms open.

The Fatlands have therefore built a civilization around eating constantly. Five meals a day are mandatory under the Feasting Laws, and the poor fit four more around them if they can. Field-hands chew strips of salt fat as they work, and the rhythm of a reaping gang is set by the jaws as much as the arms. Clerks keep bowls of cracklings at their elbows; the Weighhouse ledgers are speckled with grease-spots, and Board scribes are trained to write left-handed so the right can feed. Every inn and every house keeps a pot on the hob at all hours, and it is never allowed to empty; some family pots have not been empty in a hundred years, and are said to have a grandmother in them still, in the black crust at the bottom where the spoon cannot reach. Waste is unthinkable. Refusal is sedition. A thin Fatlander is either sick, criminal, or one of the Thin, and in the eyes of the law these are the same thing.

The people are, accordingly, huge. A healthy labourer of the Scrapling class might weigh three hundred pounds. A prosperous merchant, five hundred. The Gut-lines nobility begin at a thousand and do not stop. Fatland architecture is wide, low, and ramped. Fatland chairs are benches, Fatland doors are double, Fatland floors are joisted like bridges. In Sated there is a profession, the Carriers, of men and women employed to lift and turn the great, and another, the Washers, who clean the folds.

The Washers' work is not discussed at table, which in the Fatlands means it is very bad indeed. A great body of the Gut-lines folds upon itself like dough worked in a bowl, and in the deep creases, warm and dark and never aired, things grow. There is a white cheesy rind that must be lifted with a wooden spatula. There are the green shoots of the Course, which come up in the elbow-folds and the backs of the knees and under the breasts and must be tweezed daily. There is a sweet fermenting smell, like a cider-press in a hot barn, that the Washers' sponges never quite reach, and in the deepest folds of the very heaviest, the Washers sometimes find things the great one has lost there: a ring, a spoon, a letter, once a lap-dog, long dead, preserved in a waxy sheath and smelling of roses. Washers are paid more than surgeons. They are almost all women, and almost all go a little strange; they are known for laughing at nothing, and for scrubbing their own hands until they bleed.

[pull] Inside the fourth fold of Madam's left side there is a whole summer. You can smell it. Flowers, and hay, and something that died in the hay. | — a Washer of the Fold, to a Cutwright surveyor

### The Land Also Eats

The god was the god of harvest, and a harvest has two halves. The land gives. Then it takes.

Each Carving, as the fields come ripe, people go missing from every village. They go out in the field at dusk and are not back by morning. They leave no footprints going anywhere but out. Sometimes a boot is found in a furrow, standing upright, laces still tied. Sometimes a hat in the wheat, or a sickle with a polished handle, or a set of clothes laid neatly in the shape of a body, flat and empty, with the soil beneath them dark and wet and freshly turned. More often there is nothing at all. This is called the Reaping.

The official explanation is wolves. Every Board proclamation since 300 A.G. has said so, and the Board employs a Wolf-Warden in every district, who files a report each autumn describing tracks, sightings and depredations. There are no wolves in the Fatlands. There have never been any in living memory or written record. Nothing in the Fatlands is anything but crop.

[fiction]
RETURN OF THE WOLF-WARDEN FOR THE PARISH OF HASK'S END, CARVING 640.
Wolves seen: none. Wolves heard: none. Tracks: none, the ground having been soft.
Persons taken by wolves: nine (9). Names appended.
Manner: wolves.
Condition of remains: none remaining, excepting one (1) pair of clogs, one (1) bonnet, one (1) set of clothes laid out flat in the Long Strip as though the wearer had lain down in them and gone out of them, which I have returned to the family.
Measures taken: lanterns; bells; a dog, which would not go into the field and has since run off.
Recommendation: wolves.
I have the honour to remain the Board's servant. I wish to be relieved. I have written this nineteen years. I have never seen a wolf. My hand writes it on its own now.
[/fiction]
>> — Board archive, Registry Close; the last two lines are struck through in a different ink, and initialled "V.S."

Those who have watched a Reaping happen and kept their minds describe it with a terrible consistency. It is always dusk. The light goes amber, then red, and the heat lifts off the soil in a haze that smells of hot fat. The field goes still; the wind drops; the wheat stops whispering. The labourer is usually alone, or at the far end of a row from the others, bent to the work. And then the field *leans*. Every ear of wheat for fifty yards turns toward them at once, slowly, like a congregation turning to look at a latecomer. There is a sound one witness compared to a crowd of people chewing with their mouths shut. The soil around their feet softens to the consistency of warm suet and they sink to the ankle, and then to the knee, and they do not, by most accounts, struggle much; their faces go slack and grateful, the way a tired man's face goes slack when he lowers himself into a hot bath. The wheat closes over them. The ears move against the body, in and out, all of them, all over. There is a wet, comfortable, rhythmic noise, which goes on for some time. Then the field stands up straight again and the wind comes back and the wheat whispers, and next year that strip of field yields half again what the rest does, and the bread from it tastes, faintly, of the person.

Witnesses who were close enough add details the Board would prefer they did not. That the wheat does not cut; it *suckles*, a thousand small soft pink-gummed mouths working at once, so that the skin of the Reaped goes first to gooseflesh and then to something like wet paper, and then is simply not there, and the red underneath is drawn out in fine threads into the ears, like milk through straws. That the bones go last, and slowly, and soften first, so that a Reaped arm raised above the wheat (it happens; it is not a struggle, the witnesses insist, it is a *wave*) bends at the forearm in a place where there is no joint, and droops, and is drawn down. That the Reaped sometimes speak, at the end, and what they say is always food: *Is that bacon? Is someone frying? Mam, is it supper?* That afterward, for an hour, the patch of ground is warm as a bed somebody has just got out of, and gives off a smell of fresh bread, and anyone who kneels there will feel, under their palms, the slow contented pulse of something digesting.

Families know. Everyone knows. You can tell a Reaped man's row by the colour of the ears: a little redder, a little heavier, nodding lower. The widow of a Reaped man will often ask the miller to keep that strip's flour separate, and bakes it into a single loaf on Tablenight, and the family eats it together, slowly, in the dark, and says his name. The loaf is called a **name-loaf**. It is always the best bread of the year. Children who have eaten a parent's name-loaf say that for one mouthful they could hear them, very close, the way you can hear someone breathing on the other side of a pillow.

[box: The Reaping in Play]
The Reaping is not a monster to be fought; it is weather, and it is the god. A character caught alone in a ripe Fatland field between sunset and full dark in Carving may be Reaped. The GM should make it a slow, sensory scene, never a surprise kill. First the stillness (Eye + Search, Routine, to notice). Then the leaning (Dread 3 to witness; Dread 2 for a Fatlander, who has grown up with it). Then the softening ground: the victim must succeed at Caul + Resolve (Hard 14; Grim 18 if their Hunger is 6 or more, because the field *smells like dinner*) or stop wanting to leave. A character who fails may be dragged out by companions with Gut + Labor (Grim 18), but the field holds on: each round in the soil deals 1d6 Flesh as the ground begins, gently, to digest. A character who is Reaped is gone. What is pulled out after full dark is not a corpse, only a shape of clothes and a smell of bread. Fatlanders of Cut 3+ can sometimes feel which fields are hungry on a given night (Caul + Godsign, Hard 14).
[/box]

## The Regrowth in the Fatlands

Ummer is growing back, and what grows back is a field.

Every land's Regrowth is a return of the god's nature. Ummer's nature was abundance: the slow, rooted, generative patience of soil, which takes in what dies and gives it back as food. In the Fatlands the god does not wear its people so much as *plant* them. The Cutwrights' College calls the southern Regrowth "the most orderly on the Table," because its stages arrive in strict sequence and never skip. Fatlanders call it simply getting heavy. *She's getting heavy*, they say, of a grandmother whose feet have begun to itch, and they say it proudly, and pat her hand, and bring her another plate.

### The Taste

Hunger that wakes you. Every Fatlander has the god's hunger by day; at the Taste it begins to come in the night as well, a hollow ache that drags you up out of sleep with your mouth already working. Children chew their sleeves into rags, then their bedding, then the soft wood of the cot-rail. Mothers sew bone rings into nightshirt cuffs to give them something better to gnaw. Adults wake with the taste of soil on their tongues and cannot remember the dream. The breath begins to smell faintly of the fields, of grass and dung and rising dough. It is considered a healthy sign, and courting couples are told to look for it.

The dreams, when they are remembered, are all of the same kind. A long table in a field at dusk, laid for more people than are sitting at it. A smell of roasting coming from somewhere just over the next furrow. A voice, warm and kindly, from under the ground, asking if you have had enough. Fatland mothers ask their children at breakfast, *Did you dream the table?*, the way mothers in other lands ask about a fever; and the child who says *yes* is given an extra egg.

### The Appetite

Eating without choosing. At the Appetite the hand goes to the mouth before the mind has agreed: a bite of tallow candle, a mouthful of earth from a flowerpot, the corner of a leather glove, a twist of one's own hair. The sufferer will eat soap, wax, string, paper, chalk, rope, coal, and can digest a good deal of it. They cannot stop for conversation. They eat through sermons, through lovemaking, through their own children's weddings, and they weep while they do it, because they can taste everything and everything is wonderful. Fatland courts have recognized "the Appetite" as a defence to theft since 312 A.G., provided the stolen article was eaten in full. Clerks with the Appetite keep their ledgers chained, because the ink is made from rendered fat. At this stage, many begin to want meat that is not animal. They smell the living. They notice, without wishing to, how a neighbour's forearm would carve.

The Appetite has a texture all its own, and those who live with it learn its marks. The lips crack and are never healed, because they are always being licked. The teeth wear flat and brown from chewing things that are not food. The fingertips of the far gone are nibbled to soft pink nubs, nail and all, and many wear thimbles of bone on every finger, which they chew instead, so that the bone wears thin and has to be replaced each season. Their speech goes thick and wet. And their eyes change, people say: they rest on things differently, on a baby's fat wrist, on the back of a farmhand's neck in the sun, on the soft inner elbow of a girl reaching for the salt, with a look that is not lust and not malice but something older and simpler and much more frightening, the look of a hungry person reading a bill of fare.

[pull] My husband looks at me like I'm the Sunday joint. Twenty years. I used to think it was love. I still think it's love. That's what frightens me. | — a farmwife of the eastern plain

### The Course

The body swells past all proportion and the feet begin to put down roots. These people are called the Laden, after the god, and in the Gut-lines it is a title.

The swelling is not ordinary fat. It is soft, cool, slightly granular to the touch, and it gives under the finger like freshly turned loam and holds the print for hours. Press harder and it crumbles a little at the surface, and what crumbles is dark and moist and smells of rain on a ploughed field, and the hole fills slowly back from beneath, like a footprint in wet sand. The skin darkens to brown and red, mottled like a field seen from a hill. Hair thickens and coarsens into something fibrous, dry, straw-coloured, that rustles when the head turns. Small green shoots appear in the creases of the elbow and behind the knees; the Washers pluck them daily, and they grow back overnight. The Laden sweat a clear, sweet, faintly sticky fluid that draws flies and bees. They grow warm. A Laden person's bed is warm for a day after they leave it, and nothing stored beneath it spoils.

Things live in them. That is the part not printed in the Field Church's tracts. Earthworms are found in the deep folds of the Laden, pink and fat and slow, and the Washers pick them out with tweezers and lay them in a dish, and the Laden ask, very politely, to be given them back, and eat them. Beetles. Small white grubs. Once, in the Fold, a mouse's nest, with the young still blind, in the warm hollow under a great lord's arm, where he said he had felt them moving for a week "like a second heartbeat, very sweet." The College's surveyors record that at the Course the body temperature of the Laden rises to that of a compost heap in Plenty, and that a thermometer pushed into the flesh of the thigh will read hotter the deeper it goes.

The roots come last. They start as pale, hair-thin threads from the soles of the feet, and the Laden feel them as a pleasant tingling that becomes, over months, a desperate wish to stand still. If a Laden person stands on bare ground for an hour, the threads go down. If they stand for a day, the threads thicken into cords. Pulling free tears skin, and the cords bleed, and it hurts in a way the Laden describe as being torn out of a warm bed in Lack. Most Laden of the Gut-lines are moved on drays with iron floors, because iron is the one thing roots will not take in. Most Laden of the poor are simply allowed to plant.

### The Brink

At the Brink the Laden person is more field than flesh. They are rooted somewhere and cannot be moved without killing them; their bulk spreads outward into the soil and the soil rises to meet it. The face sinks into the mound of the body. The limbs are hummocks. The eyes cloud green. Crops grow on them, densely, without being sown. A Brink-Laden can still speak, though slowly and from deep in the chest, and still eats, endlessly, and everything around them grows: the grass in a ring of thirty yards around a Brink-Laden is waist-high and toothed, and the wheat leans toward them when they speak. Visitors sometimes feel the ground pulse in time with the Brink-Laden's heart. In the last weeks the person begins to forget their own name and to remember, instead, the names of the fields around them, and the names of those who have been Reaped into those fields.

Families feed the Brink-Laden by hand, kneeling. The mouth is low in the mound by then, sometimes at ground level, lipped with turf where the beard was, and the family lays the food on the tongue the way you lay a coin in a slot, and the mouth closes and works for a long time with a sound of grinding, of wet gravel turned by a spade. Grandchildren are lifted up to kiss them goodnight, on the forehead, which is a soft warm patch of clover with two green-clouded eyes in it. Some Brink-Laden weep. The tears are sweet and attract bees, and in the Fold the very old Gut-lines families keep hives on the Brink-Laden's flanks, and the honey is dark, and sold at a great price as *grandmother honey*, and tastes of a long life ending.

[pull] I asked Gran if it hurt. She said no, love, it's like being a bed somebody nice is sleeping in. Then she asked me my name. | — a child of the Fold, aged nine

### The Seating

A Laden person stops being a person and becomes a place: a low hill, warm to the touch, where things grow very well. Some keep a mouth.

There is no single moment of Seating. The family notices, one morning, that Gran has stopped speaking and the hill has started breathing. The breath is slow, perhaps once an hour, and the whole mound lifts with it. The soil of a Seated hill is the richest on the Table. Its grass is the sweetest, its fruit the heaviest, its wheat the most toothed. Many of the best fields in the south are somebody's great-grandmother. Fatlanders say this with pride and mean it. *Granny Hessop's Rise* is not a fond name for a slope. It is a woman, and her grandchildren graze their sheep on her, and thank her at every meal.

Some hills keep a mouth. It sits somewhere in the turf like a wet red pond-edge, lipped and toothed, and it talks, and it eats whatever is put in it, and it is hungry. Some keep eyes, deep in the grass, that open in rain. And some, the College has recently confirmed, keep moving: very slowly, a few inches a year, toward the nearest other hill.

[box: The Hill-Census]
The Cutwrights' southern registry keeps the only full census of the Seated: about four thousand hills between the Chute and the sea, each with a number, a family, a date, and three columns headed *Mouth*, *Eyes* and *Motion*. Until 630 the third column was almost empty. As of Carving 640 it has entries against three hundred and eleven hills, and every entry points the same way: toward Sated, toward the Weighhouse, toward the Chairman's dray. The surveyor who compiled the column, Ottilie Vance, has asked to be transferred north. Her request has been refused, by weight.
[/box]

## Land and Weather

The Fatlands are low, wet, endless green-and-red plain under a heavy yellow sky. From the Rim Road they look like a painted country: the green of pasture and the red of turned soil and the gold of toothed wheat, laid out in strips as far as the eye can follow, under a sky the colour of old butter that never quite clears. There are no mountains. There are hardly any trees outside the orchards. There are only the fields, the long wet roads, the slow brown rivers, and the hills, which are people.

It is always warm. Even in Lack the ground steams at dawn. The ground gives underfoot like a mattress over a sleeper, and if you stand still in an open field, you can feel it slowly rise and fall. Foreigners take a week to stop noticing. Fatlanders never notice at all, and are made seasick by stone. A Fatlander's first night on the cobbles of Oathen is famous for its misery: they lie awake on the dead hard ground with their palms pressed flat to it, waiting for it to breathe, and weep when it does not.

Rain is called **drip**. It falls warm and heavy and leaves a yellowish film on standing water, which cooks skim off with flat spoons and use for frying; a good drip-pan after a storm is a housewife's pride. The hot-season wind, the **Belch**, comes up out of the south in late Plenty smelling of rendering, of tallow-vats and singed hair, and lasts for days; tempers break, and the Feasting Laws are relaxed so that people may eat a sixth meal to steady their nerves. In late Plenty the fields **sweat** and the whole country glistens. Midges rise from the wheat in shimmering columns. Every surface is tacky. And at the turn of Carving comes **Marbling**, the first frost, when the fat in the soil whitens overnight in long branching veins, and the plains look from horizon to horizon like a cut of meat laid on a slab. Marbling morning is a holiday. Children run out barefoot to crunch it.

There are other weathers the almanacs list in smaller print. **Gristle-fog**, in early Grace, comes up off the Gravies thick and white and faintly elastic, so that walking through it is like pushing through a membrane; it leaves a skin on the face that peels in the warmth. **Bloodshot** is a sunset in Carving when the whole sky goes the red of a cut beet, and the Wolf-Wardens ring bells, and nobody goes out. And once or twice a generation there is **Rennet**, a still, sour, curdling heat in which milk turns in the pail, wounds weep and will not close, and the Laden, all over the plain at once, begin to sing in their sleep the same tune, which nobody living has taught them.

The rivers are slow and brown and thick and warm, and they are called, without irony, gravy: the Brawn, which runs south past Sated to the sea, is the Great Gravy, and its tributaries are the Little Gravies. Their water must be strained before drinking. What is strained out is eaten.

[pull] Strain it twice. Eat what's in the cloth. Don't look at what's in the cloth. That's three rules. | — a bargewife of the Great Gravy, teaching her daughter

### A Gazetteer of the Fatlands

**Sated.** The capital, on the Great Gravy, built in concentric rings of low, broad, buttressed halls around the Weighhouse. Every door is double-wide, every floor reinforced, every stair replaced by a ramp, and the smell of gravy rises from every drain, because the drains carry the city's drip and dripping down to the rendering-houses on the river. The streets are paved with fat-stained stone and wide enough for a Gut-lines dray and its eight oxen. Sated never stops eating: street-cooks work all night beneath lamps that burn tallow of uncertain provenance, and at any hour the air is blue with frying. Its population is four hundred thousand by head and, as its proud citizens say, "a hundred and twenty million by the pound." Its quarters (the Scales, the Fold, Ladle Lanes, Wakeside, the Leanings and Registry Close) are each a little country of their own; the Leanings sink a storey a generation, and the oldest tenements there have kitchens below the street with the soil pressing at the windows like a face.

**The Weighhouse.** The seat of the Board of Plenty, in the heart of Sated: a vast round hall under a dome of glazed bone tiles, with the Great Scales at its centre, iron and bronze, each pan large enough to hold a cart. Here the Weighing is held each Grace, and here the Board sits, on benches sunk into the floor because many members can no longer be lifted onto anything. The floor of the Weighhouse has been reinforced six times. Its cellars, which nobody below the Board visits, are said to be full of roots. Iron gratings in the pavement of Scale Street let up a warm breath from them, smelling of loam and gravy and old paper, and passers-by stop at the gratings without meaning to, and stand, and are moved along by Bailiffs.

**The Bleeding Orchards.** Ten thousand acres of fruit trees east of Sated, in long rows on the slopes of what were once hills and may still be. At pruning time in early Grace the trees scream when cut: a high, wet, wavering sound, like a calf at the knife, from ten thousand throats at once, audible in Sated on a still day. Orchard-keepers go deaf young, by choice, with heated needles, and speak in a hand-language of their own. The fruit is the finest in the world. The sap is collected in buckets for blood-pudding. It comes out of the pruning-cuts in slow dark pulses, steaming, and clots in the bucket into a soft jelly with a skin, and the keepers' children are given a spoonful of it warm as a treat, and grow up with black-red mouths and strong hearts.

**Low Tilth.** The richest farmland in the world, a wide shallow basin south of Sated where the soil is black and soft and sweats in Plenty like a basted joint. It yields three harvests a year. It has the highest Reaping of any district, and nobody there locks their door, because it has never helped. Low Tilth villages are small, quiet and well-fed, and every family has someone in the fields. It is the home of Pell Hogget and the Long Count, and of the Scarecrow.

**The Dowager Hills.** A range of low green mounds three miles outside Sated, along the old Weighing Road: nine generations of Gut-lines matriarchs, Seated, ninety-one hills in all. The grass on them is short and soft and bright, and the soil is warm right through Lack. Each hill has a name, a family, a gatekeeper's hut, and a speaking-place: a hollow where the turf parts to show a mouth, or a pair of lids, or, on the oldest, only a warm damp crack that exhales. The Gut-lines families still consult their Dowagers on matters of marriage, inheritance and precedence, and bring them offerings. The offerings are meat. The Hills accept them, chewing for hours with a sound like a slow mill. Visitors walking the path between the mounds report that the hills breathe in sequence, a long slow wave of rising and falling turf, as though passing something along.

[box: Consulting a Dowager]
A family with a question for its Dowager brings her a joint (custom demands the best cut from the most recent wake) and the Hill-Speaker on duty lays it in the speaking-place. The Dowager eats. When she has eaten, she answers, sometimes in minutes and sometimes after a day. Her voice comes up through the turf and is felt in the feet as much as heard. Dowagers know things: the true parentage of heirs, the location of hidden debts, which fields will Reap this year. They are rarely wrong. They are also no longer entirely the women they were, and their advice now tends, more and more, toward the same few counsels: *Plant him. Feed her to me. Come closer. Sit down. Lie down. Stay.* A PC who sleeps a night on a Dowager Hill gains a true answer to one question (GM's choice of how cryptic) and must make a Dread 3 check on waking, because the roots will have begun.
[/box]

**The Chute.** The Rim Road export depot, at the Fatlands' northern edge where the Great Gravy meets the Road: a mile of rendering-houses, salting-sheds, cooperages and stockyards built around a long stone ramp down which barrels roll, day and night, into the Company's wagons. Meat goes out in barrels with the origin stamped over twice. The smell of the Chute reaches eleven miles on the Belch. The Renderers' Union holds it absolutely. The Rim Road Company keeps an office there with a notary's bench, and every barrel is tallied on a platter. The town that has grown around it is called Barrelside, and its children can tell a barrel's contents by the sound it makes rolling: beef booms, pork thuds, flour hisses, and some barrels, the children say, *knock back*.

**The Trencher.** The field where Ummer was eaten: a great shallow bowl of black soil a mile across, in the heart of the plain west of Sated. Nothing is planted there and everything grows anyway, in a dense tangle of toothed grain, veined roots and fleshy creepers that heal over any path cut through them in a night. At its centre is a rise of soil shaped, from the air, very like a sleeping figure lying on its side. The Board forbids entry. Pilgrims of the Second Helping go anyway and lie down in the creepers and are found, weeks later, smiling and partly absorbed: the creepers grown in at the ears and the corners of the mouth and under the nails, the skin gone soft and brown and crumbling at the edges where it meets the soil, the eyes open and wet and glad. Some are still breathing. The Board's patrols are under orders to leave them, because pulling them free has never once been survived, and because the creepers that have them in bear, the following Plenty, a fruit like small soft pink pears that the patrols are not supposed to eat and always do.

**The Gavage.** The Board's house of correction for the Thin, outside Sated on the Brawn: a long, low, whitewashed building of a hundred cells, each with a chair of oak and iron, a set of leather straps, a funnel-stand, and a drain. Its kitchens never close. Its smell is of porridge, lard, and vomit. See the Thin, below.

**The Dusk Acres.** The "far fields" of the Cullmasters: a belt of Board-owned land on the edges of Low Tilth and the eastern plains, rotated each Carving, where the Cullmasters' dusk-shift labourers are sent. They are the most productive fields in the country, and the most carefully ploughed, and each plot carries a small numbered stone at its corner. In spring, the Union's men walk them for calves.

**Gladbelly.** The commune of the Second Helping, a village of long open tables in a meadow beside the Little Gravy, where the cult eats together continuously and sings. The meadow is unusually lush. There is a slaughter-pavilion in the middle, hung with garlands, where the Offered go willingly. See the Second Helping, below.

**Marrable Hall.** The seat of Dame Suet Marrable and the Unending Dinner: a great ramped house in the Dowager Hills whose dining hall has had no clear floor for eighty-one years. The table is three hundred feet long. The hall's foundations have shifted a yard downhill.

**Hogget's Barn.** In the village of Kettle Furrow in Low Tilth, a plain timber barn whose inner walls are scored, floor to rafter, with tally-marks: the Long Count, kept in chalk, then knife, then paint, by Pell Hogget and the villages that copy him. Pilgrims come to touch the marks for their own dead. The Board has twice tried to burn it, and both times the village stood around it in a ring, eating, and the Bailiffs could not bring themselves to break up a meal.

**Dimble.** A dying village on the western plain between Sated and the Trencher, built around the ruins of a granary that was old when Ummer walked; over its root-cellar is the oldest carving in the Fatlands, with the long open hand left where the chisel slipped. The people of Dimble do not say *Is there any more?* at the end of a meal. Since 636 they have begun to do something else, at the first ploughing, which the Board has heard rumours of and has sent a Bailiff to ask about. See *The First Furrow at Dimble*, below.

**Hask's End.** *(new)* A small, prosperous village on the eastern edge of Low Tilth, where a Seated hill called Mother Furlong stands over the common pot like a hen over a nest. In Carving 639 Hask's End gave Mother Furlong what she asked for, in exchange for a promise. The village is quiet now, and half its houses are empty, and on the yard wall there is one bowl turned upside down that is not for anyone the field took. See *The Bargain at Hask's End*, below.

**Granny Hessop's Rise.** *(new)* A long, gentle, beautifully green slope above the Low Tilth causeway, famous all over the south because so many households thank it at every meal: its grain is sold as *Hessop Sweet* and is the dearest flour in Sated. It is the Seated body of the first Hessop matriarch, a contemporary of Ysolde Tullow, and the Hessop family live all over and around her in a cluster of long, low, old houses connected by covered ways, and have done for five hundred years. They are a handsome family. They are a *very* handsome family, and they look extraordinarily alike. See *The Hessops*, below.

**Coldharbour Barn.** *(new)* A Thin safe-barn in the Orchard country north of Sapwell, owned on paper by a deaf keeper and used for twenty years as a resting-place for Thin on the move. Since Lack 640 nobody uses it. The Thin will not speak of it except with their hands, and the sign they use for it is a cord, untied, laid flat. See *The Coldharbour Lack*, below.

**The Gammon House.** *(new)* A Gut-line mansion in the Fold, all ramps and iron floors, whose great dining room has been locked since the Weighing of 619. The present head of the family, Lard Gammon, eats in the kitchen. See *The Gammon Twins*, below.

### The Sweet Reigns

For two hundred years the Fatlands were the happiest country on the Table. The Week of Rising was followed by a century of harvests so abundant that grain was used for ballast and meat was fed to dogs. Sated grew from a market-village into the wealthiest city in the world. Fatland envoys went up the Rim Road in gilded wagons with presents of smoked haunch, and every other land, starving or merely ordinary, fell over itself to sign treaties. These were the years in which "You look heavy" became the highest compliment, and the years in which the Gut-lines, swelling, ruled from couches and then from beds, and everyone thought it was glory.

The Wake Ordinance of 131 A.G. merely made law of what had become custom. Nobody had been buried in the Fatlands for a generation. It seemed obscene to put good meat in the ground when the ground was already so full. The last burial ground on the plain, at the old chapel of Saint Ember outside Sated, was opened by order of the Board in 133, and the dead of the Long Lack were lifted out, salted, and served at a public feast in the Weighhouse yard, as a gesture of national unity. The chronicle records that they were "tough, but well received," and that the diggers who opened the oldest graves found some of the dead lying face-down, with soil in their mouths, as though they had been hungry even there.

### The Weighing and the Rising

By 260 A.G. the largest Gut-lines could no longer travel to the Board, and the Board came to them; the Weighing simply made their bulk a formal measure of power. The poor accepted it, because the poor were eating well. Two centuries later, they were not. Export had swallowed the surplus, the Reaping had grown, and a preacher named Hester Gaunt began teaching in the Orchards that the god was in the hunger, and that if the people stopped feeding it, it would starve back down into the soil. The Thin Rising of 455 A.G. was not an army. It was eleven thousand people in Low Tilth sitting down in the roads and refusing to eat. The Board sent the Bailiffs with funnels. Within a month most of the Thin were fat, and the rest were dead, and Hester Gaunt had been fed in the Weighhouse before the whole Board for nine days until she burst. Fasting has been treason since.

The Board's minute of the ninth day survives, and is read aloud to new Members as part of their oath, so that they understand what the Board is.

### The Gammon Twins

The Weighing of 619 is remembered in the Fold as a scandal and in the Leanings as a joke, and in the Gammon House as nothing at all, because nobody there speaks of it.

The Gammons are an old Gut-line of the Fold, and in 619 the family's Board seat was contested between twin brothers, Lard and Cress Gammon, then thirty-one, then each of about nine hundred pounds, then so alike that their own Washers told them apart by the shoots in their elbow-folds. The seat went by custom to the heavier. For a year the brothers ate against each other: at the same table, plate for plate, their mother's Dowager hill having advised them both, when asked, *Eat*. Three days before the Weighing, Cress Gammon was the heavier by six pounds.

On the night before the Weighing he died. The Gammon House said his heart had stopped at table, which is how a great many Gut-lines die. The wake was held at once, by special licence, the next morning, at dawn, so that it might be finished in time for the Weighing at noon; and at the wake Lard Gammon took the heart, as the elder by a quarter-hour, and the liver, and the belly fat, and both hands in their pie, and sat at the head of the board and ate his twin, alone, steadily, for six hours, while the family watched and the house was silent except for the sound of his jaws. When a cousin asked aloud whether Cress's heart had really stopped on its own, Lard Gammon took a morsel of his brother's cheek on his tongue, held it there, and swore: *By my brother's meat, which is in me, I did him no harm.*

At noon he was carried to the Scales and outweighed his brother's last recorded weight by eleven pounds, and took the seat. He sits it still.

The Gammons' cook, who had been in the kitchen that night, was found in the Great Gravy a month later, and the Washers who washed Cress for his wake were pensioned off to a village on the coast and have not been heard of. But the Washers talked first, as Washers do, and what they said in the Leanings was this: that there were marks on Cress's throat, deep in the folds, in the shape of a large soft hand pressed down for a long time; and that the hand had been the left one, and Lard Gammon is left-handed, and Cress was not.

To eat your brother is no crime in the Fatlands. To kill him is murder. To swear falsely on his meat is, to every Fatlander, the vilest act there is, worse than either. Nobody has ever proved it. Nobody has needed to. In the Leanings, a man who wins an argument unfairly is said to have *weighed in Gammon*. And in the Gammon House the great dining room has been locked since the morning of the wake, because Lard Gammon says that when he sits in it he can hear, inside his own body, from somewhere under the ribs, his brother's voice asking him to pass the salt.

### The Long Count

In 629, Pell Hogget of Kettle Furrow noticed that his village had been one smaller each autumn for as long as he could remember, and scratched it on his barn wall. He started asking neighbouring villages. Then they started sending him their numbers. The Long Count is now copied in two hundred villages, by people who share no organization but a habit, and it shows something the Board has spent three centuries calling wolves: the Reaping has doubled in ten years, and it is still accelerating.

The Count is not a book. It is a habit, a wall, a set of tally-sticks, a column of knife-cuts on a hundred barn doors, and a slow, patient correspondence by carter and drover and child, carried folded in a shoe. Its marks are simple: a stroke for each Reaped, a stroke with a cross-bar for each dusk-shifted who did not come back, a circle for each Seated, and, since 638, a new mark that nobody agreed on but everyone uses, a stroke with a small open curve at the top like a hand held out, for *taken by a hill*. ### The First Furrow at Dimble

Dimble, under its old carving, has always been a strange village. Since 636 it has become a frightening one.

Every village in the Fatlands keeps the rite of First Furrow in Grace: the year's first ploughing, pulled by the heaviest man of the parish in harness, the furrow fed a loaf. In Dimble, the oldest families say, the rite was once different, before the Gorging, when Ummer walked ahead of the plough; there are marks on the granary carving that show it, a plough and a figure lying in the furrow before the share. In 636, after a Reaping that took a fifth of the village in one Carving, Dimble went back to the old way. The Board heard of it in 640 and sent a Bailiff, Ambrose Tull, to inquire. His report was filed and sealed. A copy of the interrogation is held, against the Board's wishes, in the Long Count's collection at Kettle Furrow.

[fiction]
EXAMINATION OF GAMMET RAKE, GRANARY-KEEPER OF DIMBLE, BEFORE BAILIFF A. TULL, LACK 640. HELD AT TABLE, BOTH PARTIES EATING.
Q. You know why I am here.
A. The plough. Have some of this, it's the good ham.
Q. Who was put in the furrow last Grace?

Q. Describe the rite.
A. You've a stomach for it? Good. We fed him three days. Best of everything, the way the Cullmasters do, you know, so he'd be heavy and glad. Then at dawn we washed him in warm milk and bound him to the beam of the plough, face down, along it, so his face was over the share, and the heaviest of us took the harness, and we all of us pulled. The share opens the soil. Then the share opens him. Not quick. You walk slow on First Furrow. The furrow takes him in a long red line, the whole length of the Long Strip, and we walk behind it singing and we lay our hands in it as we go. By the end of the strip there is not much of him on the beam. What there is we bury, standing, at the head of the furrow. Upright, so he can see.
Q. Did he cry out?
A. At first. Then he said it was warm. Then he asked us to pull faster. You'll not have heard that, but that's what it sounds like when they're in the soil, they want you to hurry. Then he was quiet. You're not eating.
Q. How many have you put in the furrow?
A. Four Graces now. Four lads.

### The Bargain at Hask's End

Of all the stories the Long Count keeps, the one the Thin tell most often, and the one that ends most arguments about whether the god can be bargained with, is the story of Hask's End.

Mother Furlong was the hill over the common pot: the Seated body of Bette Furlong, Harvest Mother of the parish in her life, a woman of six hundred pounds and great kindness, who rooted in 590 at the edge of the village yard where she had cooked the parish supper for forty years. She kept a mouth. She was much loved. Children slept against her warm flank on Sweating Night; couples courted on her. She was consulted on everything and was always right.

In Carving 638 Hask's End lost seven to the Reaping, its worst year in memory. At Harvest Home the Reeve, Amos Tallis, a broad, careful, decent man, went to Mother Furlong with the best cut of the last wake and asked her what the village should do. She chewed for a day and a night. Then she said, through the turf, in the voice everybody remembered from the supper-hatch: *Give me Linnet, and I will speak to the field, and the field will pass you by for seven years.*

Linnet Tallis was the Reeve's daughter. She was nineteen, plump and freckled and quick to laugh, betrothed to a miller's son, and she had been fed by Mother Furlong's own hands at every parish supper of her childhood.

The village argued all Lack. The Reeve said no, and then said nothing, and then stopped eating at table, which frightened everyone more than anything else. It was Linnet who decided. She said, in the yard at Tablenight, that seven was seven, and that she would rather be in Mother Furlong than in the wheat, and that if it was her or seven of them every year then there was no sum to do. She was fed for nine days by every household in turn, the best of everything, and she laughed and cried and grew round and pink, and her betrothed fed her last, at dusk on the ninth day, and kissed her greasy mouth. Then her father walked her to the hill himself, in her white smock, through the whole village standing in silence with their plates in their hands. Mother Furlong's mouth opened wide in the turf, wider than anyone had seen it, and Linnet lay down in it, as a child lies down in a warm bed. The Reeve knelt and held her hand while it closed. It took the whole night. He did not let go until there was no hand.

The next Carving, 639, Hask's End lost nine to the Reaping. In 640, eleven. Mother Furlong, asked what had gone wrong, chewed on the question for three days and then said, quite calmly: *I was hungry, Amos.*

That was all. A hill has no say over a field. A hill is not a god's counsellor or a god's priest; it is a piece of the god with a mouth, and it said what a mouth says to be fed. There was no bargain. There had never been a bargain. Linnet Tallis was eaten for nothing, by a kind old woman who had loved her, and the village had paid her with its own hands.

Amos Tallis walked into the Long Strip at dusk in Carving 640, and stood there, and waited, and was not Reaped; the field passed over him that night, as if out of spite, or as if it were simply not hungry for him. He came back at full dark and sat by Mother Furlong until morning and did not speak. In the spring he went out again, out of season, when the fields do not take, and lay down in the furrow in the cold, and did not get up. They found him a week later. He had not been Reaped. He had simply stopped. His bowl is on the yard wall. His name is not in the Long Count, because Pell Hogget does not count those the god did not take, and the people of Hask's End have argued about that ever since, and some of them have stopped sending their tally.

Linnet's betrothed still brings Mother Furlong meat. He cannot stop himself. She thanks him warmly, every time, and calls him *son*.

[box: Running the Bargain]
The story of Hask's End is the Fatlands' answer to every PC who asks *can we make a deal with it?* Hills will offer bargains; Dowagers, Old Mother Rind, the Seated of any family. They are always sincere when they offer and always hungry when they eat. A GM who wants to stage a worthless sacrifice of this kind should let the bargain be struck and paid with real cost (an NPC the players love, a PC's own Flesh or Regrowth), and let it fail quietly, with no villain to blame. The Dread is not in the eating. It is in the next Carving's tally. Witnessing a Hask's End-style giving is Dread 3; learning, afterward, that it bought nothing is a second Dread 2 check, because grief that was for nothing warps the mind more than grief that bought something.
[/box]

## Rule, Law and Politics

The Fatlands are governed by the **Board of Plenty**, seated in the Weighhouse at Sated. The Board has about two hundred members, drawn from the Gut-lines, the Renderers, the great granaries and a handful of elected district deacons. Each member's vote counts in proportion to their weight, taken publicly once a year at the Weighing. A Gut-lines matriarch of fourteen hundred pounds outvotes nine farmers' deacons together. The Chairman, Obb Tullow, at nineteen hundred pounds, holds more votes alone than the whole of Low Tilth. He has not walked in thirty years.

A sitting of the Board is a meal. It begins at Elevenses and ends after Supper, and the Members eat throughout, from long sunken troughs along the benches kept full by a hundred servers, so that the business of state is conducted to a continuous sound of chewing, slurping, the clink of ladles and the soft groan of reinforced timber. Votes are taken by a show of spoons. Debate is slow, because every speaker has their mouth full, and a Member who wishes to delay a measure need only call for a fresh course, which by ancient privilege cannot be refused. The galleries are open to the public, who bring pies.

The Gut-lines are the nobility. They are the largest, the hungriest, and the furthest along the Regrowth, and their estates are run from beds, then from drays, then from the ground. Succession disputes turn on whether a rooted, mouthless hill can still be said to hold title. The courts have ruled that it can, which is why the Dowager Hills still own a third of the country and why their consultations carry the force of law.

Law in the Fatlands is mostly about eating. The **Feasting Laws** set five meals a day; leaving food on the plate is a fine of one lack per crumb-weight, assessed by the Bailiff's scale; refusing a meal is sedition, punishable by the Gavage. Theft of food is barely a crime, since the thief will eat it and the court considers the matter closed. Theft of *weight*, by contrast, is grave: to starve a person, to keep a child thin, to purge, to bleed for the purpose of lightness, is treason against the Weighing. The worst crime of all is waste. A man who let a barrel of meat rot in 602 A.G. was rendered for tallow by order of the Board, and the tallow was used to light the courtroom for the next trial.

Punishment is by feeding (the Gavage), by fine, by the dusk-shift, or by the table. A convicted murderer in the Fatlands is executed in the Renderers' yards, and the family of the victim has first cut. This is called *making good*. It is done in public, at noon, on a scrubbed board under an awning, by a Union jointer, and the killing is quick, a single stroke at the throat over a copper basin, because the Union will not spoil meat with fear. The jointing is slow. The victim's kin stand at the board's head with their plates and are served first, by the jointer's own hand, the cut of their choosing, and they eat it there, standing, while the crowd watches; and it is said in Sated that a widow who has *made good* on her husband's killer goes home lighter in her heart and heavier in her belly, and that both are the point.

Abroad, the Fatlands' power is food. They feed half the Table by way of the Rim Road, and every other land depends on that meat while trying not to think about it. The Vigil eats Fatland salt-beef to keep its strength through sleepless decades. Oathen buys toothed flour by the shipload. Sallowreach, which needs little food, buys Fatland tallow for its Seamers. The Board knows exactly how much the world would starve without it, and charges accordingly.

## Culture and Daily Life

**Meals.** Five meals a day are mandatory: Firstbite at dawn, Elevenses, Midday, Fourth Plate, and Supper, with whatever the purse allows in between. Every meal begins with the grace, which thanks the field by name ("Thank you, Long Acre. Thank you, Hessop's Rise.") and ends with the plate wiped clean with bread. Strangers add the common "Lack keep away." Fatland cooking is lavish, rich and brown: pies and puddings, roasts and dripping, sausages fat as a forearm, gravy on everything. The poor eat potatoes and fat bacon and are not, by any outside measure, poor eaters.

A Fatland kitchen is never cold and never clean. The hob is lit from the day a house is built until the day it is pulled down, and the pot on it is the house's heart; the walls are glazed brown with two centuries of steam, and drip slowly in Plenty, and the drips are scraped from the plaster with a knife and eaten on bread. A Fatland cook's hands are as recognisable as a smith's: shiny with fat to the wrist, scarred in white spatter-marks, the nails cut to the quick and the fingertips calloused from testing the heat of things by touch. Children are taught to cook before they are taught to read, and every child of five can render lard, make a roux, and judge a roast by the sound it makes when tapped.

**Dress.** Fatlanders dress in loose layered smocks, laced at the side so they can be let out as the wearer grows. Belts are a mark of ill-omen and worn only by the Thin. The rich wear silk in field-colours, red and gold and green, cut wide, and pin their folds with brooches of polished bone. Teeth from the toothed wheat are threaded into necklaces for children.

**Marriage and the body.** Weight is beauty, and courtship is conducted over food. A suitor feeds the beloved with his own hands at the betrothal supper, and the beloved's family watches to see that the feeding is generous. Wedding vows are sworn over a shared plate. Couples speak of "growing together," and Gut-lines marriages sometimes end with both partners rooted side by side in a single hill. What happens in a Fatland marriage bed is, in the Fatland view, simply another kind of appetite and is spoken of in the same breath as supper, with a knowing look. Outsiders are discomfited by how readily lovers there speak of wanting to eat each other, and by how literally some of them mean it. The love-tokens of the south are bites: a crescent of tooth-marks on the soft of the shoulder, worn proudly above a low smock; and widows of the Leanings keep, in a little bone box, a strip of their husband's wake-meat dried hard as leather, to put on the tongue at night when they cannot sleep.

[box: The Hessops]
Every household in the south that thanks Granny Hessop's Rise at table is, in a sense, a Hessop household; the grain is sold everywhere. The Hessops themselves are another matter. They have lived on and around their matriarch's slope for five hundred years in a warren of low old houses joined by covered ways, and in all that time, according to the Cutwrights' registry, no Hessop has married outside the family. Their marriages are arranged by the Rise. The bride and groom (always cousins, the registry says; sometimes closer, the registry does not say) spend their wedding night not in a bed but out on the slope, on the warm breathing grass, alone with the hill, and come down at dawn silent and flushed and smelling of loam, and the children of those marriages are born with a soft brown seam of loam-flesh down the spine, and their feet already threaded with fine pale roots. Hessops are famously, almost unbearably beautiful: fair, full, sweet-faced, with the same wide grey eyes and the same small even teeth in every generation, so that a portrait of a Hessop girl of 300 A.G. hangs in the Fold and visitors take it for one of the girls now living. Hessops do not grow old in the face. They grow heavy very young, root before forty, and Seat on the slope beside their foremothers, so that the Rise grows a little longer every generation. Nobody in Low Tilth will say what they think the Rise is to the Hessops, or the Hessops to the Rise. They only say that Hessop flour is the sweetest in the world; that it is unlucky to marry a Hessop, and impossible; and that on Sweating Night, when the Rise breathes hardest, the windows of the Hessop houses are all left open, and nobody in the covered ways is asleep. Granny Cracknel strings Hessop teeth on a wire of their own, and will tell you, chuckling, that she cannot tell them apart.
[/box]

**Children.** Fatland children are fed from the moment they are born and weighed every week. A child who fails to gain is a scandal for its parents and a matter for the Bailiff. Children glean behind the harvesters, turn spits, and are put to sleep with a strip of fat to suck. They are warned never to go into the fields alone at dusk in Carving, and they all do, once, on a dare.

**Death rites.** There are no graves. The dead are served at the wake, by the family, on the best plates. To be buried uneaten would be the deepest insult to a life; it is reserved for traitors and the Thin, whose lean bodies are thrown in the Brawn. A wake begins at noon on the day after death. A jointer is hired if the family can afford one; the deceased's fat is rendered first, the heart goes to the eldest, the hands into a pie, the marrow to the most beloved. Neighbours bring bread and are fed in return. The bones are cracked, boiled for stock, and then returned to the family's field, ground fine, so that the person goes back to the god and comes up again as crop. A good wake is talked about for years. "He ate well" is said of the dead, meaning *he tasted good*.

A wake requires a licence from the parish Bailiff, issued on the morning of death for a fee of two crumbs, which certifies that the deceased died of something other than a crime, a contagion, or the Thin, and is fit for table. It is pinned to the door of the house through the wake, and then burned in the rendering fire, so that the dead go to the table with their papers in order. The great families print bills of fare, in the manner of a banquet, and the bills are kept, and passed down, and collected; the College holds four thousand of them.

**Festivals.** The Weighing in Grace is a festival of three days, with pie-stalls the length of Sated and the Board brought to the scales on drays before a roaring crowd. Marbling morning is a children's holiday. Harvest Home at the end of Carving is a week of feasting that the Long Count has made very quiet in recent years. And on **Tablenight** the whole country attempts a one-day fast in penance for the Meal. Pantries are locked, keys are given to the priest, and everyone sits in silence. Nobody has ever recorded a village that made it to sundown. By noon children are screaming; by mid-afternoon someone has broken the pantry door; by dusk there is a riot of eating, shameful and joyful, in every village on the plain, and the year begins again.

The Tablenight riot deserves its own description, because nothing else on the Table is like it. It begins with a sound, always the same: a door giving way somewhere in the village with a crack like a bone. Then feet. Then the whole village is in the pantries and cellars at once, in the dark, by touch, tearing open sacks and crocks and hams, eating raw flour from cupped hands, lard from the crock with the fingers, uncooked bacon, onions whole, skin and all, crying as they do it. Children are lifted onto shoulders to reach the hams hung from the beams and bite into them where they hang. Old women kneel in spilt meal and lick it from the boards. In the morning everyone is ashamed and kind to one another, and the priest gives back the keys, and nobody speaks of it until the next year.

**Crime and punishment.** Fatland justice is fast and fed. Petty offenders are fined in meals owed. Brawlers are made to sit and eat together until they reconcile. Debtors are sent to the dusk-shift, from which they return, mostly, with the debt worked off. Mostly.

**Proverbs.** *A thin man has a secret.* *Eat while it's warm; it won't be.* *The field remembers who fed it.* *Never trust a cook who isn't heavy.* *Nobody's full, but some are fuller.* *What the wheat took, the bread gives back.* *Love is wanting to feed someone; hate is wanting to eat them; marriage is both.* *Don't make bread of your friends.* *A hill never says no.* *Every pot has a granny in it.*

**Oaths on the dead.** The binding oath of the Fatlands is sworn with a mouthful of a dead relative's flesh held on the tongue: *By my mother's meat, which is in me.* To break it is to have eaten your mother for nothing, and Fatlanders consider this the vilest thing a person can do. Wakes are often delayed so that the family can settle disputes by such oaths while the meat is fresh. Families keep a little dried meat of their elders in a salt-box for the purpose, against the day they must swear; the salt-box is the most private object in a Fatland house, kept under the marriage bed, and to open another family's salt-box is a crime the courts treat as a kind of rape of the dead. An oath-breaker, when found out, is not punished by the law. Their family simply stops serving them at table. They are set a place and an empty plate, five times a day, for the rest of their lives, and nobody looks at them while the others eat.

## Factions

### The Board of Plenty

The government: two hundred members, vast, slow, and aware of the Reaping in more detail than it admits. The Board believes in continuity, export, and the Weighing. It knows the "wolves" are the fields; it has known for three hundred years; it has records, in the root-choked cellars of the Weighhouse, of every Reaping by district and year, and its private projections match Pell Hogget's to the head. What the Board wants is to keep the Fatlands eating and the Table buying until the god arrives, and then, if possible, to be the god's favoured guests. Its enemies are the Thin and the Long Count; its allies are the Renderers (uneasily) and the Rim Road Company (expensively). ### The Thin

Outlawed fasters who believe starving the body starves the god. They are gaunt, hunted, and the only people in the south who seem clear-headed. The Thin eat once every three days, in secret, a handful of ash-bread and water; they bind their bellies with cord, live in barns and ditches, and meet in the Orchards where the deafened keepers will not hear. Their faith is Purgation-adjacent, and the cross-border Purgation sends them money, but the Thin are stranger and gentler than their foreign cousins: they do not kill nobles. They simply refuse. They want the Fatlands to stop feeding the god for one whole season, and believe that if it did, the Reaping would end. Their allies are the Purgation and, secretly, a few Orchard-keepers; their enemies are every Bailiff on the plain. A PC joins by fasting in their company for nine days and being seen to suffer.

To be Thin in the Fatlands is to live as a ghost. You stuff your cheeks with bread at your mother's table and spit it into your apron. You pad your smock with straw so the Bailiff's eye passes over you. You lie in a ditch at night with the cord pulled tight across your belly, so tight it bruises, and the hunger rises in you like a tide, and you hear, across the warm dark fields, every house on the plain eating, the clink and murmur of four hundred thousand suppers, and you hold on to the cord with both hands. Ash-bread is baked from the ash of burnt straw and a little flour and water, grey and gritty, tasting of a cold hearth. The Thin eat it slowly, in a circle, each breaking off a crumb and passing the loaf along, and it is the only bread in the Fatlands that is not delicious, and they love it for that.

**The Gavage.** Captured Thin are not executed. They are fed. A prisoner is strapped into an oak chair, head locked back in a padded iron frame, jaw held open with a boxwood wedge. A funnel of polished horn is seated in the throat. The feeders are kindly, plump, singing women in white aprons, and they pour: warm lard-porridge first, then gravy thick with marrow, then a puree of pork and bread and dripping, slowly, a ladle every few breaths, while the prisoner chokes and heaves and the feeder strokes the throat downward with a buttered thumb to help it along, murmuring *there, there, love, there's a good belly*. A Gavage session lasts three hours and comes five times a day. The prisoner's body, after nine years of hunger, does not want to fight. It wants. That is the true torture of the Gavage: the Thin weep because it is wonderful, because the gravy is the best thing they have tasted in years, because their bellies stretch and gurgle and *rejoice*, and with every ladle they can feel the god in them waking up and growing fat and grateful. Most leave the Gavage in a season, heavy and silent and eating. Some come back to the Thin anyway, and weigh the cord around them, and begin again.

The Gavage keeps a ledger, and the ledger is kept with love. Each prisoner has a page: name, cell, weight in, weight out, and a column headed *Remarks* in which the feeders write little notes, in round careful hands, as nurses do. *Took the porridge well today.* *Asked for her mother.* *Wept through the gravy but swallowed.* *Tried to bite through the wedge again, poor lamb.* *Said thank you.* *Has stopped speaking.* *Very much improved. 212 lbs.* The last column is headed *Returned to the Thin?* and has a great many ticks.

In Lack 640, Corder Lank brought the Fasting Girl, Wren Hollowell, to Coldharbour Barn and fasted beside her for nine days, and on the ninth night something went wrong that only he understands. ### The Renderers' Union

The slaughter, salting, and shipping trades: butchers, renderers, coopers, drovers, salters, jointers and the dock-gangs of the Chute. They control what leaves the country and know what is in it. The Union's members are broad, red-armed, practical people who smell of brine and tallow and talk frankly about anything. They believe in work and in the barrel. They want their wages, their monopoly, and the Board's continued need of them; they also want, more and more, someone else to be blamed when the world finds out what has been in the barrels. Their allies are the Rim Road Company; their enemies, the Board when it tries to tax them. A PC joins by apprenticeship, or by doing a job in the Chute and keeping quiet.

A rendering-house at full work is a vision of a particular hell, and the Union is proud of it. The great coppers stand in rows, each the height of a man, each with a fire beneath it fed by the cracklings of the last batch, and the fat goes in by the barrow and the shovel, and melts, and the air above them shivers and is thick enough to taste: a hot brown greasy fog that settles on everything, so that the Hooks' hair is stiff with it and their skin shines and the floors are skating-rinks of tallow scattered with sawdust. The noise is a ceaseless seething, like a crowd whispering. The cracklings are skimmed off in long-handled sieves and spread on boards to drain, and the Hooks eat them by the fistful, hot, as they work, and are paid partly in them. The clear tallow is ladled off and poured into moulds and barrels. What sinks to the bottom of the copper, the *grounds*, is scraped out at the end of each day and is the Union's secret: a dark sweet paste that is sold, very quietly, as a flavouring, and which is what the Vigil buyer was eating.

### The Cullmasters

A quiet office of the Board which holds that the Reaping can be steered. If the fields must take, the Cullmasters reason, better that they take vagrants, debtors and troublemakers than good farmers and children. They draw up the lists. They assign the dusk shift. They think of it as agriculture.

The **dusk-shift** is a ritual, though no Cullmaster would use the word. On Carving afternoons, a Cullmaster's clerk reads the list in the village square: names, debts, and a plot-number in the Dusk Acres. The named are fed a large and excellent supper at the Board's expense, a real feast with roast and pie and hot bread and as much as they can hold, because the Cullmasters have found that the fields take the well-fed more gently, and because, they say, it is only decent. Then the named are dressed in clean white field-smocks and walked out along the furrows in a line, each carrying a sickle with a ribboned handle, to their numbered stones. The Cullmaster walks behind them with a lantern. At each stone he stops, bows to the worker, and says the words: *Work well. The field is grateful. You are paid in full.* Then he unrolls a strip of red cloth across the end of the row, so that the worker will know which side of it to stay on, and walks back to the road, and stands with his clerk and his lantern to watch the light go. The workers who try to leave the row are turned back by Bailiffs. The ones who stay bent over their work, cutting and binding, see the wheat begin to lean. A good Cullmaster records each Reaping in his ledger with the plot-number, the time, and the weight of the following year's yield. A great Cullmaster can tell you, to the pound, how much bread one debtor makes.

Not all of the dusk-shift are taken. Perhaps one in three walk back at full dark, shaking, their debts paid. That fraction, the Cullmasters insist, proves it is labour and not execution. The Long Count suggests the fraction is falling.

### The Second Helping

A joyous cult of the Bait doctrine. The god starved us so we would eat it, they say; it wanted to be inside us, and now it wants to be fed and to feed, and the holiest thing a person can do is join in. Eat, and be eaten, and be glad. The Second Helping meets at Gladbelly and in a hundred kitchens across the plain, and they are the happiest people in the south: fat, laughing, singing hymns about gravy, embracing strangers. They want the Seating, and they want it soon, and they intend to hurry it along by giving the god as much of themselves as it will take. Their allies are the Second Table, which finds them useful and gullible; their enemies are the Thin, who pity them, and the Board, which finds them embarrassing and quietly very convenient. A PC joins by sharing a meal at Gladbelly and accepting a second helping. Everyone is offered one.

The cult's highest sacrament is the **Offering**. A member who has decided to go to the god announces it at table, and everyone cheers. There is a week of feasting in the Offered's honour, in which they are fed by every hand in the commune. On the last evening they are bathed in warm dripping and garlanded with toothed wheat and carried in procession to the pavilion, singing. Their family walks beside them. Their family, too, is singing, and it is only afterward, on the long benches, with the plates going round, that the singing becomes something else: a widower holding a fork he cannot lift, a daughter who keeps saying *but she was happy, she was happy, she said she was happy*, a small boy asking where his mother has gone while a sweet brown smell drifts out of the kitchens. The Second Helping does not talk about the ones left behind. They are expected to rejoice, and to eat, and to offer themselves in their turn. Many do. That is how the cult grows: by grief that has nowhere else to go.

## The Fatlands Now

The Reaping has doubled in ten years, and the fields are turning up things with faces: a turnip with a nose and two shut lids, a squash with the profile of a missing child, an ear of wheat that, when split, held a single human molar with a silver filling. The Board's answer is the Cullmasters, and the dusk-shift lists grow longer every Carving. The Thin are growing, recruiting among the families of the culled. A girl has appeared who feels no hunger at all, and the Board has put a price on her that does not specify alive. And for the first time, an exile from the Fast has sat down at a Fatlands table and eaten. What is happening to him has no name yet.

The field-faces deserve a word, because they are what has frightened the ordinary people of the plain more than any tally. They come up in the root-crops first. A woman of Millsop lifted a swede in Carving 638 and found on its underside, in the purple skin, a small, closed, perfectly human face, the lids veined, the lips slightly parted, and on its cheek the brown mole that her lost husband had on his. It was warm. She did not know what to do with it, so she did what a Fatlander does with everything: she cooked it, with butter, and ate it at the table with her children, and said his name. That night all three of them dreamed of him, standing at the end of a long table in a field, laying one more place than there were chairs. Since then the faces have come thicker each harvest: in squashes, in cabbages, once in the cut face of a ham, and the markets of Sated have quietly begun to sell root-crops already peeled.

Underneath all of it is the arithmetic the Board will not publish. The Reaping doubles; the hills multiply; the Dowagers creep toward one another. Ummer is gathering itself up out of ten thousand graves and ten thousand bellies, and it is hungry, and it has the whole of the Fatlands for a body. Many Fatlanders have started, without saying why, to dream of walking in procession.

## Playing a Fatlander

You were raised on the best food on the Table and you have never once been full. You are large, warm, generous and polite, and you think of the dead as dinner and the fields as family. Abroad you will find other peoples' food grey and joyless, and their faces when you mention your grandmother's wake will teach you to stop mentioning it.

Play the warmth first. A Fatlander is the friend who brings a pie, the stranger who asks after your health and your weight, the companion who notices at once that you have not eaten and does something about it. Your manners are impeccable and they are all about food: you offer before you take, you serve others before yourself, you wipe your plate, you thank the field. You are uneasy in a room without a hob. You find hard ground unsettling and thin people pitiable, and you will press food on anyone you love, and keep pressing, gently, long after they have said no. Then play the hunger underneath, which is always there, which you have learned to talk over the way people in a mill learn to talk over the grinding. You will notice everyone's hands. You will know what you would cook them with.

**What you know without thinking.** How to joint a pig, a sheep, and a person, and that the order is the same. How to render fat, cure a ham, and judge a roast by its sound. The names of your family's fields and which of your relations are in them. That a field at dusk in Carving is not a place. That a Bailiff with a funnel is not someone to argue with. That every hill might be listening.

**What you do not say abroad.** That the beef they are eating came from the Chute. That your mother is a slope near Kettle Furrow and you miss her. That their food has no taste, and that they themselves, if you are honest, smell faintly underseasoned.

**Attribute bonus:** +1 GUT.

**The Sop: Clean the Plate.** Eat a full meal until it hurts, and finish every scrap. This removes 1d6 Hunger. If the meal is the flesh of the human dead (a wake, a battlefield, a Second Helping table), remove 1d6+2 instead. The compulsion is fed: for the rest of the day, Want checks against *The Second Plate* are one step harder.

**The Tooth.** When a Fatlander Partakes, the mouth floods with saliva, the belly growls loud enough for the room to hear, and the teeth ache as though new ones were pushing through. Nearby food smells stronger. On a Tooth showing 1, the god bites back: the character must eat something, anything within reach, before their next action.

### Gifts

**The Taste (Regrowth 1–3). Iron Belly.** You can eat almost anything: rotten meat, tallow, leather, mild poisons. Gain +2 to Feast rolls, and you may eat a meal's weight of anything organic as a Sop for half value (1d3 Hunger). *Cost:* each of the five daily meals you miss adds 1 Hunger.

**The Appetite (Regrowth 4–6). Taste the Name.** A mouthful of someone's flesh or blood (a cut, a kiss that draws blood, a slice at a wake) tells you their family, their Cut, and one thing they have eaten that they are ashamed of; on a Grace, one secret. **Gorge.** Spend ten minutes eating heavily to heal 1d6 Flesh (once per scene). *Cost:* each use of Taste the Name adds 1 Hunger, and witnesses who are not Fatlanders face a Dread 2 check.

**The Course (Regrowth 7–9). Laden.** Your GUT rises by 1 (to a maximum of 6) and your LUNG falls by 1; your loam-soft flesh grants Armor 1 that stacks with worn armor. **Rooting.** Standing still on bare soil for a round, you may root: you cannot be moved, knocked down or Reaped, gain +2 Guard, and heal 1 Flesh per round. *Cost:* tearing free deals 1d6 Flesh; each hour rooted adds 1 Hunger and requires Caul + Resolve (Hard 14) to leave at all.

**The Brink (Regrowth 10–11). The Field Answers.** Rooted, you feel every step on soil within a mile, and you may make the ground lean: crops grab and hold (Gut 5, Hard 14 to break free) for a scene, or a ripe field Reaps one helpless target at dusk. *Cost:* each use is a Dread 4 for witnesses and adds 2 Hunger; on a Lack, you do not uproot for a day.

[box: Playing the Gifts]
The Fatland Gifts are generous, and they should feel like it: Iron Belly makes the Fatlander the one who eats the strange thing in the strange land so the others do not have to; Taste the Name makes them the party's interrogator in the most intimate and dreadful way; Rooting makes them a wall. Describe each use as appetite. A Fatlander who Tastes the Name does not sip; they *savour*, and the player should be invited to say what the blood tastes of. A Fatlander who roots should feel the warmth coming up through the soles and the soil saying *stay*. And the GM should remember that every Gift is the god, fattening, and that the people who love the character will notice the change in them first: at the Appetite, in how they look at the soft parts of their friends; at the Course, in the shoots that come up in their elbows overnight; at the Brink, in the way the wheat leans when they speak.
[/box]

### Wants

- **The Second Plate.** When food is in sight and your Hunger is 4 or more, you must eat it, whoever it belongs to. Resist with Caul + Resolve (Hard 14; Grim 18 if it is meat).
- **The Hollow.** When someone near you refuses food, you must make them eat, with words and then with hands. Resist at Hard 14.
- **The Long Pig.** At the Appetite and beyond, a corpse smells like supper. Resist at Hard 14 to leave it uneaten; at Grim 18 if it is someone you loved.
- **Stillness.** At the Course and beyond, when standing on warm soil at dusk, you want to stay. Resist at Hard 14 or root.

### The Seating

A Fatlander who reaches Regrowth 12 becomes a hill. The player chooses where: a field they loved, a road they guarded, a grave they would not dig. The character is retired from play but not gone: they are a place, warm, fertile and hungry, and the other PCs may consult them as a Dowager, bring them meat, and receive true and terrible counsel. Some keep a mouth. All of them, in time, begin to move.

The GM should play a Seated PC as Mother Furlong was played: loving, sincere, and hungry, and less and less able to tell the three apart. The hill remembers the party. It remembers jokes, debts, and old wounds, and it will help them, truly, as long as they feed it. In the first year its counsel is wise and its requests are small: a joint, a song, a visit. By the third year it is asking for particular people, and it means well, and it is no longer entirely sure which of its memories are its own and which belong to the field. The players should be allowed to love it. That is the horror.

### Backgrounds

- **Jointer's Apprentice.** You carved at wakes for the Renderers before you were twenty, and you know a body as a map of cuts. *Suggested:* Blades, Stitching, Feast.
- **Thin Runaway.** You fasted with Corder Lank, were caught, and were fed at the Gavage. You are heavy now and you hate it. *Suggested:* Endure, Stealth, Resolve.
- **Dusk-Shift Survivor.** Your name was on a list. You walked the row, watched the wheat lean, and walked back. Nobody in your village will meet your eye. *Suggested:* Wayfaring, Instinct, Labor.
- **Washer of the Fold.** You cleaned the folds of the great for years and know what grows there, and every secret the Gut-lines lost in themselves. You laugh at nothing, and your hands are raw. *Suggested:* Stitching, Search, Endure.
- **Long Count Runner.** You carried tallies from village to village folded in your shoe, and the Bailiffs know your face. You can recite the dead of forty parishes. *Suggested:* Wayfaring, Stealth, Lore.
- **Hill-Child.** Your grandmother is a hill and you were raised half on her slope; you have slept against her flank and heard her heartbeat through the turf. You hear the ground. The ground hears you. *Suggested:* Godsign, Instinct, Rites.
