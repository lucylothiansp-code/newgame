## Prologue: The Night of Seven Tables

Nobody agrees whether it was one night or a whole black season of nights. Every people remembers it as one. These are seven of the memories, set down in the words of the people who keep them, and arranged in the order the Rim Road Company's chroniclers prefer, which is clockwise, ending at the middle, because that is where every road on the Table ends whether it means to or not.

Each was taken down by the College's Linemen between 140 and 160 A.G., from the oldest tellers then alive, who had it from their grandparents, who had it from their own mouths. They have been copied, recopied and argued over since. None of them agrees with the official chronicle of its own land in every particular. All of them agree with each other on the one point that matters: the gods did not fight.

[pull] Every people remembers it as one night. Every people is lying, and every people is right. | — Archprovost Hesper Lune, lecture at Quire, 629 A.G.

### I. The Fens

[fiction]
Nobody wanted to be first. That is the whole of it, and the fen-folk tell it as if it were a virtue.

Ossel lay where it had lain down, half in the black water and half on the reed-bank below the place that would one day be Lastgate. It was grey the way fog is grey, long the way a road is long, and its hands lay open on the peat, palms up, each one wide enough to cup a cottage. We stood around it with knives we had ground from the shoulder-blades of cattle that had died in the fifth year. There were thousands of us. You could hear stomachs. You could hear the fog dripping off the reeds.

Every so often, while we stood there, one of the children at the back of the crowd died. That was ordinary then. You heard a mother make the sound and then stop making it. And each time, one of those great hands lifted, slow as a heron, reached over our heads without touching anyone, and closed the child's eyes with a fingertip the size of a loaf. Then it settled back onto the peat and lay open again.

It did that eleven times before anyone moved.

We have kept the count. Every fen child can still say it on the fingers, the eleven closings, the way children elsewhere count buttons: *Wenna, small Hob, the Tasker twins, Arn's girl, the one with no name yet*, and on through the eleven. There were twelve children and eleven closings, because the Tasker twins died in the same breath holding hands, and the Hand closed both pairs of eyes with one fingertip laid across them like a bar across a door. The mothers did not scream. The famine had taken the screaming out of us in the fifth year. The sound the mothers made was short, and dry, like a latch going down.

The one who moved was Agna Corse, who was ninety and had buried four husbands and nine children and was dying where she stood. She did not cut the god. She asked her grandson to. She said, I will not last till morning and I would like to have eaten something. He took the smallest finger of the left hand, the last joint, and it came away like the stalk of a mushroom, and inside it was cold and pale and wet and smelled of cellars and turned earth and the inside of a closed box. She ate it raw, with her gums. She did not die that night. She did not die the next night either. She is, by some accounts, still upright in a Lastgate loft, and asks after her grandson, who has been sunk in the Sump for five hundred years.

After her it went easier. It always does. We ate it slowly, because it was cold and because we were ashamed, and because every time a knife went in the hand nearest would lift a little, as if to close our eyes, and then think better of it and lie down. The families who took the hands sat apart and ate them in silence, joint by joint, cracking the knuckles with stones. Their lips went grey by morning. We bowed to them for it.

The meat of the Hand was not like other meat. It was cold all the way through, cold as a cellar stone, and it did not warm in the mouth however long you held it there, and those who ate a great deal of it found in the morning that their own mouths had gone cold and stayed cold, and that their breath no longer smoked in the frost. The bones of the fingers had more joints than a man's, nine and ten to a finger, and each joint came apart with a small wet click, like a box being shut. The people who ate the long meat of the arm said it tasted of nothing at all for a long while and then, at the very back of the tongue, of the moment just before sleep.

There was one boy, they say, a peat-cutter's son, who stood at the back the whole night and never ate. He was staring past the god, over its shoulder, at the empty bank on the far side of the fire, where nobody stood because there was nothing there to stand near. Just before dawn he made a small sound, and turned, and ran out into the fen in the dark, and we heard the water take him. Nobody went after him. We were eating.

When it was done there was a long grey shape of bones lying in the reeds, and the hands were gone, all but the last joint of the left thumb, which nobody would take. It lay on the peat until dawn. In the morning it was found curled in on itself, closed, as if it had been holding something very small and had decided to keep it.
[/fiction]

### II. The Plains

[fiction]
We were fastest. Say that too. Say it proudly; we do.

Ummer the Laden came in off the stubble on the last evening of the ninth Carving and lay down on the great threshing floor at Brawn Tilth like an ox that has decided. It was the size of a barn and it smelled the way an oven smells when you open it, and the starving went toward that smell before anyone had spoken a word, on their hands and knees, the old ones crawling, the young ones running on legs like rake-handles.

There was no ceremony. There was no first cut, because there were a thousand first cuts. People bit it. I want you to understand: before the knives came out, people had their faces in its side and were biting, and the skin gave like the crust of a loaf, and under the crust it was fat, yellow, a hand's depth of it, warm as a body, and under the fat it was meat that bled gravy. Children were pushed in to the shoulder. Men pulled ribs apart with their arms and handed the meat back over their heads to whoever was behind. A woman sat in the hollow of its flank with her baby and chewed and put the chewed meat in the baby's mouth with her tongue, chewed and gave, chewed and gave, crying the whole time, and the baby lived, and that baby's grandchildren's grandchildren sit on the Board of Plenty today.

The Gut-line families got there first because they were nearest the belly. They opened it and grain came out. That is what is remembered: the belly opened and the threshing floor was knee-deep in grain in a heartbeat, a wave of it, hot, smelling of summer, and in that grain were small white things like teeth, which we took for husks. We ate those too. Nobody asked.

A drover called Kell Sowerby ate until his belly split along an old rupture-scar, and he went on eating with the grain running out of the wound into his lap, laughing, crying, and his wife sat behind him and stitched him shut with a harness needle and waxed thread while he ate, and pushed the grain back in with her thumbs, and he lived to be ninety and never once felt full. His grandchildren show the needle at weddings.

By midnight the big bones were bare. By the second hour we were cracking them on the floor-stones for the marrow, and the noise of that was like a forest coming down. By dawn there was nothing on the threshing floor but grease and grain and people asleep in it, curled up, fat-faced, with their mouths still moving. The god had not made a sound. It had not stopped us. Old women say its eyes were open the whole time, and kind, and that it watched us eat it the way a mother watches.

The hunger did not stop. We noticed that around noon. We were full to the throat and the hunger had not stopped. It still has not.
[/fiction]

The threshing floor at Brawn Tilth is the place the south now calls the Trencher. Nobody farms within a mile of it. The stones are still greasy.

[pull] We ate the god and the god watched us do it, kindly, the way a mother watches. That is the part I cannot forgive it for. | — Grace-Keeper Amity Rowle of Dimble, 590 A.G.

### III. Longslab

[fiction]
Dromm would not come up on its own. The others lay down; the Fathom had to be fetched.

It took ten thousand hooks. Every family on the coast brought every hook it owned and hammered more from door-hinges and pot-handles, and we rowed them out over the deep place in boats with nothing in their holds, and we set them, and we hauled. The ropes sang. Men's hands opened to the bone on them and they wrapped the bone in sailcloth and hauled again. It came up out of the dark slowly over three days, a shape like a reef that breathed, and it did not fight us, but the sea did: the water around it thickened until boats could not row through it, and the pressure of it pressed our ears until they bled in threads down our jaws.

On the second day the hooks began to bring up the drowned. Every body the coast had ever rowed out to the Fathom came up on the barbs, caught in the god's hide where it had kept them: some only bone wrapped in weed, some white and soft as dough and swollen out of their clothes, some fresh as the week they were let down, with the lantern-wax still on their faces. A woman at the third rope saw her husband come up, nine years drowned and not a day changed, his eyes open, his hair moving in the water as though he were nodding. She did not let go of the rope. Nobody let go. We hauled them up with the god, our dead, and laid them out on the shingle in rows, and when we were hungry enough, later, we did not look at the rows.

We dragged it onto the shingle at Longslab on the third evening. It was grey and slick and vast and its skin moved in long slow ripples like the surface of the water it had left. It had no face you could find. It had an enormous slow breathing, a sound like surf turned inside out, and every breath it took pulled the tide a little further out from under the boats.

It took four days to stop moving. We did not wait for it to stop. We were too hungry for that. The families who would be the Lung-lines cut their way in under the ribs on the second day and found the lungs, which were the size of chapels, folded and grey and full of cold water, and they ate them in the water, standing in the god, chest-deep in the brine that poured out of it. One girl put her whole head inside a lobe to drink and came out and said she had heard the sea from the inside, and that it was talking. She walked into the water a week later, the first of all the Called, though nobody had a name for it then.

The beach has never dried. Stand on Longslab in the hottest week of Plenty and the shingle is wet under your soles and the wet is warm. On the fourth night, when it finally stopped moving, every one of us woke at the same moment coughing salt water. We thought it was grief.
[/fiction]

[pull] The sea gave back every one of them that night. It has not given back one since. | — Lowmark tide-post inscription, cut 118 A.G.

### IV. Ludmere

[fiction]
Hush. That was the whole instruction. Hush, and do not wake it.

Iss the Lidded slept in the high valley above Ludmere, where it had always slept, curled on its side like a hound, as long as the valley, with its head on its paws and its great lids shut. It had never been seen awake. Its breathing was the weather of that country: slow, warm, regular, the grass along the valley floor lying down and rising up again with each breath. We had slept beside it all our lives, and dreamed well, and in the famine years we had dreamed of food, nightly, until the dreams were the only food we had.

So we came up the valley in the dark, in stocking feet, with our knives wrapped in wool so they would not clink. Mothers sang. That is what we kept from that night: the lullabies. A thousand women singing low, so the god would go on sleeping while we opened it. The knives went in to the singing. The blood came out warm and slow and smelled of linen and milk and the hollow of a lover's neck, and the people who drank it yawned, all of them, and had to be slapped awake to go on eating.

The song was the old one, the cradle-song every Ludmere child went down to: *Lie down, lie down, the Lid is low*. They sang it in shifts, the old ones taking up the line when the young ones' voices went, so that it never broke, not once, not for the whole of the work, which the Vigil's chronicle now says took nine nights and which felt, to the singers, like one. Some of the singers fell asleep singing, and their neighbours sang on over them.

It never woke. Write that down and underline it. It never stirred, never flinched, never sighed. We took the flesh from the haunch and it went on breathing. We took the heart and it went on breathing for an hour without one. Near dawn the families who would be the Eye-lines climbed the face with ropes and cut out its eyes, still shut, lids and lashes and all, each one the size of a cartwheel, and lowered them down, and sliced them like bread, and ate them closed. The lids were the best part, they said after. Like cream.

Some of us dozed, toward the end, full and warm against the cooling side. And some of those who dozed dreamed of a long dim room, and woke weeping, and could not say why. We put it down to the occasion.

A girl of six who had been left to sleep in the grass under the god's chin was found at dawn curled against its last warm breath and could not be woken for three days. When she woke she asked her mother where the other people in the long room had gone, the ones who had been sitting at the table with their backs to her. Her mother told her it was a dream. She grew up and kept a dream-book in the temple of the walkers-softly, and that is where the College found this telling.

The breathing stopped at sunrise. The valley grass lay down flat, and has never quite stood up again.
[/fiction]

### V. The Red Hills

[fiction]
Vey was the smallest of them and it would not stay killed. Every midwife in the highlands was called up to the round hill to help, and the midwives tell it best, so this is how they tell it.

It was the size of a calf when it lay down, a soft red thing with a hundred small closed openings in its skin like buds, and its warmth came off it in waves you could see in the frost. A Caul-wife of Kest put her knife in under the breastbone, cleanly, the way you open a sheep, and it died. And then it opened. The wound became a mouth, and the mouth drew breath, and out of it slid Vey again, smaller, wet, and new, and lay down on the grass beside its own body and waited.

We killed it seven times that night. Each time we killed it, it began again out of the death, out of the cut, out of the place where the knife came out. By the fourth time we were eating as we killed, fast, so that what we had cut would be swallowed before it could begin. By the sixth the women were holding the openings shut with their hands while the men cut, and the openings pushed at their palms like a child's head pushing. The meat was hot and sweet and tasted of iron and new milk, and those who ate it felt their own bodies clench and loosen and clench in the belly, all of them, men and grandmothers, a pain like a door being tried from the other side.

Every Vey that came out of a death was born with its arms spread wide, like a small figure standing in a doorway to keep it open. The midwives say it never once cried. It looked at whoever was nearest, each time, with an expression the oldest of them called *patience*, and lay down again to be cut.

The seventh time it was very small. The size of a hand. A woman named Harrow Bede, who had lost nine in the Lack and buried every one in the red clay with her own fingers, took it up in her palm and looked at it for a long while. Then she put the whole of it in her mouth and swallowed, and held her jaw shut with both hands until it stopped moving, and then she sat down in the clay and laughed until she was sick.

The families who ate the caul wore it out of the hill on their faces like veils, wet and red in the torchlight, and it did not come off in the morning. Behind us, on the empty hilltop, a door stood in a frame with no house around it, open a crack. Nobody had brought it there.
[/fiction]

The families of Kest tell it differently: a birthing-house instead of a round hill, a drift of small gods across the floor instead of seven deaths, and a woman named Hollan Kest who crushed the last of them, the size of a barleycorn, under her heel. The midwives of the round hill say the Kest families were not there, and that a thing told by people who were not there is a thing that grows. The Attendance, which descends from both, teaches both, and asks its apprentices to notice that in neither telling did anyone manage to stop it beginning.

### VI. The Canyons

[fiction]
...

Nothing.

They ate it without speaking, and the telling of it in Oathen is also without speaking, so this is set down by an outsider who was told it with hands.

Tolm the Witness lay at the bottom of the deepest canyon with its ear to the stone. It was long, and bare, and listening. It was always listening; everything said in front of it was so, and the people of the canyons had learned over ten thousand years to say little near it and to mean all of it. In the Lack they had come to its ear and said we are hungry, and it was so; they had said we will die, and that was so; and they had stopped coming.

On the night, the whole people came down the canyon walls on ropes in silence, carrying their children with leather straps over their mouths. They spread out along its length in silence. They raised the knives in silence. The only sound was the knives. The god heard the knives, and that was so.

They ate. Imagine a hundred thousand people chewing in a canyon that repeats everything. The echo of the eating went up the walls and came down again and went up again for the whole night, a wet enormous patience, and the god lay with its ear to the rock and heard it, and heard it, and heard it.

An old man, partway through the night, began to weep. He had spent his life learning to weep in the canyon without a sound, and he made none; but his tears fell from his face onto the god's great ear, one, and then another, and the god heard them fall, and that was so. His eyes have been wet ever since, and his children's, and his grandchildren's. There is a family in Tacit called the Drips who have wept without stopping for six hundred years, and who are hired at every funeral in the canyon, and who are said to be the only people in Oathen who never have to explain how they feel.

A boy, partway through, stopped chewing. He looked up at his mother with his mouth full and his eyes wide, and drew a breath to say something. She put her hand over his mouth. She did not take it away until dawn. When she did, the boy had chewed through the side of his own cheek trying to keep from speaking, and she held a strip of the god's tongue against the wound until it closed. The families who ate the tongue sat in a circle, cutting it into slivers no bigger than a fingernail, and laid each sliver on their own tongues, and closed their mouths, and let it dissolve. They say it was the only thing any of them ever tasted that had no taste at all.

Nobody spoke for a year afterward. The first word said aloud in the canyons after the Supper was a merchant's "I swear," three springs on, and you know what happened to his hands.
[/fiction]

### VII. Hallowboard

[fiction]
We are the people who said no. Teach it so. Our children learn it before their own names.

Orrum the Host lay on the table at Hallowboard, on the bare hill at the middle of the world, the eight-sided stone table that is always clean. It lay as the others lay, quiet, warm, its great stooped body folded along the board, and the board was the right size for it, as if it had been cut to measure. It smelled of bread. All of it. The whole hill smelled of bread fresh from the oven, and of woodsmoke and of the inside of a kitchen on a winter evening when everyone you love is home.

We stood around it and we did not eat. We stood in the cold with our children on our shoulders, skin over bone, and the god lay on its table and waited, and we said, as we say now, No, thank you. And again, No, thank you. And again. Nine in ten of us died where they stood that winter, or on the walk home, or in their houses with the smell of bread still in their noses. We did not eat. Remember it. We are the only clean people in the world.

Remember also, because the elders do not forbid it, how long the walk to Hallowboard was. The heartland is wide, and we were weak, and the snow lay deep on the old roads. Remember that when the first of us came over the last ridge, the fires on the far horizons, where the other peoples were, had already burned down to red. Remember that the god on the table opened its eyes when it heard us, and looked at us, and its look was the look of someone who has waited a very long time for guests and has just heard the door.

Remember that the walkers sang the counting-song as they came, one verse for each day of the road, so that the weakest would know how far they had come and not lie down. Remember that the song has ten verses. Remember that nobody now living can tell you why it was written with ten.

Remember that it turned its great head, once, and looked at the eighth side of the table, where none of us stood, where the snow had not settled, where nothing was. Remember that it looked there for a long while. Remember that when it looked back at us, it was afraid.

Then it got up. It got up off the board, slow and stooping, the way an old man gets up from a chair. It stood over us in the dark, warm, enormous, smelling of supper. And it began to lay the table.

No, thank you. No, thank you. No, thank you.
[/fiction]

Read any one of these vignettes aloud at the start of a campaign set in that land; read all seven if the campaign will travel. Each contains a detail the land's own chapter treats as history and a detail it treats as heresy. The Fen boy who ran is Grandfather Peat. Each vignette also hides one small cruelty a GM can lift straight into play: the twelve children and eleven closings (somebody's ancestor was the one the Hand did not reach); the drover's harness needle (a Fatlands heirloom worth a wedding); the drowned brought up on the hooks (Brinehollow families still argue whose); the girl who asked about the long room (her dream-book is somewhere); the Kest telling and the round-hill telling (two Cradlewrack families, each certain the other is lying); the Drips (who know more about grief than anyone in Oathen is allowed to); and the ten verses of the counting-song (the Unfed sing only seven verses of their oldest hymn, and do not know why that matters).
[/box]

## Why "the Long Grace"

On every table on the continent, before the first mouthful, someone says the words: *Lack keep away.* It is the shortest grace in the world and the only one every people shares. It is said quickly, with the eyes down, the way you touch a doorframe for luck. It is the pause between sitting down and eating.

The poets of the Rim Road began calling the age itself by that name around the third century, half in mockery: six peoples had sat down to the biggest meal in history, and somehow they had not yet finished saying grace over it. The joke was that the meal was eaten in a night, and the bill had not come. Priests took the phrase up a century later without the joke. To them it meant what it sounds like it means: that the six hundred and forty-one years since the Gorging are not an age at all but a held breath, a little ceremonial pause, and that the real eating has not yet begun.

Scholars of the Cutwrights' College use the term precisely. The Long Grace, in their ledgers, is the span between the swallowing of the Providers and the completion of their Regrowth: a finite period, with a measurable rate, approaching its end. Every land's chronicle in the last decade has started using the phrase in the past tense by accident. "In the Long Grace, my grandmother kept bees." Nobody corrects it. Everyone hears it.

In common speech, "the Grace is ending" is what you say when the milk turns, when the dog will not come in, when the neighbor's daughter starts bailing at the wrong hour. It is said lightly. It is meant the other way.

[pull] In the Long Grace, my grandmother kept bees. | — overheard at Fogmouth; the speaker's grandmother is still living

[box: The Grace in Seven Mouths]
The words are the same everywhere. The manner is not, and a traveler who gets the manner wrong is noticed.
- **Sallowreach.** Said by the living for the Kept, who cannot always manage the consonants; the Kept nod. Gloves stay on.
- **The Fatlands.** Said after the field-names, never before, and fast, because the food is getting cold: *Thank you, Long Acre, thank you, Hessop's Rise, Lack keep away.*
- **Brinehollow.** *Lack keep away, and keep you dry.* Said with the bucket set down, never held.
- **The Vigil.** Said with the eyes wide open, then a flat slap on the table, so nobody at the board has nodded off during it.
- **Cradlewrack.** Said with a knock on the table, three times, to warn whatever is in the food.
- **Oathen.** Signed, not said, unless foreigners are present; then said very slowly, with three exits attached.
- **The Fast.** Said to the empty air, standing, before eating from the hand, by people who share their food with no one.
[/box]

There is an older and longer form of the grace. A few fen grandmothers know it, and a few Oathen Clausewrights, and none of them will say it aloud for money, and the two or three scholars who have heard it say only that it is not addressed to the eater.

## The Providers

They were not distant. That is the first thing every child is told and the last thing any adult understands. The gods of the old world did not live on mountaintops or behind the sky. Each lived bodily among one people, immense and present, the way a mountain is present: you could walk to it, you could put your hand on it, you could smell it on the wind on a warm day. It had a temperature. It shed. It had moods you could read the way farmers read cloud.

There were eight. Seven are named in every carving older than the Gorging, standing or lying in a ring, facing inward, around something that is never shown. The eighth place in the ring is always there, and always empty, because the figure in it has been cut away.

### Living Beside a God

A Provider did not rule in any sense a magistrate would recognize. It did not make laws or demand prayers. It simply did what it was, enormously, and the people who lived in its shadow arranged their lives around the fact. Fen-folk did not have to bury their dead, because Ossel closed them, and what Ossel closed went quietly into the peat and was clean in a season. Plains-folk did not have to fear a bad harvest, because Ummer walked the fields. Canyon-folk did not need courts, because a thing said in Tolm's hearing was so, and liars learned to keep away from its ear.

The cost was intimacy. You lived with a god the way you live with weather in a small house. You heard it at night. You smelled it in your bread. Children were sent to sit in its shade and came home with its dust on their clothes. Sweethearts met in its lee. People died against its flank in old age, deliberately, the way an old dog crawls under the stove. Every people had words for the god's breathing, the god's stillness, the god turning over in the night. Every people believed, without needing to say so, that the god knew each of them by name.

Worship, in the old world, was mostly feeding. Every people brought their Provider a portion of what the Provider had given them: the first sheaf, the first catch, the first cry of a newborn, the first word of a child. It was called *the giving back*, and in Table-speech the verb for it is the same as the verb for eating. The gods ate what they were given, slowly, over days. Nobody remembers them ever asking for more. Nobody remembers them ever asking for anything at all.

[pull] We did not pray to them. You do not pray to a mountain. You sit in its shade and you are grateful and you try not to think about what it would do if it moved. | — Grandfather Peat, as interpreted, Lastgate, 640 A.G.

The pages that follow describe the seven named Providers as the old world knew them: how they were shown, how they were served, what was sung to them, and what is left. Almost nothing is left. The Gorging was thorough. The relics given here are the ones every land acknowledges; there are others, in cellars and under altars, which nobody acknowledges at all.

### Ossel, the Closing Hand

[sigil: sallowreach]

God of death and endings. It lay in the northern fens for as long as anyone remembered, long and grey and still as a fallen ash, with hands that moved when nothing else did. Its iconography is two open hands, palms up, and in later carvings a single hand drawing a lid down over an eye. Fen-folk wore a grey thumbprint on the brow at funerals and pressed their own thumbs to the eyelids of the dead in imitation of it. When someone died, the family carried them to the god on a reed hurdle and laid them by a hand, and the hand closed their eyes and their mouth and folded their arms, and then the body was sunk in clean black water and was gone in a season, bones and all, leaving the water sweet. People who lived near Ossel were calm about everything. They knew how things went. An old fen saying, from before: *The Hand comes for the candle when the candle is done.* Nobody now alive in Sallowreach can say it without weeping.

**Iconography.** Ossel was carved in bog-oak and cast in bog-iron, because those were the materials that lasted in the fen, and its images were always grey: grey slip on red clay, grey ash rubbed into wood, grey tallow on the palms of the great figures that stood at the fen crossings. The hands were the whole of it. The fen shrines show no face, no body, only the two hands lying open on the ground, palms up, and between them a little hollow where a dead person would be laid. The oldest carvings give the fingers too many joints, nine and ten to a finger, which the fen once thought a sculptor's mannerism and now does not. Votive hands of fired clay, thumb-sized, palms up, turn up by the thousand when the peat is cut, and fen children still find them and are told to put them back.

**Worship.** The rite was called *the Carrying*. The dying were brought to the god while they could still see it, on reed hurdles borne by four kin, and laid down within reach of a hand, and the family sat on the bank and talked to them of ordinary things: the eel-traps, the price of salt, who was courting whom. When it was time, the hand came. Nobody wept until it had closed the eyes, because weeping before was thought to make the dying hold on out of politeness. Afterward the body was sunk in the sweet black pools that the fen called the Sweetwaters, and the family kept one candle-end lit at home until it guttered by itself, and then the mourning was done. There were no priests, only the *hurdle-folk*, old men and women who carried for families with no strong kin, and were paid in eels.

[fiction]
Close the door, the day is done,
Close the eye, the light is gone,
Lay the hand on what is through,
Hand, come down, and close me too.
[/fiction]
>> — Carrying-hymn of the old fens; sung in Sallowreach now only by the Finishers, at the edge of a Hush

**The Relic.** In the Hall of Closings at Lastgate, behind three doors that each close by themselves, there is a fingernail the size of a roof-slate. It is grey, ridged, slightly curved, and cold enough to raise frost on the velvet beneath it in the warmest week of Plenty. It is held to be the nail of the left forefinger, pared by the Corse family on the night itself and never eaten. The Regent lays his palm on it once a year, on the First Morning, and the Court watches to see whether his hand comes away. It always has. The Court has never asked what would happen if it did not.

### Ummer, the Laden

[sigil: fatlands]

God of harvest and plenty. It walked, unlike most of the others: a vast, slow, broad-backed shape like a laden haywain made of earth and bark and pelt, ambling across the southern plains with its head down, and the fields bowed toward it as it passed and rose up heavier behind it. Its iconography is a full belly with a sheaf rising out of it, and a mouth, always a mouth, smiling. Plains-folk followed its track with seed-bags and sowed in its footprints, which never failed. They thanked it by name at every meal, as they still thank the fields, and the first loaf of every baking was set on the doorstep for it, and in the morning the loaf was gone and the doorstep was warm. It was the most loved of the gods and the most taken for granted. Children rode on it.

**Iconography.** Ummer is the only Provider whose images move: it was shown walking, always, head down, with the furrows bending toward it like grass toward a wind. The granary lintels of the south carry long friezes of it ambling across the stone with the sheaves bowing in its wake, and the oldest grace-stones at the corners of Fatlands fields still show it, worn to a soft bump. Its colours were gold and red ochre. Its mouth was always carved open and smiling, and the corn-dollies the plains-folk plaited in its shape at harvest had a mouth stitched on in red thread, so that the doll could be fed a crumb at supper. Children were given them to hold at night. They are still made in Gristmoot, and sold on the Rim as toys, and the red-thread mouths are sewn on wider every decade.

**Worship.** At Harvest Home the children of a village were lifted onto its back, and it carried them, very slowly, the length of the parish and back, and that was called *the Riding*, and every child who rode was said to be lucky in bread for life. In Carving it shed a down like thistle from its flanks, warm and faintly sweet, which the women gathered from the hedges and spun for swaddling-bands. A baby swaddled in Ummer-down never went hungry. That was the saying. It is still said in the Fatlands, of babies swaddled in ordinary wool, by people who know better.

[fiction]
Walk on, walk on, the furrow's yours,
Walk on, walk on, the bread is ours,
Bow down, bow down, the wheat to thee,
And leave a little left for me.
[/fiction]
>> — the old sowing-song; now a clapping game for Fatlands children, who sing the last line *and leave a little left of me*

**The Relic.** In a bare field outside Brawn Tilth, a short walk from the Trencher, there is a footprint. It is the last one: the print of a forefoot as broad as a cart, pressed a hand deep into the clay at the place where Ummer finally folded its legs in the seventh year of the Lack. The Board of Plenty has roofed it, fenced it and set a Field Church warden over it. The clay at the bottom has never dried. Seed dropped into the print comes up within a day, and the wheat it grows has teeth before it has ears, and the warden pulls it every evening with tongs. Pilgrims are allowed to kneel at the fence. Some of them lick the rail.

[pull] Nobody's full, but some are fuller. | — Fatlands proverb

### Dromm, the Fathom

[sigil: brinehollow]

God of the deep sea, of pressure, of what is kept under. Nobody living saw more than its back, a long grey reef far out past the shelf that broke the surface on the stillest days and breathed. Its iconography is a closed circle with lines pressing in from every side, and the oldest coastal carvings show it as a hand holding something down beneath the waves. The coast gave it the first fish of every boat and the bodies of the drowned, rowed out with lanterns and let down on weighted ropes. In return the sea was full and the storms broke on its back before they reached the shore. Divers who went deep came up with nosebleeds and a feeling of being enormously, patiently watched. The old coast-folk said Dromm kept the sea's secrets for it, everything that had ever sunk, and would never give a single one of them up.

**Iconography.** The closed circle with lines pressing inward is still painted on every anchor-stock in Brinehollow and cut on every tide-post. In the old world it was tattooed on the breastbone of every coast child at its first bailing of the sea, in soot and fish-oil, a ring the size of a palm with nine short strokes pointing into it. The great coast carvings show a hand rising from waves to press something down; what is pressed is never shown, only the water closing over it. Ship-figureheads of the old coast had their hands folded on their chests, as if keeping something in. Since the Brimming, the coast has found that image less comforting.

**Worship.** The drowned were rowed out on the night after the death, and the lantern was let down after them on its own rope so that they could see their way. But the strangest rite was *the Keep*. A coast family with a secret it could not bear, a theft, a bastard, a killing, a love, wrote it on a scrap of linen, sealed it in a bottle with pitch and lead shot, and rowed it out, and dropped it over the deep place, and Dromm kept it. Nobody ever told a secret that had been given to the Keep. To ask about one was the worst manners on the coast. Divers who went deep said the seabed under the god was paved with green glass.

[fiction]
Hold them down, the drowned and dear,
Hold the storm off, hold it there,
Keep what sank, and keep it deep,
Keep it, Fathom, keep, keep, keep.
[/fiction]
>> — net-mending song of the old coast; the Anchorites of the Hanging Abbey still sing it, with the last line said through the teeth

**The Relic.** The bottles of the Keep. The sea has been leaving since 512, and the Uncovered is giving them back: thousands of them, green and brown and black with pitch, standing upright in the cracked mud where the water left them, by the acre. Salvagers dig them out by the cartload and sell them at Brackwater Bridge for five lacks a bottle or fifty if the pitch bears a family seal. The linen inside is always dry. The secret is always true. It usually still matters, because the families who sank them are still there, and their names are still on the Admiralty's rolls, and a six-hundred-year-old killing is still a killing. The Admiralty has banned the trade four times. The Drowned Bench has never once been asked to rule on it, and nobody has asked why.

[pull] The sea gives back, but not change. | — Brinehollow proverb

### Iss, the Lidded

[sigil: vigil]

God of sleep. It lay curled in the high valley above old Ludmere with its lids shut, and no one in recorded history ever saw its eyes open. Its iconography is a closed eye, two curved lines, sometimes with a row of small figures lying asleep along the lower lid. Worship in Ludmere was dreaming: oracles slept against the god's flank and woke with prophecy, and families brought their dreams to the temple-keepers each morning to be written down and offered. Sleep was holy and easy; the Ludmere bed was a famous luxury, exported up and down the old roads. People who lived near Iss were the best-rested and most reasonable people in the world. It is a small horror the Vigil does not dwell on that its ancestors were famous for their naps.

**Iconography.** The closed eye, two curved lines, was drawn with a thumb in ash on the forehead of every Ludmere child at bedtime, and on the doorposts of houses where someone was sick, and on the lids of the dead. It was carved at the head of every Ludmere bed, which is why the Vigil burned every bed in 342. The finest images of Iss were in pale soapstone, worn smooth by the cheeks of people who had slept with their faces against them, and showed the god curled like a hound with its head on its paws and a row of tiny sleepers lying along its lower lid like children along a bolster. In the Vigil these are smashed on sight. A few survive in the cellars of the Height, wrapped in felt, and their owners visit them alone.

**Worship.** The walkers-softly kept the temples, which were long low halls full of beds open to any pilgrim, and every morning they sat at the bedsides with a pen and wrote down what the sleepers had dreamed, and the dream-books were offered to the god in great stacks against its flank. The oracles' prophecies were mostly about weather and marriages, and all of them came true. In summer the god shed its lid-scales, thin and warm as a moth's wing, and the walkers burned them as incense, and the whole valley slept in the smoke for a night and woke rested for a month. Nobody in Ludmere ever worked hard. It was considered rude to the god.

[fiction]
Lie down, lie down, the Lid is low,
The long room's warm, and in you go,
The hill is breathing, so breathe too,
And Iss will keep the dark for you.
[/fiction]
>> — Ludmere cradle-song; to sing it within the Vigil's walls is a crime, listed in the Waking Laws as *the song*

**The Relic.** A walker-softly's dream-book. A few hundred survived the burnings of 342, smuggled down the Sleepers' Mile in the flight from the First Night, and the College holds most of them at Quire. They are heavy, soft-paged, the ink gone brown, each a year of one temple's mornings: *Sowerby, the miller: dreamed of his mother's kitchen. Ama Pell: dreamed of rain on a tin roof.* In the volumes from the years of the Lack, the dreams change. Everyone in the temple dreams, night after night, of the same long dim room. Nobody dreams of the eye that the Vigil's sleepers see now; that comes later. In the Lack the dreamers write only that the room had a table in it, and that someone was sitting down.

### Vey, the Opening

[sigil: cradlewrack]

God of birth and beginnings, of doors and first breaths. The smallest Provider, the size of a heifer, soft and red and warm and always, somehow, new. It moved from house to house in the highlands when a birth was near and lay across the threshold. Its iconography is a doorway with something small just stepping through, and in the oldest stones a round opening ringed by petals or teeth. Highland women gave birth with one hand on the god. They gave it the caul, the cord, and the first breath of each child, which the midwife caught in her cupped hands and blew into Vey's nearest opening. Nothing was ever born wrong in the old highlands. Nothing was born too early. Doors stayed shut until they were opened, and when they were opened the god was usually standing on the other side.

**Iconography.** A doorway with something small just stepping through: carved on every lintel in the old highlands, painted on every birthing-stool, pressed into the clay of every new hearth. The older stones show the round opening ringed by petals, or teeth, and the very oldest show it ringed by both, alternating, which the Attendance has never commented on. Vey's colour was the red of the highland clay, and highland doors were painted with a red handprint at the height of a child's hand, to show the god it was welcome. In Cradlewrack today a red handprint on a door means a birth the Attendance has not yet ruled on. Neighbors cross the road.

**Worship.** The god lay across the threshold of a birthing-house so that nothing would come through the door that should not, and the caul and the cord were laid in a basket at its side and were gone in the morning. When a new house was built, the god was led to it and asked to step through the door first, and the house was not lived in until it had. The midwives were few, respected and underworked, and spent most of their time drinking with the shepherds.

[fiction]
Open, open, door and gate,
Open, little one, don't wait,
Vey is on the other side,
Open, open, open wide.
[/fiction]
>> — highland midwife's song; the Attendance forbids it, and teaches its apprentices to sing over a labor the two words *not yet*, instead, on one note

**The Relic.** The breath-cups. Every midwife of the old highlands had one, a little bowl of red clay, unglazed, the size of two cupped palms, in which she caught first breaths when her hands were too slow. The Attendance keeps the last nine in the Lying-In at Kest, in a locked case, and consults them. When a birth of any kind is coming anywhere within a day's walk, the cups fog from the inside. Lately they are fogged all the time, and the Midwife-Paramount has stopped having the case opened, because she says she does not need a cup to tell her what the whole country already knows.

[pull] You knock to warn whatever is on the other side. | — Cradlewrack manners, taught before walking

### Tolm, the Witness

[sigil: oathen]

God of oaths and true speech. It lay along the floor of the deepest canyon in the east with one ear pressed to the stone, long and bare and grey-pink, listening. What it heard was so. Its iconography is an ear, sometimes an ear inside an open mouth, and in the canyon carvings a long figure with a finger to its own lips. Canyon-folk did not worship it so much as consult it: contracts were spoken into its ear, marriages were said there, and the guilty were brought to say their innocence in its hearing and see what happened. Parents warned children not to say *I could die* near the canyon floor. There was very little crime and almost no poetry. Visitors found the canyon peoples' honesty so frightening that the word for an Oathener in two other dialects still means *someone you cannot sit near*.

**Iconography.** An ear, carved in red sandstone, sometimes as small as a thumbnail and sometimes as wide as a cartwheel; an ear inside an open mouth; and the long figure, lying down, with one finger laid across its lips. The canyon ear-shrines that survive are worn smooth at the lobe where six centuries of foreheads have pressed against them. Oathen rings, worn on the thumb, one for each oath outstanding, were in the old world worn in Tolm's sight as a courtesy, so the god could count. In the canyons, to point at your own ear is to say *I heard that*, and it is a threat.

**Worship.** Canyon-folk walked the length of the god to reach its ear, which took most of a day, and the walk was called *coming to the hearing*. What was said at the end of the walk had been rehearsed for weeks, and was said quietly. No offerings were brought; the god was given only true sentences, and the canyon believed it fed on them. Children were taken to the ear at seven to say their names, once, so that they would be so.

[fiction]
I say what is.
I hear what is said.
What I say, I am.
Hear me, and let me be what I said.
[/fiction]
>> — the Hearing Prayer, never sung, only mouthed; given here in its handtalk gloss, as taught in the Quiet

**The Relic.** The Bench of Clauses keeps, in a cedar box in the Hall of Conditions, a lump of the god's ear-wax the size of a loaf: amber, translucent, faintly warm, smelling of hot stone and old honey. It was cut from the ear on the night of the Silent Supper by a Tongue-line woman who could not bear to eat it. A fragment the size of a lentil, pressed into the ear of a Clausewright, lets her hear every lie spoken in her presence as a small dry scraping, like a knife on a slate. The Bench uses it in treaty negotiations with nations that can lie. The lump has been shrinking for six hundred years. The Bench has calculated when it will run out, and has drafted a forty-page conditional about what to do then.

### Orrum, the Host

[sigil: fast]

God of the hearth, of welcome, of the shared table. It lived in the rich heartland at the middle of the Table, very tall, stooping under doorways, warm, and it went from house to house. When it came to a door the hearth inside lit itself and the table was laid. Its iconography is a table with a single figure bent over it, laying it, and on the oldest stones an open hand holding out a bowl. The heartland was the richest country in the world because it was the most generous: every traveler was fed, every stranger seated, every quarrel settled over bread. Orrum was the god of the other seven too, in a way. The great carvings show all eight facing inward around a table, and the figure that pulls out the chairs is always Orrum.

**Iconography.** A table, and a single stooping figure laying it. On the oldest stones, an open hand holding out a bowl. In heartland houses before the Gorging, a chair carved into the wall beside the hearth, pulled out at an angle, for the god or for whoever it brought; in the heartland temples, a long stone board with every chair pulled out. The heartland's lintels were set a hand higher than any other land's, so that the god would not have to stoop so far. The Unfed have torn down every temple of Orrum but one and bricked every hearth. They have kept the high lintels. Nobody can afford to rebuild a house.

**Worship.** The heartland worshipped Orrum by being hosted: when the god came, the household sat down with whoever was there, kin and stranger alike, and ate, and that was the rite. Between visits the heartland practiced: every house kept its door open at supper, every table had one more chair than the household needed, and the first and the last mouthful of every meal belonged to the stranger, if there was one, and if there was not, to the empty chair. The heartland was famous for never letting a traveler sleep hungry. It was also, the other peoples said, impossible to leave a heartland house in under three days.

[fiction]
Come in, come in, the board is laid,
The fire's lit and the bed is made,
There's bread for you and bread for me,
And a chair for whoever the guest may be.
[/fiction]
>> — heartland hearth-hymn, before the Gorging; at Wanting it survives only as a list of words a child must never put together

**The Relic.** The Host-bells. Before the Gorging every heartland door had a small bronze bell hung inside the lintel, and it rang by itself when the god turned into the lane, so that the household would have time to wash its hands. Most were melted down in the Thin Years and sold on the Rim for bread. The Hostwatch carry the rest: a bell on a cord, at the belt, which tells them, by ringing, that the god is near. In the Fast they ring several times a season. In the last two years they have begun to ring on the Rim Road, at inns, at crossroads, in the middle of the night, and the Hostwatch trackers who carry them have begun to wrap them in wool.

[pull] If a chair is pulled out for you, do not thank it. | — the fifth of the Five Rules of the Fast

### The Eighth

[sigil: eighth]

There is an eighth place in every carving older than the Gorging. In the ring of figures around the table, between the Host and the Closing Hand, there is a gap exactly the width of one of the others. Sometimes it is a smooth recess. Sometimes the stone is gouged and pitted. Sometimes the chisel marks are fine and tidy and sometimes they are frantic, a mess of strokes cut over strokes, as if whoever did it could not stop. In Brinehollow the stone was hacked with a boathook. In the Vigil it was ground out with a rasp so patiently that the recess shines. Analysis by the Cutwrights' College in 588 A.G. established that the removals were done by different tools, in different centuries, some of them before the Gorging and some of them after.

Nobody knows its name. Nobody knows its people. Nobody knows who ate it, or whether anyone did. No land claims it, and every land has, at some point in its history, made it a crime to ask. The question is called, on the Rim Road, *counting the chairs*. It is considered very bad luck.

A few things can still be noticed by those who look closely. In the carvings, the seven named figures are always shown facing inward toward the table, except that in perhaps one carving in ten, one or two of them have their heads very slightly turned toward the gap. On the oldest temple floors, in the places where worshipers would have stood, the stone in front of the eighth recess is unworn. In Table-speech there are two words for *guest*, and the older one has no plural, and no dictionary on the Table will print it.

Every other Provider has its page: its image, its rite, its song, its relic. The Eighth has this one, and this is all of it.

**Iconography.** A gap the width of one figure, between the Host and the Closing Hand. Chisel marks.

**Worship.** None recorded. In every land the stone in front of the eighth recess is unworn.

**Hymn.** None that is sung. The Unfed's oldest hymn has eight verses, and they sing seven.

**The Relic.** Under some of the recesses that nobody has ever swept, there is a little drift of grey dust that is not stone-dust, and is slightly too heavy, and is never cold.

[pull] Don't count the chairs, love. Just sit where you're put. | — a Rim innkeeper, to a child, overheard at the Groaning Board

## The Long Lack

> Nobody knows whether the Lack was a punishment, an accident, or a plan. That is the oldest wound in the world.
>> — preface to the College's *Concordance of the Nine Years*

For nine years, nothing began. That is the shortest true account. What follows is a longer one, assembled from the chronicles of all seven peoples, from the tally-sticks of granaries, from bone-pits, and from a great many songs that are no longer sung in company.

[bigquote] For nine years, nothing began. Not a seed, not a calf, not a child, not a loaf. Learn that sentence. Everything else in this book is a footnote to it. | — first lesson of the College's Copyists, Quire

### The First Year: the Thin Harvest

It began in Grace, with seed. Across every land at once, the spring sowing did not come up. Not poorly: not at all. Farmers dug up their furrows in the second month and found the seed exactly where they had put it, swollen, soft, and rotting, with no shoot and no root. The second sowing did the same. The Providers were consulted and gave no answer, but that was not yet alarming; the Providers had never given answers. Ummer walked the fields as always and the fields did not bow. People said it was a bad year. The granaries were full from the year before. Nobody went hungry that winter, and the Tablenight feasts, which were then a celebration of the longest night, were held as usual.

Nobody alive remembers the smell of the furrows that spring, but the songs do: a sweet, soft, cellar smell, like apples left too long in a barrel, rising off every field on the Table at once as the seed rotted in the warm ground. In the plains they dug it up by the handful and held it out to Ummer, and the god looked at it, and lowered its great head, and walked on. In the fens the eel-roe went white in the shallows and did not hatch, and lay along the reed-banks in long pale ropes that the herons would not touch.

The tally of the great granary at Brawn Tilth, as the clerk cut it into the door-post year by year, survives in the College's cellars:

| Year of the Lack | Bushels in Store | Mouths on the List | Clerk's Note |
| First | 41,000 | 2,210 | a bad year; seed-corn issued twice |
| Second | 22,600 | 2,240 | rationing by the half-loaf from Carving |
| Third | 9,100 | 2,180 | the herds slaughtered; meat in store, not counted |
| Fourth | 3,300 | 1,960 | the bins turned; ash; the bakers taken up |
| Fifth | 400 | 1,410 | leather, glue, bark issued by weight |
| Sixth | none | 1,020 | the list kept by households now, not names |

*There is no entry for the seventh year. The clerk is not on the sixth year's list.*

### The Second Year: the Empty Nets

The nets came up empty along the whole western coast in the first week of Plenty and did not fill again. Hens stopped laying. Cows dried. Orchards flowered and the flowers fell and nothing set. The rationing began in the towns, and with it the first bread riots, and the first appearance of the phrase that would become the whole world's grace: *Lack keep away*, muttered over thinned porridge. In the north, Ossel's hands were seen to move restlessly at night. In the heartland, Orrum went from house to house as always, and the hearths lit, and the tables were laid, and there was less and less to put on them.

The first bread riot recorded on the Table was at the old market of Sated, in the second Carving, when a baker named Odder Fane put up his price by a farthing-weight and the crowd came over his counter. He was not hurt. His boy was, trampled under the trestles while he tried to save the loaves, and the crowd stood back afterward and looked at what they had done, at the small flat shape among the spilled bread, and then a woman bent and picked up a loaf from beside the boy's hand and ate it, and then they all did. Odder Fane closed his shop the same day and walked out into the plain, and was not seen again. The phrase *Lack keep away* first appears in the record that winter, chalked on his shutters.

### The Third Year: the Barren Stalls

Livestock went barren. No calf was dropped on the whole Table that year, and no lamb, and no foal. Then the herds began to be eaten, against every law, because a cow that will not calve is only meat. The old breeds died out that winter, all of them, in a single season of slaughter. The Providers grew visibly thinner. Plains-folk measured Ummer's flank against a barn wall and marked it in chalk, and marked it again each month, and the marks moved inward. The great temples began demanding larger gifts, and got them, and the gods ate them slowly as always and grew no fatter.

The slaughter took a single Carving. Every byre on the Table rang with it: the bellowing of beasts that could smell the blood of the beasts before them, the thud of the poleaxe, the slap of hides on the yard stones. The red Tilth ox went that year, and the white goats of Ludmere with their soft drooping ears, and the fen-buffalo, and the long-legged canyon sheep that could climb a wall, and the little highland cattle that slept in the house with the family. Every one of them. People who were children that winter remembered for the rest of their lives the smell of a whole country salting meat at once, and the way the gutters ran, and how fat everyone was by Tablenight, and how quickly they stopped being.

[pull] We ate the last red ox on Tablenight and sang over it. My father wept. I thought it was for the ox. | — deposition of Amos Ruddle, Gristmoot, 58 A.G.

### The Fourth Year: the Ash Bread

The flour went wrong. Grain that had been stored sound for years began to turn in the bins, and bread baked from it came out of the oven as grey ash in the shape of a loaf, which fell to dust when touched. Nobody has ever explained this. Bakers were hanged for it in three lands. Then the women stopped conceiving. It was noticed first in the highlands, where Vey lay across thresholds and no labor came; then everywhere. The last child born in the Lack is recorded in the Cradlewrack Tally as a girl delivered at Kest in the fourth Carving, who died on her third day. After her there were none for five years. The Providers stopped speaking that winter: not that they had ever spoken much, but the sounds stopped, the breathing grew shallow, the moods ceased to be readable. They lay still and grew thin.

The bakers were hanged in their aprons. In Sated the Board of that day had them hanged in a row of nine from the beam of the great granary gate, each with a loaf of the ash bread tied round his neck on a string, and the wind took the loaves to dust within the hour and blew it over the crowd, so that the people who had come to watch went home grey to the elbows, coughing, with the taste of it in their mouths: no taste at all, like breathing in from a cold hearth. The tenth baker, a woman, had fled, and the crowd went to her house and found her loaves in the oven, risen and brown and smelling of bread, and broke them open, and they were ash inside, and the crowd stood in her kitchen and wept.

The last child of the Lack, the girl born at Kest, was named Hope by her mother, which no midwife of the highlands had ever done before, because names wait. She lived three days. Her mother carried her up the round hill to Vey and laid her across the god's threshold, where she had been told all her life that nothing that came into the world could be lost; and the god lay there, thin and red and still, and did not open. The mother is recorded in the Cradlewrack Tally twice more: as the first woman of the highlands to go to the god's door in the night and not come back down, and, in the margin, in a later hand, *found in the clay at the foot of the hill in Grace; her arms still crossed as if carrying.* The highlands have not named a child in its first year since.

### The Fifth Year: the Leather Winter

The fifth year was when people began to eat the things that are not food. Harness. Shoe leather. Boiled glue. Bark, then the inner bark, then the wood. Grass soup. Clay with a little salt. Lamp oil. In the cities, the rat-catchers became rich and then were eaten by the people they had sold to. The old and the very young began to die in numbers that overwhelmed every rite; in Sallowreach, Ossel's hands lay still and the dead piled at its side unclosed, and the fen-folk learned for the first time what a corpse does when no god tends it. In the Fatlands, people followed Ummer across the bare fields on their knees, licking its footprints. Ummer did not stop. It walked more slowly than anyone had ever seen it walk.

What a corpse does when no god tends it, the fen-folk learned that winter, and it is the root of everything Sallowreach has become. In the old world the Hand closed the dead and the Sweetwaters took them in a season and the water stayed sweet. In the fifth year the dead lay at Ossel's side in rows, unclosed, their eyes open, their mouths open, and they swelled. The bellies rose and tightened and went green and then black, and on the warm days of Plenty they split with a sound like wet cloth, and what came out ran down the bank into the pools and the pools went sour and then foul, and the flies came up out of the reeds in a cloud that put out the lamps at noon. The fen calls that weather a Black Noon still. The families who had carried their dead to the god could not bear to look at them and could not bear to take them home, and many of them sat on the bank all summer, between the god and the dead, waiting for one of them to do something.

In the plains, some of those who licked the god's footprints went on to eat the soil itself, and their bellies swelled hard and round with clay, and they died fat, which the plains-folk still think the cruellest joke the Lack ever told. In the canyons people boiled their sandals and chewed the leather straps of their children's carrying-slings. In the highlands they ate the clay. In Ludmere they slept, and slept, and dreamed of food, and some of them stopped waking up to eat at all, and were found in the spring curled in their beds with their faces thin as paper and a look of perfect satisfaction.

### The Sixth Year: the Kitchen Year

This is the year the songs leave out.

It began with the dead. A family would lose a grandmother in the night, and in the morning the grandmother would not be carried out. The pot would be put on. There are chronicles from all seven peoples that say the same thing in the same flat way: *in the sixth year the dead were kept at home*. The first laws against it were passed in every land, and none of them were enforced, because the magistrates had grandmothers too.

The old went first, and many of them went of their own accord. That is remembered in every land with a particular grief. Grandmothers who could no longer stand stopped taking their share of the bark soup and pushed the bowl across the table to the youngest child, and died with their hands folded, and were glad, the families said, to be useful. Grandfathers walked out into the snow at night so that they would not have to watch the pot being put on for them, and the families found them in the morning, and the pot was put on anyway. In the Fatlands there were old women who asked to be eaten, plainly, at table, the way they would ask for the salt, and their families wept and promised, and kept the promise. The Fatlands wake, with its best plates and its *he ate well*, begins in that year. It began as an apology.

Then it went further. The College has assembled, from coroners' rolls and the confessions of the old taken down in the first decades after the Gorging, a picture it publishes only in cipher. Households drew lots. Children were sent to stay with cousins and the cousins sent theirs back, so that no one would have to eat their own. The phrase for this, still known in Fatlands dialect as a curse, is *trading tables*. In the Oathen canyons, where a word spoken could not be unsaid, families made solemn promises to one another that they would not, and then had to watch the promises take their hands off. In Brinehollow a man rowed his dead wife out to the deep place to give her to Dromm, as was proper, and could not let go of her, and came back with her, and the town watched him carry her up the shingle and did not say a word, and that night every chimney on the street smoked.

The lots were drawn with knucklebones, mostly, because every house had a set, and because a knucklebone does not lie. The College has a set from a Brinehollow cottage, yellow with handling, five bones in a leather bag, and a sixth, smaller, separate, which is not a sheep's. Children who were sent across the village to *trade tables* knew where they were going and why, and some of them went singing, because they had been told it was a visit, and some of them knew better and went anyway, holding a cousin's hand, and the cousins who were sent the other way passed them in the lane, and nobody looked up. What happened in the cousins' kitchens is not written down. What is written down, over and over, in the depositions of the old, is the walk back across the village afterward, and the bowl brought to the door, and the fact that it was always, in every telling, a little warm.

There was murder. The chronicles do not soften it. Men killed brothers for a hen's egg that was not there. In the Fatlands a father killed his second son so that his first might live, and ate with the first at the same table, and the first son lived to be the grandfather of a Gut-line house that still sits on the Board, whose arms show two spoons crossed, and nobody in the house will say why. In Ludmere a whole street sealed its doors and lit no fires and was found in the spring asleep around a single table, every one of them thin as a lath, and in the middle of the table, cleaned, the bones of the street's one fat man.

The smell of that year, survivors said, was the smell of a cooking-pot in a house where nothing has been cooked in months: so sudden and so rich that it brought the neighbors to the door. They would stand outside in the snow and not knock. They would just stand there, breathing it.

The Kitchen Year has a long tail. Builders in every land still find what it left: small bones under hearthstones, cracked lengthwise for the marrow; a skull in a chimney-breast, wrapped in a christening gown; a cupboard bricked up in a canyon gallery with a pot inside it and a spoon still standing in what is left. Such finds are not reported. They are wrapped in clean cloth, by custom, and carried to the nearest crossroads at night, and left. In Brinehollow there is a street in old Saltwick where every chimney smoked on the night the widower carried his wife up the shingle, and no house on it has been lived in since, and no one has pulled them down, and the doors are kept painted.

### The Seventh Year: the Bone Dole

Governments, such as they were, took over the dying. In every land, the dead were collected by the authorities and rendered in public kitchens, and the meat was given out by ration, and nobody asked whose it was. It was, the chronicles say, the most orderly year of the Lack. The first ration-discs were cut that winter: rounds of salt-cured bone, stamped, each one good for a day's ration at the public kitchen. The bone was the bone of the dead. The name stuck, and the value: one day's bread. People stopped fighting over food in the seventh year, because there was a system, and a queue, and a coin, and that is the year the old world truly ended.

The kitchens were kept by priests, because there was no one else left who was trusted, and the priests kept tallies. Every body brought to the vats was entered by name, parish and weight; every ration given out was entered by name and disc. These were the tally-priests of the Bone Dole, and their lists, carried out of the kitchens in the ninth year and copied and recopied, are the oldest documents in the Master Concordance at Quire. The Cutwrights' College was born at a vat-side, with a pen.

The kitchens themselves are described in every chronicle the same way: a long shed, a row of coppers, a queue that went out of the door and round the square and down the street and did not move quickly, and over all of it a smell of boiling that soaked into the clothes of everyone who waited and could not be washed out. People in the queue did not speak. They held their discs. Collectors call those first discs Dole-bones, or *grandmother coins*, and the few that survive are worth a great deal and are never spent. The coin in a modern purse descends from them in name and value only (see *The Lack*, below).

The Providers lay down. All of them, everywhere, within days of one another. Ummer stopped walking at last in the middle of a bare field and folded its legs. Orrum, in the heartland, stopped going from house to house and sat on the bare hill at Hallowboard beside the eight-sided table, with its head bowed, as if waiting for someone to arrive.

### The Eighth Year: the Eldest Walk

In the spring of the eighth year, the priests of all seven peoples met at Hallowboard. It was the only time in history all seven sat together, and the only record of the meeting is a list of resolutions carved into the hill's foot-stone, worn almost smooth. They had come to read the carvings on the table's eight sides, which were older than any of them, and they found, or believed they found, a demand. The carvings showed, beneath the ring of figures, a line of smaller figures walking toward the table with their arms at their sides. The priests read it as a tithe: the first of every house. The gods had given each house its firstborn; the gods were starving; the gods wanted the first back.

They called themselves the Moot. They were what was left of seven priesthoods: the hurdle-folk of Ossel, grey-thumbed and silent; the Sowers of Ummer, who had walked behind the god all their lives and could not now keep up with its stillness; the Soundings of Dromm, divers with burst ears; the walkers-softly of Iss, who had not slept since the dreams went bad; the door-wives of Vey, highland midwives with no births to attend; the Listeners of Tolm, who came with their mouths bound; and the board-keepers of Orrum, who were the hosts, and who fed the Moot for nine days on the last of the heartland's stores, and who ate nothing themselves. Most of them had walked for a month to get there. Several died on the hill and were not, the foot-stone records, put in the pot, out of respect. Nobody recorded what was done with them instead.

[fiction]

By common counsel of all seven peoples, it was done. In the last month of Plenty, in the eighth year of the Lack, the firstborn adult of every hearth on the Table walked to the feet of their people's god.

They walked, mostly, of their own will. That is the part every chronicle insists on. They were sons and daughters of twenty, of forty, of sixty; they were the heirs of farms and shops and fishing boats; they kissed their younger siblings and handed over their tools and walked out of their villages in long lines, singing, with the whole village walking behind them to the boundary stone and stopping there. They walked for days. They were very thin. Many died on the road. The rest arrived.

Who was firstborn was argued in every house. Twins were the worst of it. A mother who had borne twins and could not, or would not, say which had come first watched both of them walk, in most lands, because the Moot had said *no house shall be spared*, and the village elders would not choose. A family whose eldest had died in the Kitchen Year sent the next, and argued all the way to the boundary that it was not fair, and sent the next anyway. A man of seventy, the eldest son of a dead mother, walked, and his own eldest son of fifty walked behind him, and his eldest granddaughter of thirty behind that, three generations of one line on the same road. Some households had no firstborn living. They gave their bread to those that had, for the walk. It was thought the least they could do.

The walks took weeks. The lines went across the bare country singing at first, the old songs of every people, and then not singing, because singing took breath. They walked in their best clothes, which hung on them like washing. The ones who fell on the road were left where they fell, with their faces covered, and the line walked round them, and those coming after walked round them too, so that by the end of the month every road to every god on the Table had its little covered shapes along it, a bowshot apart, like milestones. In the Vigil they still say *a bowshot apart* of things that happen at regular intervals, without knowing why.

The rite was the same everywhere because the priests had agreed it. At the god's feet, each firstborn knelt, and a priest opened their throat with a bronze knife, and laid them down against the god, as close as could be got. In the fens they were laid in rows between Ossel's open hands. In the plains they were laid along Ummer's folded legs until it was hidden to the knees. At Longslab they were rowed out to the deep place and let down on weighted ropes in their thousands. In the canyons each one whispered a vow into Tolm's ear before the knife: *I give myself, so that they may eat*, and that was so, or should have been. In the red hills they were laid across thresholds. At Ludmere they were laid along the sleeping flank like children put to bed. At Hallowboard, the heartland's firstborn were laid on the stone table, which was big enough for all of them, in a heap.

The priests did it themselves. That is in every account. There were not many of them and there were very many firstborn, and the knives were bronze and went dull, and the priests had to stop and hone them on the stones, and the line waited, kneeling, while they did. The work went on for days. The priests' arms gave out and their acolytes held their elbows up for them. In the fens the water between Ossel's hands turned red and then black and stood thick as gravy. On the threshing floor the blood ran in the grooves cut for the grain. At Longslab the rowers came back from the deep place for another load forty times a day, and the weighted ropes ran out, and the last of the firstborn of the coast were let down with stones tied in their own shirts. In the canyons the Listeners counted the vows as they were whispered. They stopped counting at nine thousand.

The count was never made. The College's best estimate is somewhere above sixty thousand.

Nothing happened.

The gods did not take them. The gods did not stir. Ossel's hands did not close a single pair of eyes; the dead lay in the fen staring upward until the birds that were left found them. Ummer did not eat. Tolm, which heard every vow, did not make one of them so. The bodies lay where they had been laid, through the end of Plenty and into Carving, against the flanks of gods who did not move, and nothing rose from the fields, and nothing came to the nets, and no woman quickened.

It was Plenty, and hot. That is the part the chronicles cannot leave alone. The bodies lay in the late summer sun against the flanks of the starving gods, in heaps, in rows, in the shallow water, on the stone table, and they did what bodies do. They swelled. The smell went out over the bare country for twenty miles, so that people in villages that had sent their eldest a month before knew, on the evening wind, exactly what had become of them. The flies were so thick at Hallowboard that the heap on the table could not be seen, only a black, moving, humming mound the shape of a table, and the Moot's board-keepers stood around it with their sleeves over their faces and did not leave, because they had promised the hosts would stay. Birds came from every corner of the Table, birds that should have been dead of hunger long since, and the gods did not stir to stop them. Ossel's hands lay open on the peat while crows walked across its palms to the faces. The Hand that had closed every pair of eyes in the fens for ten thousand years did not close one.

And in Carving, when it was clear, the people who had walked their eldest to the gods' feet came back for them with sacks, at night, and did not look one another in the face, and carried them home.

They went in the dark so that the neighbours would not see, and found the neighbours already there, with their own sacks, and nobody spoke. What was left by then was not much, and not whole, and had to be lifted with spades in some places and in others did not need to be lifted at all, because the birds had left only the clothes. They knew their own by the clothes. They carried them home on their backs, through the bare fields, a long dark line of stooping people with heavy sacks, the same roads the eldest had walked down singing. What they did with them at home, in the eighth winter of the Lack, after the Kitchen Year and the Bone Dole, the chronicles do not say, and every reader of the chronicles knows.

The aftermath of the Walk has never ended. The priesthoods did not survive it; in most lands the surviving priests of the Moot were the first meat of the ninth winter, taken by crowds from the temples where they had shut themselves up, and the crowds did not use bronze knives. The seven old orders were never restored. Every priesthood on the Table now, Confessor, Thanker, Grace-Keeper, Purger, was made after the Gorging, by people who remembered what the old ones had asked for. And every village on the Table still has its *walk-stone*: the boundary stone where the families stopped and watched the line go on without them. Children are told not to sit on it. Lovers do not meet at it. On the night before the anniversary, in late Plenty, somebody in every village goes out and washes it, and nobody admits to being the one.

[box: The Eldest Walk in Play]
The Eldest Walk is the act no land discusses and every land remembers. It is why the Gorging happened. Before the Walk, the peoples of the Table believed the gods were starving with them and could be fed. After it, they knew that their gods would take nothing, give nothing, and save no one. Their most precious offering had been laid at the feet of every Provider in the world and been left to rot. The Gorging was not an act of hunger alone. It was an act of fury. Every land has a quiet name for it (the Tithe, the Long Line, the Giving of the First) and every land has, somewhere in its oldest families, a story of the eldest who did not go, or who went and came back. In the present day the Purgation preaches a second Walk, and the phrase *to walk the eldest* means, in every dialect, to make a sacrifice you know will be wasted.
Every PC whose family is old enough has a walk-stone somewhere, and an ancestor who knelt at it or carried a sack back past it. A character from a Gut-line or Hand-line house may know the name of their own house's eldest who walked; a Scrapling almost certainly does not, and may learn it, by College genealogy, at the worst possible moment. Use the Walk as a measuring rod for sacrifice in play: when any character is asked to give something precious for a promise from a god, someone at the table should remember the sacks.

### The Ninth Year: the Turning

The last winter was the quietest. There was nothing left to fight over. The dead were not collected anymore, because the collectors were dead. In every land the survivors sat in their houses in the dark and looked at their gods out of the windows, and the gods lay where they had lain down, enormous, gaunt, still, with the bones of the eldest at their feet.

Nobody knows who said it first. Every people claims a different first speaker, and in every account the first speaker is a mother. What she said, in every version, comes to the same thing: *They have something left on them, and we have nothing. They would not eat what we gave. Let us see if they will be eaten.*

The knives were already ground. People had been grinding knives for nine years, out of habit, for want of anything else to do with their hands.

The grinding was the last sound of the Long Lack, survivors said: a slow scrape of iron and bronze and bone on whetstone, from every house, in the dark, all winter, like insects in a wall. Nobody told anyone to do it. Every household did it. In the Fast, too, on the long walk in to Hallowboard, the heartland's people ground their knives as they walked. They have never discussed this. No Unfed household keeps a whetstone now; the Reckoners hone their black-iron knives on the Rim, on Company stone, and think nothing of it.

[pull] They have something left on them, and we have nothing. | — the first speaker of the Turning; in every land, a mother; in no land, named

## The Gorging

Whether it happened on one night or across a season is disputed. The stories call it the Night of Seven Tables, and Tablenight is kept on the longest night of Lack in every land, but the chronicles do not agree. Brinehollow's dragging took three days and its god four more to die. Sallowreach ate last of all and slowest, and some fen accounts stretch its meal over a fortnight. Oathen's Silent Supper, because no one spoke of it, has no recorded date at all. The Cutwrights' College, which has tried to reconcile them for four centuries, settled in 501 A.G. on the formula *the ninth winter, within a single Plate*, meaning one cycle of the moon, and has quietly lost every argument about it since.

To those disputes add two more. The Vigil's chronicle gives Ludmere's cutting nine nights, done slowly enough not to wake the god; Oathen's Ledger, which records no date, records three nights of silence in the Gullet. And the heartland came over the last ridge to Hallowboard on the tenth day, and did not eat.

What is agreed is this. In every land, the people went to their god with knives, and their god did not fight. The Providers did not flee, did not cry out, did not strike, did not so much as turn over. They were enormous; a twitch of Ummer's flank would have crushed a village; a breath from Dromm drowned whole fleets in the old days. They held still. It is recorded everywhere and explained nowhere, and every argument in the world, sooner or later, becomes an argument about why.

### The Sharing

Each god was butchered and shared out, and how it was shared became the shape of every society since. The families nearest, strongest, quickest, or most ruthless took the choicest parts: the hands, the gut, the lungs, the eyes, the caul, the tongue. Those behind them took the meat. Those behind them took what fell. Those at the very back licked the bones. Nobody planned it. Nobody needed to.

The pattern was the same everywhere, which nobody remarked on at the time and every scholar since has remarked on endlessly. In every land a few families took the one portion in which the god's nature seemed to live, and those families became the high Cuts; a larger crowd took the flesh, and became the common people; and a ragged multitude at the back took what was dropped, trodden, gnawed and left, and became the Scraplings.

### What Was Left

Each god left a place behind it, and every one of those places is still there. Ossel's long grey skeleton lay in the reeds below Lastgate until the fen took it, and the fen has never let it finish going down; at low water on still days the ribs show, like the timbers of a wreck. The Trencher at Brawn Tilth is still greasy, and nothing grown in a mile of it is eaten even by Fatlanders. Longslab is wet. The valley above Ludmere is flat, the grass lying down as if something enormous had only just got up off it. The round hill above Kest has its door. The Gullet in Oathen, where Tolm lay, is a long dry trough of sand that is warm at night, where pilgrims lay their ears. And Hallowboard is clean.

The bones were used. They had to be. The Table had been starving for nine years and was suddenly rich in the hardest, whitest bone anyone had ever seen. Hand-line houses still have chairs whose legs were cut from Ossel's lesser finger-bones. The oldest Fatlands granaries are roofed on Ummer's ribs. The first true lacks were cut from god-bone that year, in every land, by anyone with a saw, and a few of those First Lacks still pass from hand to hand on the Rim, unrecognized, until someone who handles them for a month wakes with the wrong god's taste in their mouth.

### The Morning After

The famine ended the next morning.

Every land records this the same way, with the same awe. People woke on the morning after their meal to a green haze on the furrows. Hens laid. By midday the nets were full. Within the week crops stood shoulder high in fields that had been sown nine years earlier with seed that had rotted in the ground. Women quickened that month, all of them, all at once, the old and the very young and some who had long been past it. Cattle that had been barren for six years dropped calves within the season, which should not be possible. Trees that had been cut for firewood put up new shoots from the stumps overnight. The world, which had not begun anything for nine years, began everything at once.

That speed should have frightened people more than it did. Here and there it did. In the Fatlands, where the wheat stood shoulder high in seven days, an old woman named Tallis Brome is recorded as having walked out into the new field on the eighth morning, broken an ear, chewed it, and said to her grandson: *This was not grown. This was poured.* Nobody listened. Everyone was eating.

Only in the Fast did the Lack not lift. The heartland's people, who had not eaten their god, woke to the same bare soil, the same empty coops, the same rotten seed, and looked out over the Rim at their neighbors' green fields with an emotion that has shaped their entire history and that they have never once named correctly.

Nine months after the Gorging, the Table gave birth. Every land records the season the same way: the first Carving after the Gorging was one long lying-in, every house with a child or two or three, every midwife in the world awake for a season. Women of sixty bore. Girls bore. The children of that autumn were called *the Plenty-born* and were, every chronicle agrees, the healthiest generation the world has known: broad, ruddy, loud, and every single one of them, the College would establish a century later, carrying the god of their land in their blood a little more strongly than their parents. The Plenty-born are the ancestors of everyone now alive. They grew up fat on miracle grain in a world that had started again. Most of them lived to great ages. Many of them, in old age, were the first to notice the Taste.

## The Three Doctrines

Every argument in the world eventually becomes an argument about why the gods held still. There are three answers, each with its temples, its martyrs and its hecklers, and most people on the Table believe a little of each depending on how the harvest went.

Each also has a sermon that every child on the Table has heard at least once, from a pulpit, a cart-tail or a drunk uncle. What follows are three of them, preached in the last generation, given at length because every one of them is still being preached tonight, somewhere on the Rim, to a crowd that is a little bigger than last year's.

| The Theft | Confessors | penance; fasting on the Meal days | justice | the poor; Sallowreach, Oathen |
| The Gift | Thankers | the Thanksgiving Meal | a shared grief | the comfortable; Fatlands, the Rim |

### The Theft

*We murdered them. The Regrowth is justice, and we deserve it.* The oldest and most common belief, held by the majority in Sallowreach and Oathen and by most of the poor everywhere. Its priests are called Confessors, and its rites are rites of penance: fasting on the anniversaries of the Meal, self-scourging in the Fatlands, the reading-aloud of ancestral guilt in Brinehollow. The Theft explains the Providers' stillness as shock, or contempt, or the paralysis of the betrayed. It offers no hope and asks for none. Its comfort is that the world makes sense.

[fiction]
You ask why it did not fight. I will tell you why a mother does not fight when her son comes for her with a knife. She is too surprised. She cannot believe it. She stands in her own kitchen with her hands open and looks at him, and she is still looking at him when she goes down. That is how they lay. That is how they looked at us. And now they are coming back up through us, through the gut and the lung and the eye, and you call it a curse? It is a bill. It is the only honest thing that has happened to this family in six hundred years. Pay it. Bow your head and pay it.

Look at me. I am a Confessor of the Weeping Post and my grandmother was a Hand-line bastard and I can feel her god in my wrists when it rains. I know what I am. Do you know what you are? You are a mouthful. You are a mouthful that got away. Every one of you carries a bite of a murdered thing in your blood, and it is not resting, and it should not rest, because we did not let it. We did not sing it to sleep. Oh, the Vigil did. The Vigil sang lullabies while it cut out the eyes. We did not close its eyes. We did not lay it in the Sweetwater. We sat on it and chewed.

So when your daughter's eyes will not shut, when your husband walks west, when your father's hands come grey and cold to the table and will not stop closing the salt-cellar, I do not want to hear the word *curse*. A curse is something done to you by someone who hates you. This is not hate. Nobody hates you. This is an account being settled by the only creditor in the world that cannot be bribed, because you have already eaten it.

What do we do? We do what debtors do. We fast on the anniversaries, so that for one day a year we know what we did it for. We say the names. We give back what can be given back, which is nothing, so we give it anyway, with our heads bowed. And when it is our turn to be collected, when it is our line the College marks for the Course, we go quietly, and we do not ask the Second Table to save us, and we do not ask the Purgation to bleed us, and we do not ask the Host for supper. We pay. Bow your head and pay.
[/fiction]
>> — Confessor Abel Rennick, sermon at the Weeping Post, 627 A.G.; Rennick was taken by the Course in 633, and is said to have met it with his head bowed

[pull] It is a bill. Pay it. | — painted over the door of every Confessors' chapel on the Rim

### The Gift

*They offered themselves out of love. The Regrowth is a tragedy neither side wanted.* The gentlest doctrine and the most popular among the comfortable: Gut-lines families in the Fatlands, Vigil merchants, the Rim Road's innkeepers. Its priests are called Thankers and its central rite is the Thanksgiving Meal, at which the worshipers eat in silence and then say, together, the names of all seven Providers, the eaten and the one that was not. The Gift reads the Providers' stillness as consent and their Regrowth as an unwilling side effect, a grief shared by eater and eaten. It holds that the gods suffer in the blood as their hosts do, and that kindness to the godsigned eases the god. Its critics call it the doctrine of people who have never been hungry.

[fiction]
Children, think of it this way. Your grandmother is very old and there is no bread. She takes her portion and puts it on your plate when she thinks you are not looking. She says she is not hungry. That is what they did, all of them, on that night: they lay down and they put themselves on our plates and said, eat, we are not hungry. And now they are in us, and they are hurting, and we are hurting, because love that big does not fit inside a body. It was not supposed to come back. It is not their fault. It is not ours. Be gentle with the ones whose eyes do not close. Be gentle with the ones who must be gagged. They are carrying more of the gift than you.

Now, I know some of you have been told otherwise. I know some of you have a grandfather who sits in the corner and says *we murdered them* into his soup. Let him say it. He is sad, and sad people like a story where somebody is to blame. But think. Do mothers fight their children at supper? Do they? No. They put the best bit on your plate and pretend they've already eaten. And when you are grown and they are old and you take them in and feed them, is that a theft? It is the same love going back the other way.

That is why we eat in silence at the Thanksgiving Meal, and then say the names, all seven, Ossel and Ummer and Dromm and Iss and Vey and Tolm and Orrum, even Orrum, who was not eaten, because it gave too, it gave its people the chance to say no. We say the names because you thank people by name. And then we are kind. That's all. That is the whole of the Gift. When you meet a Brinehollower bailing at dusk you hold her bucket. When you meet a Vigil girl blinking you pinch her, gently. When you meet a Kept man on the Rim the day before the Drop you carry his letter. We are all carrying them. They are all carrying us.
[/fiction]
>> — Thanker Maudlin Ashe, children's homily, Lampwick Halt, 640 A.G.; three of the children present have since been Counted, and Ashe has not preached at the Halt again

[pull] Be gentle with the ones whose eyes do not close. They are carrying more of the gift than you. | — embroidered on Thanker samplers, sold at every Rim inn

### The Bait

*They wanted to be eaten. The Lack was theirs.* This is how gods breed: starve a people, be swallowed, and grow back through ten thousand bodies instead of one. The doctrine first appears in the record around 330 A.G., in a pamphlet nailed to a Rim Road toll-house, and has been illegal in four lands (Sallowreach, the Fatlands, the Vigil and Oathen) for most of the time since. It is gaining ground in all seven, because it is the only doctrine that explains the speed of the Week of Rising and the only one that matches the arithmetic of the Regrowth. Its preachers have no order and no title. They are called *Baiters* by their enemies and nothing at all by themselves. Some of them are terrified; some, like the Second Helping of the Fatlands, are joyous; all of them agree that the Gorging was not the end of the gods but their sowing.

[fiction]
Why did it not fight? Because a seed does not fight the ground. Look at your hands. Look at the joints on the Hand-line Regent's fingers, the eyes on the Vigil's lords, the cord on the Uncut, the fat on the Board. Did your great-great-grandmother eat a god, or did a god get into your great-great-grandmother? Nine years without a single child born, and then a whole world of children in one spring, every one of them with the god in the blood. You think that was mercy? It was planting. You are a furrow, friend. We all are. And the harvest is due.

Here is the arithmetic, since none of your priests will do it for you. Nine years of nothing. Then one night, and then everything at once, a week of rising, a whole world pregnant in a season. Who sowed that? You were starving; you could not sow a row of beans. Then: one god per people. Not two. Not one god shared. Each god cut so that a few got the heart of it and the rest got the meat and the beggars got the licking, in every land the same, as if somebody had drawn up the seating. Did your great-great-grandmother draw it up? She could not read.

And now the curves. Ask the College. They won't tell you, but ask them where the lines go. Every land's Regrowth, bending upward, all at once, after six hundred years, the same decade. Seven separate gods, coming up together, like a field. That is not justice. Justice is not punctual. That is not grief either; grief does not keep time. That is a crop.

So eat or don't eat, pray or don't pray, I cannot help you. I am only a man on a cart. But when your lord swells and roots and opens his mouth in the field and something looks out, do not say you were never told. You are not being punished and you are not being loved. You are being grown.
[/fiction]
>> — anonymous Baiter, preaching on the Rim Road at Saltlick Cross shortly before being drowned by a crowd in a horse-trough, 638 A.G.; the crowd, the wardens' report notes, then stood around the trough for a long time without speaking, and three of them went home and did not come out for a year

[pull] You are a furrow, friend. We all are. | — chalked on the Hem wall at a hundred posts; the wardens wash it off, and it comes back

Doctrine is a fine way to start fights between player characters who otherwise agree on everything. Ask each player, at character creation, which of the three their character believes and which their parents believed. The doctrines are not settled by the game; the Secrets chapter offers a default truth and three alternatives built on each doctrine, and the GM may choose. Until then, every sermon is equally true, and equally useless.
Doctrine also decides small things in play. A Theft-raised character fasts on the Meal days and may refuse a Gift-keeper's charity as an insult. A Gift-raised character says the seven names before a feast and will expect to be thanked for holding a stranger's bucket. A Bait-raised character has learned to keep their mouth shut in four lands, and recognizes, in the way the Second Helping or the Second Table speak of the gods, something they were taught as a child and never said aloud.
[/box]

## The Regrowth

The return of an eaten god is called the Regrowth, and its marks on a person are called godsign. It moves through four stages, in a person, a family, or an entire country: the Taste, the Appetite, the Course, and the Seating. Each land's Regrowth obeys its own rules and is detailed in that land's chapter. What is shared is the shape, and the speed.

The Taste is small wrongnesses: dreams, cravings, a tell in the body that a lover notices first. The Appetite is compulsion; the body begins wanting what the god wants and will stop at nothing for it. The Course is flesh changing: the person becomes visibly, physically something else, grey-handed or loamy or lidless or veiled. The Seating is the god, in some real portion, present again, wearing a person or a place. Between the Course and the Seating there is a last threshold the College calls *the Brink*, when the person is still a person only by courtesy.

| Land | The Taste | The Appetite | The Course | The Seating |
| Sallowreach | Dread of open things | Compulsive closing | Grey hands, extra joints; touch finishes | A Hush |
| Fatlands | Hunger that wakes you | Eating without choosing | Swelling, rooting, loamy skin | A living hill |
| Brinehollow | Salt at waking | The pull westward | Grey skin, deep eyes, no breath | The Tenant takes the house |
| The Vigil | Heavy lids, spreading yawns | Blinks | The lids are gone | A sleeper begins to move |
| Cradlewrack | About to begin | False labor | True bearing | The land itself in labor |
| Oathen | Your words, echoed | The urge to swear | Plain speech binds | A Sayer |

No land has reached a full Seating of its god. Three are close. And every Regrowth is speeding up: signs that took generations now take years; the Hushes spread, the pangs shorten, the beds fill, the sea drops. The College's figures show that every land's curve bent upward at nearly the same time, in the decade after 600 A.G., which is impossible if the seven Regrowths are separate things. Each government is hiding its own numbers. The College holds all of them.

[pull] Nobody here is waiting to die. We are all waiting to be allowed. | — fen proverb, as true now in every land as in the one it was made in

## Blood and Class: the Cuts

Who your ancestors were matters less than what part they ate. The families who took the choicest portions of a god carry the strongest godsign, and in every land they became the nobility, because in the first generations godsign looked like blessing. A Hand-line touch that could close a wound, a Gut-line appetite that never sickened, an Eye-line who could see in the dark: these were gifts, and their bearers were raised up. By the time the gifts curdled, the Cuts owned everything.

These bloodlines are called **Cuts**, and a person's Cut is the measure of god in their blood, from 0 to 5. The College grades them as follows, and its grades are used in law in every land.

| Cut | Name | Who |
| 0 | Blank | No godsign. Rare births; the Unfed of the Fast |
| 1 | Scrapling | Descended from those who licked the bones |
| 2 | Common | Those who ate the meat; most people alive |
| 3 | Prime | Old gentry; the hungry middle |
| 4–5 | High | Nobility of the choice Cut; the furthest along |

Each land prizes a different Cut: the Hand-lines of Sallowreach, the Gut-lines of the Fatlands, the Lung-lines of Brinehollow, the Eye-lines of the Vigil, the Caul-lines of Cradlewrack, the Tongue-lines of Oathen. Marriage between Cuts is arranged with ledgers. A high-Cut house that marries down dilutes its blood and its claim; a Scrapling who marries up buys rank and a faster death. In every land, the rulers are the most monstrous people in it, and the Regrowth advances fastest in palaces.

[pull] A Scrapling who marries up buys rank and a faster death. A Scrapling who marries down buys nothing, and lives. | — Rim proverb

### Scraplings

The poor descend from those who got the scraps and licked the bones: the stragglers at the back of the crowd, the too-weak, the too-slow, the ones who arrived after the good parts were gone. They are called **Scraplings**, and they are the least changed people alive. A Scrapling of Sallowreach still cannot die, a Scrapling of the Vigil still cannot sleep, because the Rule of each land touches everyone in it; but their Regrowth crawls where a noble's gallops. Scraplings do the work of the Table: they haul, stitch, bail, nudge and render. They have always been told that their thin blood is the mark of their ancestors' weakness. They are only now, in the last generation, beginning to understand that it is the reason they may outlive their masters, and some of them are beginning to say so out loud.

### Blanks

Rarely, a child is born with no godsign at all. A Fatlands Blank feels no hunger. An Oathen Blank can lie. A Vigil Blank could sleep, if anyone let it. No one knows why they happen; the College's ledgers show no pattern of parentage, and Blanks have been born to High Cuts and to Scraplings alike. They are hunted in every land: as saints, as specimens, or as spare parts.

### The Blank Trade

The trade is old, quiet, and enormously profitable. Blank blood, transfused, is believed to slow the Regrowth for a season. Blank organs, sewn into a noble body by a skilled Seamer or a Fatlands surgeon, are believed to slow it for years. Blank marrow is boiled into a jelly sold in the Vigil as a sleep-cure and in Cradlewrack as a contraceptive. None of it works, or none of it works for long, and the price only rises. A Blank kidney sells on the Rim for about 3,000 lacks; a Blank heart, delivered beating in brine, for a sum the buyers do not write down.

Because the Unfed of the Fast are, in effect, a whole nation of Blanks, the trade's main source is the heartland. Poachers cross the Fast's border by night with nets and sedatives. Fatlanders like Kale Dragoman feed their catch generously on the road home, so the meat arrives in condition. Dr. Mercy Thrane of the Fast has traced vanished patients to surgeries in four countries. The ultimate buyers are, almost always, people of High Cut, and an increasing number of the largest purchases are made through intermediaries who never give a name.

[pull] Blank kidney, fresh, three thousand. Blank heart, beating, in brine: ask. Blank girl, Fatlands, fifteen, alive: her weight in bone, and do not bruise her. | — price-slate found in a burned surgery outside Gristmoot, 640 A.G.

## The Shape of the Table

People call the known world the Table. It is one continent, roughly round, about nine hundred miles across, and its geography is a ring around a hole. Beyond its edges there is sea to the west and wasteland, mountain and salt on the other sides, and nobody who has gone looking for anything beyond has come back with anything worth the trip.

- **The Fast** lies at the center: the rich old heartland, the best farmland on the Table, now almost empty, where the uneaten god still walks. It is always early Carving there.
- **Sallowreach** to the north: grey fen and fog, where nothing dies.
- **The Vigil** to the northeast: a lit plateau city that has not slept in three centuries.
- **Oathen** to the east: dry red canyons where words break bones.
- **The Fatlands** to the south: warm, wet plains of meat.
- **Brinehollow** to the west: a coast the sea is abandoning.
- **Cradlewrack** to the northwest: red clay highlands in labor.

Nobody crosses the middle. The old roads that once ran from every land to the heartland are still there, still beautifully kept by no one, and they are used by Crumb-Runners, Reckoners, poachers, pilgrims and fools. Everyone else goes around.

### The Rim Road

The Rim Road is a ring of highway, some two thousand four hundred miles long, that runs around the border of the Fast, touching every land in turn. It was built in the Thin Years after the Gorging on the foundations of the old heartland ring-roads, by caravaneers who wanted to carry the Fatlands' miraculous grain to every market on the Table without ever setting foot in the empty middle. A wagon caravan takes about a hundred days to make the full circuit; a fast rider changing horses at every post can do it in thirty.

The road is paved for most of its length in great fitted slabs of grey Oathen stone, cambered for drainage, with a ditch on the outer side and, on the inner side, facing the Fast, a low wall about knee-high. The wall is called the Hem. It does nothing; anyone can step over it. Nobody does. Every mile is marked by a milestone with the distance carved in both directions to the Company's headquarters, and every milestone bears the Company's mark, a wheel with seven spokes.

Every land's politics is shaped by needing something from a neighbor it finds obscene, and all of that need moves along the Rim. Fatlands meat goes everywhere. Oathen sells contracts and gold bits. The Vigil buys oil and brine and sells mirrors and rouse. Sallowreach exports wire, lacquer, embalming spirits and, illegally, Closings. Brinehollow sells salvage from the Uncovered. Cradlewrack exports soldiers, the Foundling Companies hiring out as caravan guards. The Fast sells nothing, and buys every mouthful it eats.

[pull] Every land is certain that the cure is next door. | — Rim Road saying

#### Tolls

The Rim Road Company owns the road, every inn and post on it, and every toll. Tolls are charged at every waystation, posted in Table-speech on a bone slate by the gate, and paid in lacks or Company platters. A standard schedule:

| Toll | Charged On | Rate | Note |
| Foot | Each traveler on foot | 1 crumb a post | children under seven free |
| Rider | Each traveler mounted | 3 crumbs a post | the horse is charged as Head |
| Wheel | Each wheel of a cart or wagon | 2 crumbs a post | the Groaning Board charges Fatlands drays by the axle |
| Head | Each animal, or anything walked like one | 1 crumb a post | Sal Trotter's herds pay as Head; nobody checks |
| Weight | Cargo, by the hundredweight | 1 lack a post | Chute barrels sealed and unopened |
| Breath | Any traveler showing godsign at the Course | 5 lacks a post | waived for Company officers |
| Hush | Corpses, by the body | 1 lack a post | the Kept are not corpses; they pay as Foot, once |
| Litter | Each traveler carried by bearers | 1 lack a bearer a post | the Laden of the Fatlands, mostly |
| Cradle | A traveler with child, leaving Cradlewrack | 2 lacks at Knocking Gate | paid after the Marshal's inspection, if let through |
| Cage | Live cargo in a closed cart | 3 lacks a post | no questions; the Company calls it livestock |
| Seal | A courier under an Oathen oath | none | the Company will not delay a sworn word |
| Ditch | Sleeping within a bowshot of the road | 2 crumbs a night | collected at dawn by whoever finds you |

The Breath Toll was introduced in 604 A.G. after a Course-stage Gut-line rooted himself in the middle of the road near the Chute and had to be dug around. The Hush Toll is so called because it is charged on the dead, who in every land but one are silent.

The Cage Toll is the most profitable on the Rim and the one the Company least likes discussed. It was introduced in 301 A.G., the year of the first recorded poaching out of the Fast. The Company's position is that it carries what it is paid to carry and does not open the cart. The Fast's envoy, Prudence Lowell, has read the toll schedule aloud at every Company congress since she was appointed, slowly, and always stops at that line.

[pull] Toll paid, grace said, mind your head. | — rhyme painted on the gate-bars at Gristmoot

#### Rim Law

The Company's charter, sworn in Oathen in 71 A.G., grants it jurisdiction over the road surface and a bowshot either side. Within that strip, no land's law runs; Company law runs instead, and Company law has three articles, recited at every post at dawn: *Pay the toll. Keep the peace. Say the grace.* Murder on the road is punished by the Company's wardens with a rope at the nearest post. Theft of Company cargo is punished by being made cargo. Refusing the grace at a Company table, the universal insult, is punished with a fine of one lack and a night outside the gate.

The Rim's neutrality is its whole value. A Sallowreach Closer and a Fast Reckoner can share an inn table on the Rim, and each knows that if the other draws a blade the wardens will hang them before the blood is dry. They cannot share it anywhere else on earth.

The Charter of 71 was the Company's second. Its first, the simple bond of the seven caravan-houses, was sworn at Tacit in 61 A.G., the year Oathen's Rule first came to be trusted abroad; the Ring Charter of 71 extended it to the road itself, the strip a bowshot wide, and the Hem. Company lawyers cite both. Oathen's Ledger records the first and the Company's milestones the second, and the College considers the ten years between them the most important decade in the history of commerce.

### Customs of the Company Inns

Every Company house keeps the Three Articles. Most keep a great deal more, by custom older than anyone can explain, and a traveler who learns them is fed better and robbed less.

- **Salt and a crumb.** A guest arriving after dark is met at the gate with a pinch of salt in the palm and a single crumb. The salt is licked; the crumb is kept, and given back on leaving. A guest who leaves without returning the crumb is said to have *taken a bite of the house*, and is not let in again.
- **The grace at the gate.** At Company houses the grace is said twice: once at the table, and once at the gate, by the warden on duty to each party that comes in, whether or not they are eating. *Lack keep away.* A party that cannot answer is watched.
- **The honest bench.** Every common room has one long bench nearest the fire where strangers of different lands are seated together, by the factor's choice, not their own. It is a Rim saying that more marriages, partnerships and murders have begun on the honest bench than anywhere else on the Table.
- **The Night Count.** At dusk and at dawn the innkeeper walks the house and counts heads aloud, and writes both numbers in the ledger. If the dawn count is lower than the dusk count, the wardens are called. If it is higher, nobody is called, and the extra place at breakfast is set and left empty, and the innkeeper burns the page at noon.
- **The head of the table.** The long table in a Company common room has a chair at its head, and nobody sits in it, not the factor, not a Spoke. Ask why and you are told that it is the Company's chair. Nobody has ever seen the Company sit.
- **Licked-clean nights.** On the new moon no guest leaves the common room after the last bell. Chamber-pots are provided. A guest who insists on going out to the privy is let out, and the door is barred behind them, and they are let in again at dawn if they knock in the right voice.
- **Land manners.** Company houses keep rope rooms for Brinehollowers, nudgers for Vigil guests, slates for Oatheners, brick-in rooms near Cradlewrack, and a basin of vinegar by the door for anyone from Sallowreach. No Company house will refuse a Fatlander a fifth meal. No Company house has ever had an Unfed guest who would accept a fourth.
- **The Plate's share.** Every traveler leaves one crumb under the plate at their last meal before the road. It is for the next traveler. In practice the potboys take it; in theory it buys the next traveler's luck. It is the only charity on the Rim that everyone gives.
- **Tablenight.** The gates close at dusk, the extra chair is set at every table, turned to face the wall, and nobody in the house says *come in* to anyone for any reason until dawn.

[pull] Never answer a knock on a licked-clean night. Never answer it twice. | — Rim innkeepers' rule

### Waystations of the Rim

The Company keeps a post every twenty miles: a toll gate, a stable, a well and a bone-slate board of news. Every hundred miles or so there is a waystation proper, a walled inn-town with a market, a smithy, a wardens' barracks and the Company's factor. Clockwise from the north, the best known are these.

**Fogmouth.** The northern station, where the road from Lastgate meets the Rim. A painted line crosses the road outside the gate, red, renewed every Grace: the Drop. North of it, the Kept walk. South of it, they fall. Families from Sallowreach come to the line to say goodbye to living relatives going abroad, and stand on their side, and hold hands over it. The inn, the Long Farewell, serves vinegar-wine and has a back room where Tam Ruddock's clients wait to cross north. Rooms smell of cedar if you pay for it.

**Quire.** Between Fogmouth and Lampwick Halt, on the northeastern stretch, a walled college-town of grey stone on a green hill: the seat of the Cutwrights' College. The Company keeps a post at its foot and an inn, the Inkhorn, full of Linemen in grey, nervous noble agents waiting on certificates, and genealogists for hire who will trace any line for a fee and find whatever the client prefers. The library sits on the hill; the vault is under it, cool with spring water. Every guest at the Inkhorn is asked their mother's name by the porter, in a friendly way, and it is written down.

**Lampwick Halt.** At the Vigil's border on the plateau road. Never dark: the Company keeps a hundred lamps burning on the walls to accommodate Vigil merchants, who will not stop in a place they cannot see. Under Vigil custom there are no beds within sight of the plateau, so the Company built the Sleeping Sheds a quarter mile down the road on the Rim side of a stone marker, long low dormitories with shutters, where travelers bound for the Vigil take their last sleep for months. Rouse is sold at the gate. Many travelers lie awake in the Sheds all night anyway, out of nerves.

**Mumchance.** On the canyon road where the Rim enters Oathen. The quietest inn in the world. Orders are taken on slates; the bill is chalked; the innkeeper, a Forsworn woman whose jaw broke on a promise thirty years ago, nods. The walls are hung with felt so nothing echoes. A sign over the door, in four dialects, reads *Say Less*. Foreign travelers are given a printed card of safe phrases on arrival and a lecture by gesture. Every year someone ignores it and leaves on a stretcher.

**Treaty Stair.** A Company depot cut into a canyon wall a day east of Mumchance, at the head of the long stair down to Tacit. Here every Company contract is copied, sealed in wax and bone, and carried down to be sworn before the Company's notary, Jessamy Quill. The Stair is guarded more heavily than any treasury on the Table. Its clerks work in silence. Its archive holds, in theory, the binding text of half the commerce in the world.

**Quillhouse.** The Company's eastern waystation on the high causeway across the north of Oathen, where the road leaves the canyons for the open plateau. Felted bridges, slates at every door, and a long stone wall carved by travelers with the conditions of their promises: *I will come back for you, if able, barring weather.* The factor's clerks remind every traveler, by slate, what country they are in, and the inn, the Exit, keeps a Clausewright on retainer to read the small print on any deal struck at its tables.

**Wanting Post.** On the inner side of the eastern Rim, at the head of the road into the Fast's only town. The only Company station that faces the middle. The Crumb-Runners buy grain here at a markup the Company calls a risk premium. The Fast's envoy, Prudence Lowell, keeps an office in a bare stone room off the yard and receives all callers standing. Travelers who arrive after dark are asked, at the gate, whether anyone has invited them in on the road. It is not a joke.

**Saltlick Cross.** The southeastern crossroads where the dry country gives way to the Fatlands' first green. The old heartland road meets the Rim here, running straight in toward Hallowboard, and the crossroads is marked by four stone posts, each with a lamp. In 639 A.G. a table was found laid in the middle of the crossing at dawn, with eight places, steaming. The Company had it burned and the ground salted, and posted a warden on the spot every night since. The wardens rotate weekly. None will say what they see.

**Thimble Cross.** On the southeastern stretch, where the Rim, coming up out of the Fatlands, bends hard to skirt a lobe of the Fast. A two-storey inn of grey fieldstone with seventeen chimneys, a walled yard, a smokehouse, and a little graveyard of nine stones. A rutted track called the Hungry Lane runs off from the bend into the gold country toward Wanting, used by Crumb-Runners and Hostwatch and nobody else. The station has been kept by the Thimble family since 402 A.G. under a charter sworn in Oathen, whose one strange clause binds the keepers never to turn a traveler away, nor refuse any guest. The wardens say it is the friendliest house on the Rim. Lately it has had more callers than it can feed.

**The Groaning Board.** On the Fatlands stretch, two days north of the Chute. The Company's largest inn, with a common room that seats six hundred at a single table, built to impress Fatlands merchants and to feed them five times a day as the law requires. The floor slopes so that grease runs to a gutter. The smell reaches travelers a day out. The dishes are excellent, the portions vast, the meat unspecified. Foreign guests are advised to eat lightly and to watch the table's far end, where the Gut-line merchants sit, and to not be there when the wardens take one away at closing to be rolled out by six men.

**The Chute.** The Fatlands' Rim Road export depot, technically the Renderers' Union's yard and not the Company's, and therefore the one place on the ring where Company law runs only on sufferance. Barrels go out by the thousand a day with the origin stamped over twice. The Company keeps a factor and a weighhouse here and a standing order not to open anything.

**Gristmoot.** A market town on a bend of the Rim a day west of the Chute, where every stall-keeper on the road comes at last: rope by the fathom, Seamers' thread, Fatlands sausage by the yard, Oathen paper, Vigil glass, cures. The great Rim Market is held here four times a year, at the turn of each season, and lasts a Plate. Prices are chalked on bone slates; haggling is loud; pickpockets are hanged on the market cross in the morning and cut down by noon so as not to put off the trade.

**Toll Nine.** The Company's counting-house: a long, low, windowless building of grey Oathen stone on the southern stretch between Gristmoot and Saltbridge, so named because it stands on the site of the ninth toll-gate of the seven founding houses' road. The Company's treasury of platters is here, and its oldest ledgers, and the clerks who reconcile every post's take with every other's. Over the door is painted *Everything on the Table has a price. The trick is finding out who is paying it.* There is a cellar under the counting-house with a locked room that the factor visits weekly, alone, with a measuring rod. Nobody else goes down.

**Saltbridge.** Where the Fatlands road meets the Brinehollow road, on the southwestern stretch: the Company's Cure-House, the only lawful mint on the Table since 212 A.G. Bone comes in from the Chute in sealed carts stamped BONE: MIXED; salt comes up from the coast, boiled out of what Brinehollow coughs up at dawn and dusk; lacks go out. The steam of the copper vats can be smelled from the next toll. The people of Saltbridge are famous for their good skin and their bad dreams, and the coin-cutters, mostly Scraplings on twenty-year bonds, have the steadiest hands on the Rim and the worst nightmares.

**Brackwater Bridge.** On the southwestern stretch, a magnificent stone bridge of eleven arches carrying the Rim over the estuary of the river Slake, which ran into the sea. The sea left in 512 A.G. and the river followed it out across the Uncovered. The bridge now spans a dry bed of cracked mud and the hulls of barges, sitting upright. The Company still charges the bridge toll. The waystation inn, the Dry Anchor, is full of salvagers and Admiralty recruiters, and the cellars flood twice a day.

**Lowmark Spur.** Where the Rim meets the road down to Brinehollow's capital. Travelers coming up from the coast are checked here for the pull: wardens watch who stands too long at the west windows. The inn keeps a rack of bail-buckets for those who forgot theirs and a rope room where any guest may ask to be tied to the bed for the night, at a crumb a knot.

**Knocking Gate.** At the Cradlewrack border on the red-hill road, where Marshal Gideon Breech's men check every traveler leaving the highlands for signs of carrying. The Company's inn here, the Open Door, has no doors at all: every room has a curtain, by agreement with the Attendance, so that nothing can be accused of breaking in. Visitors knock on the frames out of politeness. Occasionally, something knocks back from inside an empty room.

**The Weeping Post.** On the northwestern stretch between Cradlewrack and Fogmouth, where weep-rain from Sallowreach meets the warm salt springs of the red hills, and the road is always wet and always tea-stained. A Confessors' chapel stands here, the oldest Theft shrine on the Rim, and a rotating congregation of travelers from both lands kneels in the brown water and asks forgiveness for the Meal. The inn is cheap and damp and everyone in it is sorry about something.

[box: The Rim Road in Play]
The Rim is the default campaign spine. Characters from different lands meet here, under Company law, and can travel together without being arrested at every border. Use the waystations as hubs: each one is a crossroads of factions, a source of jobs (escort, salvage, smuggling, auditing, finding someone who did not arrive), and a stage for the land next door to intrude. A circuit of the Rim is a hundred days; a campaign can be built as a single circuit, beginning and ending at the same gate, with the world measurably worse on the second visit.
[/box]

## Time, Seasons, and Sky

The year is 641 A.G., After Gorging. Year 0 is the year of the Night of Seven Tables; the nine years of the Long Lack before it are numbered backward by scholars as the Lack Years, Lack 1 to Lack 9, and by everyone else not at all.

The year has four seasons named for a meal: **Grace** (spring), **Plenty** (summer), **Carving** (autumn) and **Lack** (winter). Each season has three months, and the months are named in common Rim usage by the Plate: First Plate, Middle Plate and Last Plate of Grace, and so on. Dates are given as day, Plate and season: "the ninth of Middle Plenty". The year turns on Tablenight.

The moon is called **the Plate**. A full moon is *a full Plate*; a waning one is *being cleared*; a new moon is *licked clean*, and travelers do not set out on licked-clean nights. Stars are called crumbs in most dialects; the Vigil, which cannot see them through its lamp-smoke, has no word for them.

Each land's seasons are bent by its Rule. Sallowreach has no real winter, because winter is an ending, and leaves stay yellow on the branch for decades. The Fast is always early Carving. The Fatlands' first frost is *Marbling*. Oathen has one rain a year, the Kept Rain, on the first day of Grace, and this year it came three days late. The calendar is shared, but the weather is not.

[pull] Licked clean tonight. Stay in, bar the door, and don't count the plates. | — Rim almanac, entry for every new moon

### Tablenight

Tablenight is the anniversary of the Gorging, held on the longest night of Lack. It was once the feast of the longest night, the happiest festival of the old world. Every land now keeps it, and every land keeps it differently.

| Land | Tablenight |
| Sallowreach | The living cook a banquet for their dead, who cannot taste it; everyone pretends |
| Fatlands | A one-day fast in penance; no village has ever reached sundown |
| Brinehollow | A bowl of seawater at the head of the table; the west door unbarred for whoever comes home |
| The Vigil | Every lamp put out for one minute; people hold hands and count aloud |
| Cradlewrack | Every door propped wide until dawn so nothing can be said to break in |
| Oathen | Twenty-four hours without a sound; the only day Oatheners feel safe |
| The Fast | Everyone stands outdoors, dusk to dawn, backs to every door; in every house, the table is laid |
| The Rim Road | Inns close their gates at dusk; one extra chair is set at every table, turned to face the wall |

Nobody knows when the Rim custom began. The Company's oldest inn ledgers record it as already old in 90 A.G. Innkeepers who are asked about it say the chair is *for the one who isn't coming*, and change the subject. Travelers who turn the chair back around in the night find it turned to the wall again in the morning.

[box: Festivals in Play]
Festivals are the best way to tell players what a land is like without a lecture. Time a party's arrival at a waystation to a festival next door, and let them watch: the Unbricking from the road at Knocking Gate, the Kept Rain falling (or not) from the felted bridges at Quillhouse, the Lists being read in a Fatlands square. Make a d6 roll at each festival a party attends: on a 1, one of them breaks a custom without knowing it, and the GM chooses who noticed.

## Things All Lands Share

### Table-speech

The common tongue, older than the Gorging, spoken in some form in every land: drawled and wet in the fens, clipped to almost nothing in Oathen, stretched and slurred in the Vigil. Its oldest root words are the ones the Providers' peoples shared, and among them is the root *orr-*. From it come *orren*, to eat; *orrel*, to worship or to give back; *orrish*, a shared meal; and *Orrum*, the name of the Host, meaning roughly *the one at whose board*. In Table-speech the words for "worship" and "eat" share a root. Scholars insist they always did. The Purgation insists this is the whole problem.

Table-speech is rich in food-words used for everything: a coward is *underdone*, a fool is *bread without salt*, death in most dialects is *being cleared*, and a person who has been ruined is *picked*. To ask someone's Cut, politely, is to ask *what portion* they come of. There are two words for *guest*. Everyone uses the newer.

| Word | Meaning | Literally |
| orren | to eat | to take at the board |
| orrel | to worship; to give back | to feed the board |
| orrish | a shared meal | of the board |
| underdone | a coward | not cooked through |
| bread without salt | a fool | (as it says) |
| picked | ruined | the bones left on the plate |
| cleared | dead | the plate taken away |
| licked clean | a new moon; utterly lost | nothing left on the Plate |

### The Lack

The one coin honored everywhere: a disc of salt-cured bone the width of a thumb-joint, pierced at the middle for stringing, worth one day's bread, and stamped on one face with the Plate and on the other with an empty circle. Ten **crumbs**, small bone beads, make a lack; twenty lacks make a **platter**, a sealed bone tablet issued by the Rim Road Company and sworn in Oathen as redeemable at any Company post. The coin takes its name and its value from the Dole-bones of the seventh year of the Lack, the ration-tallies of the public kitchens, which were cut from the rendered dead. The First Lacks proper were cut from god-bone in the year after the Gorging, by anyone with a saw. Since 212 A.G. every lawful lack has been struck at the Company's Cure-House at Saltbridge, from Fatlands bone shipped out of the Chute in carts stamped BONE: MIXED, cured for a season in salt boiled from Brinehollow bail-water. The Company says the bone is cattle. The empty circle, its charter says, represents "the bread to be bought"; some old coin-cutters say it is a chair, seen from above. A few Dole-bones and First Lacks still circulate. They are yellower and smoother than the new coin, and people who find one in their change tend to spend it quickly. (See *The Rim Market* for coin, clipping and counterfeits.)

[pull] When the price of bread rises, nobody says bread is dear. They say the lack is thin. | — Rim saying

### The Grace

Nobody on the Table eats with a stranger without first saying *Lack keep away*. It is said with eyes lowered, hand flat on the table, before the first bite. Among family it is optional; among strangers, it is the law of every land and of the Rim. Skipping it is the universal insult: it says *I do not care if you starve*. Duels have been fought over it in Brinehollow, oaths broken over it in Oathen, and in the Fast, where nobody shares food at all, it is said anyway, to the empty air, before eating out of the hand.

## Common Folk Everywhere

> The lords of every land are monsters, each in their own fashion. The poor of every land are the same people with different buckets.
>> — Odalys Penhallow, First Spoke of the Wheel, in a letter to her Spokes, 630 A.G.

Most people on the Table are not nobles, not Seamers, not Clausewrights or Wakers or Reckoners. They are Scraplings and Commons, Cut 1 and 2, who haul and stitch and bail and nudge and render, who will reach the Course late or never, and who spend their lives in the shadow of a Regrowth that is happening fastest to someone else. They are born, they marry, they die, and every land has bent each of those three things into its own shape. A traveler who knows nothing else about a land should know these.

### What the Poor Share

Scraplings recognize one another across every border. They work the same hours (dawn to dark, and in the Vigil, dark to dawn as well). They eat Fatlands meat out of the same barrels and do not examine it. They are paid in crumbs strung on a loaf-cord and they bite every lack. They know the price of bread in three lands and the price of a Blank kidney in none, because nobody has ever offered to buy anything of theirs. Their children play the same games under different names: *Called* in Brinehollow and *Blink* in the Vigil and *Who's for the Pot* everywhere. They bury, render, shelve, brick in or lay face-down their dead with less ceremony than their lords and more grief.

And in the last generation they have begun to notice the same thing. The College's figures, which the College does not publish, say that the Regrowth runs fastest in palaces. The poor do not need the figures. They can see it from the street: the lord who has not walked in thirty years, the duchess behind her caul, the prince in his tank, the Regent who is mostly wire. They are beginning to say, in every land, in kitchens and on the honest bench, that the thinness of their blood is not a mark of their ancestors' weakness but of their ancestors' luck, and that when the Grace ends, it will end at the top of the table first. Some of them say it with fear. More and more of them say it with something else.

## The Trade in Cures

> Every land believes another land's curse might cure its own. Nothing has ever worked. The trade never slows.
>> — the first line of every Rim apothecary's licence

| Land | Wants to Cure | Buys | From |
| The Vigil | Sleep | The undeath of the Kept; bottled brine | Sallowreach; Brinehollow |
| Fatlands | Hunger | The emptiness of the Unfed | The Fast |
| Sallowreach | Endlessness | Births, beginnings, Cradlewrack cord | Cradlewrack |
| Brinehollow | The Calling | Oaths to stay ashore; anchors of Oathen iron | Oathen |
| Cradlewrack | Too many births | Endings: Hand-line Closings | Sallowreach |
| Oathen | Binding speech | Sleep-drugs that silence the tongue | The Vigil |
| Everyone | The Regrowth | Blank organs | The Fast |

Every apothecary on the Rim sells something. Bottled Kept-breath from Sallowreach. Pills of Fatlands fat. Fast dust, which is earth from the Fast's roads, sold by the pinch to Fatlanders who believe it kills appetite. Cradlewrack cord, dried, worn as a belt by Sallowreach elders who want to be able to begin again. Most of it is fraud. Some of it does something, and what it does is worse.

The trade in cures is the Rim's second commerce after meat, and nearly as large. Every waystation has an apothecary; every apothecary has a shelf of bottles labelled in four dialects and a back room with the real stock; every real stock is either fraud or godflesh. The Company licenses the shelves, taxes the bottles and does not inspect the back rooms. The Purgation calls the whole trade blasphemy and runs a brisk line in emetics. The College buys one of everything, and tests it, and publishes nothing.

What follows is a sampling of what a traveler will be offered at any Rim market, what it is sold as, and what it actually does. GMs may roll when a character goes shopping for a cure, or when a stranger presses one on them at an inn.

[box: Cures in Play]
A cure is a cheap way to give the players hope and a cheaper way to take it away. Let every character who suffers under their land's Rule hear of something, somewhere on the Rim, that is supposed to help. Let some of them buy it. The rule for cures is simple: nothing cures, but almost everything does something, and the something should always be interesting. A character who drinks a cure in desperation and gets a new Want, a stranger's god, or a place card with their name on it has had a better session than one who drank something that did nothing.
[/box]

## Powers That Cross Borders

Five institutions operate in every land and answer to none. Each has its own reasons for wanting player characters, and each has its own ways of using them up. Each entry below gives the power's history and creed, its rank ladder (with the mark by which its members know one another), how a character joins, the rite by which they are taken in, the secret at its heart, and the people who lead it.

### The Rim Road Company

[sigil: rim]

> Pay the toll. Keep the peace. Say the grace.
>> — the Three Articles, recited at every post at dawn

The merchant league that owns the ring road, its inns, its posts and its tolls. It is neutral, rich, and the only institution every government needs. It was founded in the Thin Years by seven caravan-houses carrying the Fatlands' miracle grain to every market, and its emblem is still a wheel with seven spokes, one for each founding house. In 71 A.G. it swore its Ring Charter in Oathen, and has sworn every contract there since, where they cannot be broken. Every Company platter, every inn-lease, every toll-farm, every bond of service is a promise sworn before the Company's notary, Jessamy Quill, and if broken the breaker's body breaks to match.

The Company is governed by the Wheel, seven Spokes descended by election from the seven founding houses, presided over by the First Spoke. It has no army, only the road-wardens, and no country, only the strip of stone a bowshot wide. It needs none. Every land would starve, go dark or come apart in a season without the Rim.

**Beliefs.** The Company believes in the road. It holds no doctrine about the gods, says the grace at every meal, and regards every land's horror as a pricing problem. Privately, its senior Spokes believe the Table has perhaps twenty years left, and are determined that the Company should be the last institution standing when it ends.

**What it wants now.** Oathen's rain was late. If a god's sworn word can slip, so can every Company contract, and the whole of the Rim's commerce rests on one notary's body. The Company is quietly auditing everything, pricing every land's collapse, and looking for something better than Oathen to swear by.

| Rank | Title | Grants | Mark |
| 1 | Bonded Hand | Wage of 3 lacks a day; a bed at any post; Company protection on the road | an iron wrist-ring of seven spokes, hammered shut |
| 2 | Waymaster | Command of a caravan; may call on wardens at any post | a brass whistle and the caravan's slate |
| 3 | Factor | Runs a waystation; may issue platters and hear Rim cases | the gate keys and a platter-seal |
| 4 | Toll-Lord | Governs a quarter of the Rim; sits in judgment under Company law | a black staff shod in bone |
| 5 | Spoke | One of the seven on the Wheel | a gold spoke worn at the throat |

**Joining.** Any traveler can hire on as a guard, porter or drover for a single circuit. To become a Bonded Hand, a character must travel to Treaty Stair and swear the Bond: to serve the Company, carry its goods, keep its peace and steal nothing from it, *as long as the wheel turns*. The oath is sworn in Oathen and binds the body anywhere. Bonded Hands do not steal from the Company. Those who have tried are on display at Treaty Stair, as an instruction.

#### The Bond

The candidate walks down the long stair to Tacit in silence, a day's descent, past the Company's display. The display is a gallery cut into the canyon wall and glazed, and behind the glass sit the Bonded Hands who stole from the Company, alive, at clerks' desks, with their hands folded backward at the wrist where the oath broke them. They are fed. They are given work they cannot do. The candidate is made to stop at each window. At the foot of the stair the candidate kneels on a paving-slab taken from the Rim itself, lays one hand on a milestone carried down for the purpose, and swears the Bond aloud before Jessamy Quill, slowly, in the Oathen manner, with no exits. Then a smith closes the iron ring around their wrist with three blows, and the candidate climbs back up the stair to the road, and is given a bed at the first post, and does not sleep.

[fiction]
I will serve the Company, and carry its goods, and keep its peace, and take nothing of it that is not given me, as long as the wheel turns.
[/fiction]
>> — the Bond, entire; Quill asks each candidate, before they swear it, whether they understand the last six words, and records their answer

#### The Secret

The seal is slipping. The Company knows that Oathen's rain was late, and it knows something worse: that Jessamy Quill once swore no other notary of the Rim would be sworn while she lives, and that she is old. If she dies, every platter on the Table becomes a pretty piece of shoulder in an afternoon. The Wheel is secretly grooming a second notary, in defiance of an oath it did not know about until last year, and has lost two candidates already, to accidents. And under that secret there is another, which only the coin-cutters of Saltbridge and the First Spoke know: since the Reaping doubled, the carts from the Chute stamped BONE: MIXED carry more bone than the herds of the south could possibly account for, and the Company has never once complained about the quality. Every lack struck in the last ten years has a better than even chance of having been someone.

[pull] We do not open the cart. We have never opened the cart. That is why they let us carry it. | — Factor of the Chute, to a Vigil magistrate, 640 A.G.

### The Cutwrights' College

[sigil: rim]

> Blood is a ledger. We only read it aloud.
>> — motto over the gate at Quire

Genealogists who track every bloodline's Cut across six centuries. The College began among the tally-priests of the Bone Dole, who kept the lists of who had eaten what in the public kitchens of the seventh year, and among the scribes who recorded, on the morning after the Gorging, which family had carried home which portion of which god. By 140 A.G. those lists were the most valuable documents on the Table, and their keepers built a walled college-town at **Quire**, on the northeastern Rim between Fogmouth and Lampwick Halt, to keep them.

The College's ledgers decide marriages, inheritances and executions. A College certificate of Cut is accepted in law in every land; a College genealogy can make a bastard an heir, or prove that an heir is a Scrapling. Its Provosts sit in every capital. The northern Provost, Carrion Sedge, has proven that the Hushes follow Hand-lines blood exactly. Whoever holds the ledgers knows exactly where each god is regrowing fastest, and the College holds all of them, in the Master Concordance at Quire, in a vault cut into the hill under the library, kept at a constant cool by spring water.

**Beliefs.** The College is officially doctrine-neutral and in practice dominated by quiet Baiters. Its scholars have seen the curves. They know that every land's Regrowth bent upward at once after 600 A.G., which means that the seven Regrowths are one event, and they do not say so outside the walls.

**What it wants now.** To keep the ledgers secret and whole. To be the institution that understands the end when it comes. And, among the senior Cutwrights, to answer the question of the Eighth, which the College has been forbidden to ask by every land in turn and has been asking quietly since 588.

| 1 | Copyist | Board at Quire or a chapter house; access to the open stacks | a grey gown; an ink-black right thumb |
| 2 | Lineman | Field genealogist; may demand parish, temple and Tally records in any land | a bleeding-lancet and a ledger-case |
| 3 | Cutwright | May certify Cuts, binding in law in every land | a silver spoon on a chain |
| 4 | Provost | Heads a land's chapter; holds its ledger | a ring of keys to that ledger |
| 5 | Archprovost | Holds the Master Concordance at Quire; there is only one | none; she is known |

**Joining.** Copyists are taken young or by examination at Quire. Every entrant gives a pint of blood, which is tasted, graded and entered, and their line is traced back to the Meal. The College knows exactly what every member is, and how fast they are going. It uses this.

#### The Copying

After the blood is tasted, graded and entered, the new Copyist is led down into the vault under the library at Quire, where the spring water runs in a channel along the floor and the air smells of cold stone and old ink. There, at a desk under a single lamp, they are given their own line in the Master Concordance and a blank quire, and told to copy it, by hand, from themselves back to the Meal. It takes most Copyists three days. Nobody is permitted to help, or to stop them. Everything is in the Concordance: the bastardies, the cousin-marriages, the households that traded tables in the Kitchen Year, the eldest who walked and the eldest who did not, the great-grandmother who was served at her own wake and the great-grandfather who did the serving. Many Copyists weep. Some are sick into the channel. A few have tried to tear the page, and been gently restrained, and been given another quire, and told to begin again. On the last page, below the line of the Meal, the Copyist writes their own name, and beneath it, from the College's tables, the year in which their line may be expected to reach the Course. Then they are given their grey gown, and the copy is taken from them and filed, and they never see it again.

[pull] Blood is a ledger. We only read it aloud. We do not always read it to you. | — Lineman's saying

#### The Secret

The College knows the seven Regrowths are one event, and has known since the curves were first set side by side. That is the secret every senior Cutwright keeps. The deeper one is in a box of grey stone beside the Master Concordance, in the vault: a living thing, soft and red and the size of a mouse, carried out of the highlands in an apron pocket on the night of the Meal, which begins again, newly wet, at every pang in Cradlewrack. The College's oldest copyists, who should have died a decade ago, visit the vault on the first of every month. They come out looking as if they had just begun. The Archprovost has never written down what she uses it for.

### The Purgation

[sigil: fatlands]

> What goes down can come up.
>> — painted on the Purgation's altar-bowls

A cross-border movement that believes the gods can be thrown back up. It was born at the Weeping Post in 233 A.G., when a Fatlands physician named Hurlo Sweyn took a draught of his own devising before a congregation of Confessors and brought up, after six hours of retching, a hard white thing the size of a thumbnail which he swore was one of Ummer's teeth. The Great Retching spread up and down the Rim within a year. Its emblem is a bowl.

The Purgation's methods run from fasting and bleeding to the Black Draught, a purgative of bitter roots, salt and lamp-oil taken in public; to burning, on the theory that a god cooked out of the flesh cannot regrow; to massacre. Its militant wing, the Lancets, holds that a high-Cut house is a swelling in the world's gut and must be lanced. The Lancing of Brookhythe in 488 A.G., when a Lancet company bled out a Gut-line household of three hundred, servants and all, into the drains of their own manor, is its proudest and most infamous act.

**Beliefs.** The Purgation holds the Theft doctrine at its hardest: we stole the gods, the gods are inside us, and the only penance is to give them back by any opening available. Its preachers now call for a second Eldest Walk: the firstborn of every High Cut house, walked to the Rim and emptied, so that this time the giving will be done to the gods rather than for them.

#### The Bowl

The rite is held at dusk in a Purgation hall or in the yard of a Rim inn, wherever a congregation can be gathered. The candidate kneels on bare stone in a ring of Penitents holding lamps. A Purger brings the Black Draught in a pewter cup: bitter roots, salt and lamp-oil, black as tar, smelling of a snuffed wick, warm. The candidate drinks it all, and the congregation sings the Bowl Hymn, slow and low, while the Draught works. It takes an hour, sometimes three. The candidate retches into the bowl until the bowl is full and is emptied and is full again, until there is nothing left but bile, and then past that, and the singing never stops, and the Penitents hold the candidate's shoulders and forehead and say *up, up, give it up* in the gaps of the hymn. At the end, something comes up. It always does. The Purger lifts it from the bowl with two fingers and holds it to the lamps for the congregation to see: a stone, a hair-ball, a knot of gristle, a tooth. The congregation names it. Whatever they name it, the candidate is a Penitent from that moment, and is given the bowl, still warm, to wash and keep.

#### The Secret

The Draught works, a little. What comes up, when something does, is sometimes godflesh: a sliver of grey, a fleck of loam, a curl of caul, a scrap of something that tastes of the sea. The Voided have known this for a century, and they sell it. The halls' soup, the Lancets' spears, the surgeons who cut Saint Ancel Ruel, all are paid for out of the bowl, and the bowl's best customer, through three layers of agents, is the Second Table, which buys every leaving the Purgation brings up and serves it at practice dinners. The two movements on the Table most opposed to one another have been feeding each other since 512.

### The Second Table

[sigil: eighth]

> It worked once.
>> — the whole of the Second Table's creed, engraved inside its members' signet rings

A hidden cult with one idea: when the gods finish regrowing, eat them again. Its members are mostly nobles, High Cuts of every land, and they are preparing. The first trace of it in any record is a menu found in the coat of a murdered Gut-line lordling in 412 A.G., listing seven courses, each named for a god, in a hand nobody could identify. The College has since collected forty more such menus. They are always seven courses. On the most recent ones, found in the last ten years, there is an eighth line at the bottom, left blank.

The Second Table meets in private dining rooms on the Rim and in the cellars of great houses, a dozen at a time, never more. Members address one another by course: the Fish, the Bread, the Sweet. They practice. They have eaten the fruit of Seated Laden hills, the grey fingertips of Course-stage Hand-lines, the lidless eyes of the Vigil's dead nobility, scraps of caul, and the quiet of a Tenanted heart. They keep notebooks of the effects. They have commissioned knives.

**Beliefs.** The Gorging ended the Lack. It made the eaters' ancestors into lords. If it is done again, by the right people, with preparation, the gods will be put down once more and the world given another six hundred years of grace, with the diners at its head. The first meal was eaten by a mob, shared too widely, and spread thin, which is why it did not hold; the second will be eaten by a few. Some members believe they will become gods. Most just believe they will be fed.

**What it wants now.** Blank organs, to make room. The College's ledgers, to know which gods will be ripe first. The Sayer's sentence, to make the meal certain. And, through the heretic Wont Bellamy of the Fast, the one god still whole: Orrum, the Host, as the first course.

The Second Table keeps chapters in every land, a dozen at a table: in the cellar beneath Dame Suet Marrable's Unending Dinner in the Fatlands, among the Commodores of the Admiralty in Brinehollow, at the Court in Lastgate, in the Height of the Vigil, where Lady Seraphine Dalgety was raised to Carver in 639. Members wear a silk veil embroidered with a silver fork at dinner, and a little enamel badge shaped like a two-tined fork inside the collar on the road, and they recognize one another at any Rim inn by the way they hold their knives.

#### The Secret

There is no Head of the Table. ### The Reckoners

[sigil: fast]

> Refusing was not enough.
>> — Reckoner watchword

Zealots out of the Fast who cross into other lands to kill the Godeaters' highest bloodlines. The first armed band crossed the Rim in 402 A.G.; they have not stopped since. Reckoners travel light, eat only what they carried, and carry nothing that can be traced. They select High Cut families from stolen College genealogies, cross the Hem at night, kill in beds, and are gone by dawn. The Rim Road Company hangs every Reckoner it catches on Company ground, and the Reckoners have never once killed on the road surface. Their full history, beliefs and their great captain Scant Hollis are described in the chapter on the Fast.

The first band was nineteen young Unfed under a woman named Forswear Kell, who crossed the Rim Road into the Fatlands on the night of the sixteenth of Lack, 402, and entered the manor of a Gut-line family named Brawne through the kitchen, and killed everyone in it, the Laden matriarch in her bed and her sons and their sons, and came home without having eaten or drunk or sat in the house, and was received at Wanting in silence. [box: Cross-Border Powers in a Campaign]
Every party should have a reason to be on the Rim, and the five powers supply most of them. The Company hires. The College asks questions and pays for answers. The Purgation offers absolution and then a spear. The Second Table invites the most successful characters to dinner. The Reckoners notice whoever has a high Cut and has become inconvenient. Let each power want something from the characters before the characters want anything from it, and let at least one of them be wrong about who the characters are.
Each power's secret is a campaign by itself. A party that learns the Company's bone is people, the College's box is alive, the Purgation feeds the Second Table, the Second Table has no Head, or the Reckoners kill their own, holds a knife to an institution that every land depends on. None of the five will let them keep it lightly. Decide early which of the secrets your campaign is about, and let the players stumble on the edge of a second one just as they think they understand the first.
[/box]
