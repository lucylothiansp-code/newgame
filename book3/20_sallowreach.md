# Sallowreach: Where Nothing Ends

[sigil: sallowreach]

> Nobody here is waiting to die. We are all waiting to be allowed.
>> — fen proverb

> Shut the door, love. Shut the door. Something might finish.
>> — Hand-lines nursery rhyme, first line (there is no last line)

> Brown is still hers. Black is the god's. Grey is the guild's business, and white is nobody's, and you burn it.
>> — Seamers' Guild teaching-rhyme, recited by Threaders over the tweezers

[fiction]
*From the day-book of Aubin Coyle, apprentice of the Seamers' Guild, Needle Street, Lastgate. Restitching Week, Grace, 640 A.G.*
They send the youngest of us to the grandmothers first, because the grandmothers are the worst and the guild says you should learn the worst while your stomach still thinks it can be taught.
Mistress Harl took me to the Coombe house on Weir Row at first bell. The family had already carried Great-Gran Coombe down from the attic on her board and laid her across the kitchen table, under the hook where the eels hang. They had put a sprig of rosemary in each of her hands and a cloth over her face, which was kind of them and no use at all. You smell a Kept grandmother in spring before you are through the door. It is not one smell. Mistress Harl says to learn it in layers, like a cellar. On top is the family's vinegar, and under that the old cedar oil from last year's lacquer gone rancid, and under that the sweet, and under the sweet the thing itself, which is like a pot of lentils left a fortnight in the sun with a dead rat in it, and the rat is you.
"Good morning, Gran," said Mistress Harl. "May you finish."
The cloth moved. Under it something said, wetly, "Long life to you too, you lacquered bitch." The family laughed the way people laugh at a christening. Great-Gran Coombe died in 431. She has been rude to the guild every spring since.
We took the cloth off. Her face had slipped again. That is what winter does to them, even here where there is no winter: the damp gets into the lacquer and the old stitches go soft as wet bread and everything settles downward. The left cheek had come away from the bone along last year's seam and hung like a pocket. Mistress Harl lifted it with two fingers and I saw what was in the pocket. They were grey and they were moving and some of them had already got into the eye. She said, "Tweezers, Aubin, and the vinegar, and do not you dare be sick on a client."
I did not. I want that written down. I was sick afterward, in the eel barrel, and Mr. Coombe said not to worry, the eels would not mind, they are Kept too.
You pick them out one at a time. You cannot rush it, because if you squeeze they burst, and what is inside them is her. Gran swore at me the whole while in a voice that came partly from her mouth and partly from a split under her jaw where the old wire had pulled through. When I got to the eye she stopped swearing and said, very quietly, "Mind the eye, boy. I still use it." Then she held my wrist with her good hand. It was cold and soft and the skin moved over the bones like a glove a size too big. Under it I could feel her pulse, which she does not have. That is the thing nobody tells you. Under the skin of the Kept it is always moving. It is just not them.
We cut out the black and kept the brown. Mistress Harl says brown is still hers. We packed the cheek with lint soaked in cedar-and-pitch, drew it back up to the cheekbone, and I put in my first real seam: thirty-one stitches of waxed gut, blanket stitch, with wire at the corners. My hands shook. Gran said, "That is the worst stitching I have had in two hundred years," and then, "Thank you," and then, "Will you come next year? I should like it to be you." I said I would.
Then we lacquered her. Three coats, cedar and resin, brushed thin, each one dried with the bellows. By noon she shone like a sideboard and smelled like a church and the family sat her up in her chair by the window, which is where she likes to be, so she can watch the weep come down on the fen and tell the neighbors what she thinks of them.
On the way out I asked Mistress Harl how long it would hold. She said, "Till Plenty. Then the flies come and it starts again." I asked her how long that goes on. She looked at me like I had asked how long the fen goes on. She lit her pipe with a candle Wick Tallow made, and the candle flinched, and she said:
"Write it down, Aubin. That is what the book is for. Forever is a long time to remember by hand."
[/fiction]

Sallowreach is the land where nothing ends. A cut throat does not kill. A drowned girl walks out of the fen in the morning, coughing weed. A hanged man asks to be cut down. The grandmothers are restitched each spring. The fires that were lit in the founding years are still smoldering under the peat. The songs have no last verse and the arguments no final word. For six hundred and forty-one years the people of the grey north have lived inside the stomach of the god of death, and the god of death has been unable to do its job.

Now it is learning again.

This chapter describes Sallowreach in the Long Grace: its meal, its rule, its rot, its powers and its people. It gives the Game Master everything needed to set a campaign in the fens, and gives players what they need to play a Sallowreacher, living or Kept.

[pull] We are the only people on the Table who know exactly what our grandparents smell like on the inside. | — Lastgate saying, told to foreigners at the Landing to watch their faces

A word on the smell, before anything else, because a visitor meets it before anything else. It begins on the border road, nine miles out, as a sweetness in the fog that a southerner mistakes for flowers. By the Finishing Post it has become a weight on the tongue, like a coin held too long in the mouth. By the Landing it is everywhere, in the tar and the vinegar and the cedar smoke, and in the folds of every coat, and in the bread. The Sallowreacher does not smell it. The Sallowreacher smells *through* it, the way a fisherman sees through water, to the smaller smells beneath: whose grandmother needs restitching, which house has a seam gone in the attic, whether the Kept man on the next bench was lacquered this month or last. Children in Lastgate can tell, with their eyes closed, how many years a person has been dead. It is a parlour game. They are usually right to within a decade.

## The Meal

The people of the northern fens ate Ossel, the Closing Hand, last and slowest of all the peoples of the Table, because nobody wanted to be the one to start.

In the old carvings Ossel is shown seated, robed in what may be fog, with two immense hands laid open on its knees. It was not cruel and it was not kind. It walked the fens in the evening the way a lamplighter walks a street, and where it passed, things finished. The old heron that could no longer stand closed its eyes. The sick child's breathing eased and stopped. The fire that had burned all night settled into ash and was done. The fen-people loved it the way you love a doctor who has never once lied to you. They brought it their dying, and it came, and it put its hand over their eyes, and it was over. *May you finish* was a blessing then, said at bedsides, and it came true.

In the ninth winter of the Long Lack the fen-folk were eating reed-root and boiled leather and the soft white grubs from under the peat. Ossel came to them across the water at Lastgate, which was then a landing called the Ferrying, and lay down on the black mud at the edge of the mere with its hands folded on its chest, and waited. It waited eleven days.

They knew what had happened elsewhere. Word had come along the old roads of what the southern plains had done to their god, and the western coast to theirs. Every family in the Ferrying had someone in the house too weak to rise. And still nobody went down to the shore with a knife, because everyone in the fens understood a thing the other peoples had not had to think about: if you kill death, who closes your eyes?

### The Fourth Day: Ysmay Brack, the Last Closed

The Court's histories pass over the first ten days of the waiting in a sentence. The Loft-Wardens do not, because the shelved of Hall One remember them, and some of the shelved of Hall One were there.

On the fourth day the elders of the Ferrying met in the eel-house, which was the only roofed building on the landing, and argued in the dark with the god lying on the mud outside the door. The headman was a weir-owner named Orrin Brack. His argument, which the whisper-books record nearly word for word from three separate shelves, was this: that Ossel had never once come to the fen without taking something, that it had come every evening of every year to close what was ready, and that a god lying on the shore with its hands open was a god *waiting to be given*. It was not asking to be eaten. It was asking for its due. Give it one good life, whole and young and unready, said Orrin Brack, and it would rise and go back across the water, and the famine would end, the way famines had always ended, by someone paying.

The elders agreed. The fen-folk had paid Ossel in the dying for a thousand years, and they understood debt. What they argued over until dawn was only whose.

It was Brack's own daughter, Ysmay, twenty years old, the strongest rower on the landing, who had kept the Brack family alive that winter by poling out to the far weirs alone. The accounts differ on whether she offered or was chosen. The shelved of Hall One are clear that her father asked her at the eel-house door, in front of everyone, and that in the fens a father's asking and a father's ordering were the same sentence said in two voices. She washed in the mere. She put on her mother's marriage-shawl. She walked down the mud between the families with her hair wet and her feet bare, and the landing said nothing, because there was nothing to say that did not make it worse.

She lay down in the god's left hand. The palm was the size of a punt's floor and as cold as the underside of ice. The fingers, very slowly, closed over her, as they had closed over every dying thing in the fen for a thousand years, gently, the way you fold a letter. There was no sound from inside. After a time the fingers opened again, and she was there, still and pale and finished, her eyes shut and her mouth a little open, as if about to say something polite.

The god did not rise. It did not stir. It lay on the mud with its hands open and its eyes on the fog, and the famine went on, and the next morning two more children on the landing did not wake.

That is the whole of the story, and it is the one the fen does not tell its children. Ysmay Brack was the last person in Sallowreach to die and stay dead. Everyone who has died since has come back. She is the only one who got what the god had always given, and she got it for nothing: for a guess, made in the dark by frightened men, about what a god might want. Seven days later Hessa Grue walked down with her reaping hook and said that the god was lying there so that they would, and nobody argued, because everyone on the landing had seen what the other answer had bought.

The Court's genealogies say Ysmay was buried in the old way, under a mound behind the eel-house, the last grave in the north. The Cutwrights' College has opened the Brack Mound twice, in 388 and in 590, and found it empty to the clay. In the ninth winter of the Long Lack, the shelved of Hall One say when the Readers press them, nothing on the landing was wasted. Then they stop whispering for a while.

[pull] She was the last of us to get it, and she got it for a guess. | — Sister Lorn of the Shut Eye, preaching at the Dunmere Verge, 640 A.G.

The Finishers have taken her up. They call her Saint Ysmay of the Open Hand and the First Walker, and they carry her name first on every pilgrim's strip. The Confessors of the Theft consider her the original sin of the north, worse than the Meal: not the eating of a god, which hunger excused, but the paying of a god that had not asked, which nothing excuses. The Gift preaches that the god closed her out of love, to spare her the centuries to come. Nobody in the Brack family, which survives as a line of eel-catchers on Weir Row, will speak her name. Their Kept elders turn their faces to the wall when it is said.

On the eleventh night a widow named Hessa Grue (the Court's genealogies insist on the name; the Cutwrights' College does not) walked down to the mud with a reaping hook and her six living children and said, loudly enough for the landing to hear, "It is lying there so that we will." Then she began at the left hand.

The accounts agree on a few things and on nothing else. They agree that Ossel's flesh was cold, colder than the mud, cold as a cellar step, and that it did not bleed but leaked a grey, slow, tasteless fluid like the water that collects under a stone. They agree that it had no smell at all. A body three hundred feet long, opened on the shore, and not a breath of rot or meat or blood; the gulls would not come down to it, and the flies, for the first time in anyone's life, stayed away. That absence of smell is what the oldest songs remember, more than the blood or the cold. *It smelled of nothing, and nothing was what we ate.*

They agree that it took nine days to eat. Families came down in turn and carved, and carried, and boiled, and the meat would not soften in the pot however long it boiled, so it was eaten in grey strips that had to be chewed for an hour. It tasted, said the ones who wrote of it, like the moment after a bell stops ringing. People who ate it fell asleep where they sat and woke the next morning unable to remember the night. Some woke weeping. Some woke laughing and could not say why.

The families who took the hands became the Hand-lines. Hessa Grue's children ate the left hand to the wrist; the Vanes, the Sedges and the Hethes divided the right. The Hands were the choicest cut because they were what the god *did* things with, and because they were the hardest part to eat: the fingers kept curling closed over the knives. It is said that the eldest Vane boy put his own hand into Ossel's palm, in the dark, to steady the carving, and the god's fingers closed over his, gently, and held him there until dawn. He was not hurt. He would not speak of it. His line is the strongest in Sallowreach.

Last of all they came to the face. Nobody wanted it. In the end the Scraplings of the far fen took it, as they took everything nobody else would, and boiled it in a peat pit for a week. Those who saw it before the knives went in all said the same thing, and Grandfather Peat (see the People of Sallowreach), who saw it the week he drowned, still says it in a dialect no one else speaks: the god was smiling.

The famine ended the next morning. The reeds came up green. Eels filled the traps. And in Lastgate, a thief who had been hanged at the landing the week before, and left as a warning, opened his eyes, saw the crowd gathered on the shore, and asked politely to be cut down.

The fen remembers the nine days by what the body does not remember. The carvers' hands went numb to the elbow in the first hour and stayed numb for a month; Hessa Grue's eldest, it is said, never felt anything in her fingers again, and could hold a coal. The knives frosted. The mere around the god did not ripple even when the wind blew, as if the water had stopped trying. The grey fluid that leaked from the cuts collected in the hoof-prints and footprints on the mud and did not soak in, and children who put their fingers in it said it was like touching the inside of a closed eye. At night the families slept on the landing beside the carcass, because no one wanted to walk home alone, and in the morning they found that they had all dreamed the same dream, of a long table with someone standing at the end of it, waiting to be asked to sit, and none of them could remember who.

## The Rule of the Land

Nothing ends here.

A body in Sallowreach takes the damage and goes on. A throat slit to the spine bleeds out, the heart stops, the blood stops, and the man sits up, his head lolling forward on a hinge of skin, and asks for help holding it on. A woman drowned in the mere is down for a night and comes up the next day, puffed and grey and furious, with an eel in her coat. A man burned in a barn fire walks out of the ashes as a figure of black coal that crackles when it moves and weeps clear fluid from its cracks. A starving child grows hungry and thin and then too thin and then nothing changes; she goes on, and goes on being hungry. And everyone grows old. The very old go past old into something else: shrunken, leathered, curled like a dropped glove, eyes gone to milk, whispering. They are still in there.

Those who have died and carried on are called **the Kept**. They outnumber the breathing nine to one. They have no pulse and no breath. They do not need to eat, drink or sleep, though many still try out of habit, and some still feel a ghost of hunger they cannot feed. Their bodies are cool, the temperature of the room. They feel everything.

That is the cruelty: decay still works. A Kept body rots exactly as a dead body rots anywhere else on the Table: it bloats, it slips, it blackens, it is hollowed by flies and their children. But it never finishes rotting. There is no clean bone at the end, no dust, no rest. The rot goes on, slower and slower, layer under layer, for centuries, and the mind sits inside it and feels every soft inch go. The Kept do not lose their wits to death. They lose them, if they lose them, to time.

### The Long Rot

Every Seamer learns the long rot as a calendar, because it keeps time better than any clock in the north. It is the same body after body, the same stages in the same order, only slower: what takes a fortnight in a southern ditch takes a lifetime in a Lastgate attic, and what takes a year in the south never arrives here at all.

| Time Kept | What the Guild calls it | What it looks, smells and feels like |
| First week | Fresh | Waxen, cool, the blood settled purple in the lowest skin. Smells of nothing worse than sweat. The Kept say this is the worst week, because they can still remember being warm. |
| First season | Bloat | The belly swells and strains the waistband; the face goes round and green-marbled; the tongue fills the mouth. Gas moves through the body with a sound like a cat purring. Smells of drains and sweet milk. |
| First years | the Slip | Skin loosens and slides over the flesh like a glove a size too big; it comes away in sheets on the sheets. Hair lifts out in combs. Fingernails drop. Smells of wet cellar and bad meat. Flies come in earnest. |
| First decades | the Settling | Everything goes downward. Cheeks hang, eyelids hang, the belly empties into the thighs. The soft parts liquefy and are held in only by wrapping, lacquer and the Seamers. The smell is at its height: "the brown." |
| First century | the Leathering | What is left dries toward hide and tendon, brown and black, curled like a dropped glove. The voice goes papery. The smell thins and sharpens to cheese, vinegar and old shoes. |
| Centuries | the Long Grey | A rack of bone and black string with a lacquered face, kept in shape by wire, still drying, still never dry. Under the skin something always moves. The Kept at this stage say the itch is the only thing that has not faded. |

There is no last row. A body at the Long Grey goes on being a body at the Long Grey, and the colony that lives in it goes on breeding, and it goes on feeling the colony. The Guild's oldest teaching-ledger has a column headed *Dust*, ruled in 131 A.G. in the expectation that someone would one day need it. It is empty.

The Kept feel all of it, which is the part foreigners refuse to believe until they have sat with a Kept grandmother through a spring afternoon and watched her stop mid-sentence to press a hand flat against her side, very hard, the way a living woman presses a stitch, and wait for something under the skin to finish turning over. The bloat aches like a stomach after a feast that never digests. The slip burns, the way skin burns when a plaster is pulled. The settling is a constant, dragging, downward pull, as if the body were full of wet sand and someone were tugging at the bottom of the sack. And the maggots are felt one by one, every one, for as long as they live, and then their children are felt.

[pull] Gran says it doesn't hurt any more. Gran says it the way you say the kettle's not on, when you can hear it. | — a Weir Row child, aged ten, to a Reader at the Lofts

It applies to everything that ever lived, and to much that did not.

- **Animals.** A slaughtered pig gets up off the block. Butchery in Sallowreach is the art of rendering an animal too disjointed to wander off. Eels go on twisting in the pot and, it is said, in the stomach. The rich eat imported Fatlands meat or *stilled* meat, closed by a licensed Hand-line butcher at great expense. The poor eat *lively*, and learn to chew fast.
- **Plants.** Cut reeds go on drawing water. Picked fruit rots on the shelf and never goes to dust. Leaves yellow on the branch and stay yellow for decades, waiting for an autumn that never finishes arriving.
- **Fire.** A fire in Sallowreach burns down but does not go out. Peat fires lit in the Glad Century still smolder under the fen; the ground steams in long seams across the north. To put out a candle you must close it, and only the Hand-lines can close.
- **Sound and speech.** Songs here have no last verse; they trail off and wait. Books have no last pages. An argument begun in 212 A.G. over a fishing weir is still being conducted, by the original parties, in a room in Lastgate, through a hired interpreter of jaw-clicks.
- **The border.** The dead cannot leave. A Kept person who steps across the border of Sallowreach drops, finished, at once, the whole long rot catching up in a single heartbeat. What hits the ground on the far side is bone and dust and a smell like an opened tomb. Sallowreach therefore has an army that cannot be killed and cannot invade.
- **Wounds.** A cut on the living closes in Sallowreach as it would anywhere, slowly and badly; a cut on the Kept never closes at all. It stays open, raw, wet at the lips, a mouth in the body, for as long as the body lasts. Old soldiers of the Fen Guard carry wounds from the Reckoners' raid of 402 that are still pink inside, and the Seamers' first lesson is that a seam is not a healing but a lid.
- **Pain.** It does not fade with the injury, because the injury does not end. A woman whose hip was broken in a fall in 470 has been breaking her hip, in a sense, every day since. The Kept learn to live in pain the way the living learn to live in weather.
- **Sleep.** The Kept do not need it, but many fall into a slow stupor they call *going under*, which is like sleep with the eyes open and the mind awake at the bottom of it, listening. The living call it *keeping quiet*. It is in this state that most Kept are robbed of parts.

[box: The Rule at the Table]
In Sallowreach, a character reduced to 0 Flesh suffers Ruin as normal: an Injury from the Mangling table and collapse. Further damage does not kill. A living character who would die instead becomes Kept (see Playing One of the Kept). A Kept character who suffers further damage accrues Rot instead. Enemies are the same: the GM should remember that a fight in Sallowreach ends when one side cannot move or chooses to stop, never when someone dies. Hamstringing, dismembering, sacking and sinking are the local tactics. Witnessing a Kept man coming apart is a Dread 2 horror; a Kept body that goes on talking while it is being taken to pieces is Dread 3.
[/box]

There is one exception, and it is the whole of Sallowreach's politics. The Hand-lines, who ate the god's hands, can close. Their touch, in the later stages of their Regrowth, does what Ossel once did: it finishes a thing. A candle. A conversation. A person. Since 301 A.G. that touch has been sold by license, and it is the only death in the land.

## The Regrowth in Sallowreach

Ossel is growing back, and what grows back is ending. Every other land's god returns as a presence, an appetite, a pressure. Sallowreach's returns as an absence. Its godsign is closing, and its Seating is silence.

### The Taste

It begins as a dread of open things. A Hand-line child cannot sleep with the door ajar. She gets up three times a night to push it to, and if it is pushed open again she cries until it is shut. Open jars bother her. Open mouths bother her. A book left face-down makes her feel sick in a place she cannot point to. Adults at the Taste find themselves closing drawers in other people's houses, smoothing shut the flaps of envelopes, lowering the lids of sleeping friends with a fingertip. The fingers go cold first, a chill like holding a stone that has lain all night in the fen. Candles near them gutter. Conversations near them, for no reason anyone can name, tend to stop.

Little Master Bevis is at the Taste, they say, the way you'd say a child has his first tooth, and Lady Hethe had cake. He is six. I sit up with him nights. He cannot bear the shutters open even a finger's width and screams for me if the wind moves one, and he has started on my eyes. I wake and his little hand is there, pressing my lids down, ever so gentle, cold as a spoon from the well. He says, "Go to sleep properly, Nurse." He says I sleep wrong, with my eyes all wet underneath. The other nurse, Dorcas, will not take his room any more. She says when he is in it you cannot hear the canal. I said that was nonsense. Then I sat in it and listened, Ide, and you can't.
[/fiction]

### The Appetite

Then the closing becomes a compulsion. Lids, shutters, books, boxes, eyes. Appetite sufferers cannot pass an open door without shutting it. They close the eyes of the dead, and then the eyes of the living: a stranger in a tavern finds a cold hand laid over his face, gently, and the person it belongs to apologizing and unable to stop. They finish other people's sentences, and then they finish other people's songs, and the song simply ends there, and the singer cannot remember the next verse. The fingertips grey. The nails thicken and darken like horn. The hands are always cold now, cold enough to sting, and sufferers wear mittens in polite company, partly from courtesy and partly so that they will have to take something off before they can touch. It is said in Lastgate that you can always tell a Hand-line house at night, because every window is shuttered and every door is shut, and the silence inside is the kind you could lean on.

### The Course

The hands change. They go grey all the way to the wrist and then past it, a cold slate grey like the belly of a fish, and the skin over them grows smooth and seamless and faintly shining. They lengthen. The fingers grow extra joints, first one more, then two, then more, so that a hand at the height of the Course can curl like a fern or fold flat like a fan. The joints click in silence. The nails go black. The cold is no longer a sensation but a fact: a hand at the Course will frost a glass of water and leave a pale, bloodless print on skin.

And what they touch finishes. A candle pinched by a Course-hand is not snuffed but *closed*: the wick goes dark, the wax goes cold, and it will never be lit again. A conversation touched, a palm laid on a speaker's shoulder, ends, and nobody present can quite recall what it was about. A Kept body touched with intent goes still. The long rot catches up at once, every unfinished year of it, and what was a talking corpse is in a moment only a corpse, quiet, smelling at last of nothing worse than earth. The Kept who see it weep with envy. The living who see it are often sick.

People at the Course keep their hands in gloves, then in mittens, then in lead. The highest of the Hand-lines have their gloves sewn on.

There is a smell to a Hand-line at the Course, and it is the absence of one. The skin of the hands gives off nothing at all: not sweat, not soap, not the faint salt of any living palm. People who have stood near them describe the air around the hands as tasting *rinsed*, the way the air tastes after a heavy snow in the south, though no one in the north has seen snow. Flies avoid the hands, then the arms, then the person. In a land where every room buzzes, the Course-stricken move in a little bubble of stillness, and the Kept follow them through the streets at a distance, the way starving dogs follow a butcher's cart.

[pull] Every Kept man in Lastgate knows which side of the street the Vanes walk on. Half of them cross to it. | — Ysolde Crane, Cedar-Warden of Hand Street

### The Brink

At the Brink the ending begins to leak out of the hands and into the room. Sound grows thin around the sufferer. Footsteps do not quite carry. Flies within a few paces drop out of the air and do not get up. Flames lean away. The Kept, near a Brink-sufferer, fall silent and then begin to plead. The sufferer's face grows smooth and grey like the hands, the features softening as if seen through fog, and in some of them the lips curve gently upward, in what everyone agrees not to call a smile. They stop needing to touch. A Hand-line at the Brink can finish a thing by looking at it long enough and wishing it over. Most of them have stopped wishing anything at all.

### The Seating

The Seating in Sallowreach is a **Hush**: a patch of ground where everything ends at once and stays ended. A Hand-line who reaches it does not die. They become the center of a silence, and the silence spreads. Inside a Hush there is no rot, no flies, no echo. The Kept inside it are properly, finally dead, and lie down where they stood. The grass is grey and does not move. Sound does not carry: a scream inside a Hush is a mouth opening and nothing. Birds fall at its edge. The largest, Dunmere Hush, is four miles across and growing.

The Court has known for some years, and has begun to admit in locked rooms, that Hushes appear fastest near the estates of the Hand-lines. The god is not coming back as a figure walking the fen in the evening. It is coming back as a book, closing.

To stand at the edge of a Hush is to feel the inside of your own ears. The fen beside you buzzes and drips and knocks and breathes; a step ahead, there is nothing, and the nothing presses on the eardrum like deep water. Visitors report a taste of iron. They report that their own heartbeats become enormous, and then, if they lean over the line, that their heartbeats seem to come from the wrong side of it, from somewhere out in the grey, steady and patient, like someone walking toward them who never arrives. The reeds at the line are cut clean in colour as if with a knife: green-brown on one side, the grey of cold ash on the other, and the grey does not move in the wind. A dropped stone lands without a sound. A lamp held over the line burns on, but its light looks painted.

[box: Running the Regrowth]
Sallowreach's horror works because it inverts the rest of the Table. Everywhere else, the Regrowth is the thing the players fear. Here, half the population is praying for it. A Hand-line PC at the Course will be approached constantly: by grandmothers asking to be closed, by Heirs offering fortunes to have a father closed, by Finishers asking to be blessed, by Court agents with a license, a fee and a warrant. Every touch is a moral decision and most of them are illegal. Make the players feel what it is to carry the only mercy in a country and to be told, by law, who may have it.
[/box]

## Land and Weather

Sallowreach is grey fen, black water, reed and peat, under a fog called **the Breath** that has not lifted in living or unliving memory. The Breath is warm and wet and sweet in the wrong way, like air let out of a closed room. It beads on wool and hair. It carries sound strangely: a voice across the mere seems to come from just behind you. At noon it is the color of dishwater; at dusk it goes yellow, then brown.

There is no real winter, because winter is an ending. The seasons are named as they are everywhere on the Table, but in the fens they only lean: Grace is damper, Plenty is hotter and buzzes, Carving yellows the leaves, and Lack makes the fog a little colder and leaves the leaves where they are. Trees in Sallowreach carry decades of dead foliage, yellow and brown and black in layers, rattling. A Sallowreacher who travels south and sees a bare tree in Lack weeps, or laughs, or both.

**Flies are the true weather.** They move across the fen in fronts, dense brown curtains a mile wide that tick against shutters like sleet. Lastgate's almanacs forecast them. A **Black Noon** is a swarm thick enough to put out lamps and turn day to dusk; people caught outdoors in one breathe through cloth and come home with flies in their ears, their collars, the corners of their eyes. Every house has fly-nets, fly-paper, smudge pots of smoldering peat and lavender. In the hot weeks of Plenty, when the swarms breed, the Kept are shut indoors for their own sakes, and the Seamers work without sleeping.

[fiction]
*From* Pocock's Lastgate Almanack and Fly-Reckoner *for the Year 641, Plenty, the Weeks of the Swarm. Sold at the Landing, 2 crumbs.*

Rain is called **weep**. It falls warm and leaves a tea-colored stain on linen and skin. Mist that settles overnight is called **dew-rot**. The wind, when there is wind, is called **a sigh**, and a long dry sigh off the southern road is the one weather everyone loves: it clears the Breath for an hour and you can see the sky, which is white.

The fen itself is full. Everything that ever drowned in it is still down there, moving a little: deer, dogs, horses, eels by the million, fishermen, suicides from the Glad Century, children who slipped off the boardwalks, six centuries of the weighted sacks of the Sump. On still days the black water stirs without wind. Reed-cutters wear tall boots and keep their eyes on the surface, because sometimes something takes hold of an ankle, not to drown you, only to hold on.

Nothing that falls into the fen finishes sinking, and nothing that floats ever quite rots away, and so the surface of the black water is a slow museum. Reed-cutters learn not to look too long at what drifts past the punt. The Kept who go under for their families to tend the traps learn not to look at anything at all.

[pull] Don't pull it in, whatever it is. If it wanted to come in it would have climbed. | — punt-man's rule, Lastgate south channels

## A Gazetteer of Sallowreach

### Lastgate

The capital, built on pilings over the fen where the border road ends. Lastgate is a city of tar, timber and ropes, of plank streets and plank bridges, of houses leaning together over narrow canals like old men sharing a secret. It has sunk for centuries. Each generation builds a new storey on top of the old, and the lower levels have gone down into the water one by one and are still inhabited: the **Drowned Storeys**, half-flooded streets where the light is green and the residents are Kept and long past minding the wet. Little Nell Ash rules there.

Lastgate smells of tar, vinegar, cedar, smoke and the thing all of those are there to hide. Rich houses on the Upper Boards burn cedar in braziers at their doors. Poor streets in the Sinks smell of themselves. Everywhere there are candles, and many of them are Wick Tallow's.

Notable districts: **the Upper Boards** (Court houses, the Mortuary Court's Hall of Hands); **Needle Street** (the Seamers' Guildhall and a hundred stitching-shops, wire-drawers and lacquer-boilers); **the Sinks** (the poor, the Heirs' safehouses, Marrow Jack's markets); **the Landing** (the Ferrying Post, the border road terminus, Tam Ruddock's ferry); and **the Drowned Storeys** beneath it all.

Lastgate is counted in storeys rather than streets. The planks a visitor walks on are the Ninth Storey; the cellars beneath are the Eighth, rented by the bed to the living poor; the Seventh and everything under it are the Drowned Storeys. The flat iron bell of the Ferrying Post, cast in the year 9 and never finished cooling, rings the nine hours of the day, and at sixth bell every household lights its door-candle by law, so that the whole city glows yellow in the Breath like something seen through the skin of a lantern.

[pull] Ninth, Weir Row, the Coombe house, is where you'll find Gran. Sixth, Old Weir Row, the Coombe house, is where you'll find her mother. Don't go down there without a lamp, and don't let her keep you to supper. | — directions given to a Seamer's Threader, Needle Street

### The Lofts

Warehouses of the Set-Aside, on the north edge of Lastgate: nine storeys of shelving in nine long timber halls, each shelf holding the wrapped, labeled bodies of those too far gone to stand. Forty thousand of them, still aware. Ladders on rails run the length of each aisle, and the Loft-Wardens move along them with lamps and lists. The halls are lit low and kept cool and smell of camphor, linen, wax and old meat. The whispering is audible from the street: a soft continuous sound, like rain on reeds, rising at night.

Visitors remember the Lofts for the whispering and Wardens remember them for the drip. The shelved are wrapped tight in waxed linen from the feet to the crown, and the linen holds most of what the long rot makes, but not all: through nine storeys of slatted shelving, what seeps from the upper shelves finds its way down, a slow, brown, sweetish rain the Wardens call *Loft-weather*. The lower shelves are tarred against it and the aisles are strewn with sawdust that is swept and burned each week. In the heat of Plenty the drip quickens, and the whole of a hall can be heard ticking, faintly, like a clock shop. The Wardens rewrap the oldest shelved every decade, unwinding them on the long tables in the Wrapping Room, and the families are not invited to that, because what is under the linen after a hundred years is not what they remember, and it is still talking.

The labels are the saddest thing. Each shelved body carries a card tied at the throat with its name, its dates, its family and the date of its Setting Aside, and below that a line for *Visited*, where the Wardens mark each feast day that someone came. On the oldest shelves the cards have been rewritten so often that the Wardens have given up on dates and simply ruled the *Visited* line through.

### The Sump

The deep fen west of Lastgate, where those beyond stitching are sunk in weighted sacks. It is the nearest thing Sallowreach has to a graveyard and the worst place in the north. The water there is black and greasy and smells of drains. **Bubbles rise in patterns**: rings, lines, spirals, sometimes a slow, regular pulse like breathing. Sump-watchers claim the patterns are speech. Sackmen ferry new sacks out at dusk and lower them on ropes, reciting the names. Dredgers, licensed and otherwise, rake the bottom for anything that can be sold: rings, wire, teeth, and, in the last ten years, whole bodies, for reasons the Seamers' Guild does not want to know.

The Sackmen are a hereditary trade, living and Kept, and they keep a strict and joyless decency. Each sack is sewn shut by the family, not by the Sackman. Each carries a name-strip of lead stamped with the name and the date of sinking, so that what is down there may be found again if anyone ever needs to find it. At the drop the Sackman stands in the stern, holds the rope in both hands, and recites the name, the dates and the words *Go under and keep*, and lets the rope pay out until it goes slack, which in the deep Sump can take a long time. Nobody else speaks. What is at the bottom, by the account of the Kept who walk the fen-bed and have gone near it, is a mound: six centuries of sacks heaped one on another, softened together, the ropes rotted into one mat, and the whole of it, very slowly, rising and falling.

### Dunmere Hush

The first and largest silence. Eight years ago Dunmere was a reed-cutting village of three hundred souls, living and Kept. Now it is the center of a grey disc four miles across that grows a few yards every month. At its edge the reeds go from green-brown to ash-grey in the space of a step. Birds that cross the line fall and lie still. Inside, the village stands as it stood: doors open, washing on the lines, the inhabitants lying where they lay down, unrotted, unmoving, entirely dead. There are no flies. There is no smell. There is no sound at all. Finishers camp at the edge in their hundreds.

### The Jar Room

The Court's prison, under the Hall of Hands. One shelf, very long, running the full length of a low brick vault, lined with jars of thick green glass sealed with lead. In each jar is a criminal sentenced to be Kept forever in pieces: head in one, hands in another, the rest rendered or sunk. The heads are aware. The jars are labeled with the name, the crime, and the date of sentence. The oldest is from 304 A.G. On the floor beneath the shelf there are drains.

The jars sweat. The lead seals are tight but the glass is old, and over the centuries every jar has grown a skin of green moisture on the outside, as if the vault were breathing on it. The heads inside float in a thick amber pickle of the Court's own recipe, and their hair drifts upward and turns slowly, like weed in a still pool. The hands are jarred separately, a pair to a jar, on the shelf below, and the hands are the busy ones: they open, close, drum, scratch at the glass with what remains of their nails, and on quiet nights the whole low shelf ticks and taps like a roomful of people waiting to be called. Those who have been jarred for treason are shelved facing the wall. Those jarred for unlicensed Closing have a second label, in red, which reads *Not to be closed under any license*, and those are the ones who weep.

### The Hall of Closings

On the Upper Boards, a long, plain, cedar-paneled building with no windows, where licensed Closings are performed. It has a waiting room, a robing room, a ledger room and nine small chambers, each with a narrow bed and a single chair. The building is famously quiet. Buyers wait here in their best clothes for days. Some are turned away at the door because a fee has risen overnight.

### Glovehall

The estate of the Vanes, an hour's punt north of Lastgate: a great house of black timber on a mound in the fen, every window bricked to a slit, every door double. Lady Corrow Vane lives in its east wing. Osric Vane is kept in its west. A new Hush, small, the size of a kitchen garden, appeared in the orchard behind Glovehall last Carving. The gardeners will not go near it. The pears in it are perfect.

### Brinemoss and Mother Gall's Cellar

A hamlet of stilt huts in the south fen, known for pickling. Under the largest hut, dug into the peat and lined with cedar, is Mother Gall's cellar: four hundred oak barrels of brine, each holding a paying customer, each wide awake. The barrels creak and knock gently. When the lids are lifted the faces in the brine are smooth, pale and perfect, and the eyes open.

### The Finishing Post

Where the border road leaves Sallowreach, nine miles south of Lastgate, a line of tarred stakes marks the boundary. Beyond it, in the grass, there is a long low heap that the Rim Road Company clears twice a year. It is bone and dust and rags: the Kept who crossed. Some were desperate. Some were confused. Some went deliberately, at last, and the Company clerks who rake the heap report that those ones are always found facing home. Kept relatives come to the post on feast days and stand just inside it, looking south.

### The Room of the Long Argument

A rented upper room in the Sinks where, since 212 A.G., two weir-owners named Abel Strake and Cobb Mallory have been disputing the ownership of a fishing weir that rotted away in 260. Neither has a tongue any longer. They argue through the jaw-clicks interpreted by Fenwick Lisp. A crowd sometimes gathers. People bet on the outcome. There is never an outcome.

### The Smoking Ground

A stretch of open fen east of Lastgate where peat fires lit in the Glad Century still burn underground. The ground is warm and spongy and steams from long cracks. Things lost in the fires walk there, black and cracking. The Heirs use it for meetings because no Court agent will go there in good boots.

### Coldharrow

A reed-cutters' village of stilt-houses a day's punt south-east of Dunmere, on the pilgrim channel, where the Finisher processions stop to rest before the last stretch to the Verge. Until 637 it was known for the best thatch-reed in the south fen and a choir. Now it is known for the Coldharrow Carving (see the Finishers, below). Of its two hundred and thirty souls in 636, a hundred and seventy remain. The houses have bells on every door. The living sleep in pairs, by custom, never alone, and the Finishers no longer stop there, at the village's request; their processions pole past in silence with their candles hidden under their cloaks. The villagers stand on the boardwalks and watch them go by, and some of them, everyone has noticed, hum.

### Grinholm

Three days north by punt and wading-way, a scatter of turf huts on an alder island where the Scraplings live: the families who took what nobody else wanted at the Meal, and above all the face. In the center of the island is a fenced peat pit where the face was boiled for a week in the year 0. Nothing grows in it. Its water is perfectly clear. The Scraplings of Grinholm marry each other, because no one else will marry them, and are proud, poor and suspicious of every visitor, especially Hand-lines and the Cutwrights' College surveyors who have come lately with chains and questions about a girl named Pim Sorrel. The Scraplings say the pit smiles on a still night. Visitors who have looked into it at the Plate's rising do not argue.

### The Brack Mound

A low hummock of grass behind the old eel-house at the Landing, fenced with rope, the only grave in Sallowreach and the first. Ysmay Brack is supposed to lie under it. Pilgrims of the Finishers leave grey ribbons on the rope. The Cutwrights have twice dug it and twice found nothing, and the Court has twice had it filled in again and returfed, because a country with one grave cannot admit that it has none.

[box: Traveling the Fen]
Sallowreach has one road worth the name, the border road from the Finishing Post to Lastgate, raised on a causeway of piled peat and old bones. Everything else is boardwalk, punt, or wading. A day's travel off the road is a Hard Wayfaring check; failure means a lost hour, a soaked pack and a Dread 1 encounter from the fen (see Horrors of Sallowreach). Living travelers need fly-cloth, vinegar and a hat. Kept travelers need a Seamer within a week.
[/box]

### The Glad Century and the Stitching Laws

For a hundred years it looked like a miracle. Mothers who would have died in childbed rose and nursed; fishermen swept off the weirs came home with weed in their hair. There were no widows and no orphans, and the reed-towns danced through the long fen evenings. The songs of that century are the only happy songs in Sallowreach, and nobody sings them now. The dancing stopped when the first generation began to come apart: a man who died in the year 3 was by the year 90 something that could not hold a fiddle. By 130 the state recognized what it was. The Stitching Laws made families responsible for keeping their dead in one piece, chartered the Seamers' Guild, and made it a fineable offence to fall apart in public. The fines are still collected: four lacks for a hand on the boardwalk, twenty for a face.

### The War of the Heirs and the Licensing

By 288 no property had changed hands by inheritance in two centuries, and the young rose. The Heirs won every battle, hacked their fathers apart in their own halls, and changed nothing: the fathers were back at their desks within the week, stitched, lacquered and furious, and sent their children to the jar. The Heirs' banner, a hand cut off at the wrist, still hangs in the Sinks. Thirteen years later, in 301, the Hand-lines announced what some of them had known for decades: their touch could finish a person. They did not give it away. The Court set a price and built the Hall of Closings, and made death scarce on purpose.

### The Hushes

In Carving of 633, a ferryman poling past Dunmere at dawn could not hear the village dogs, or his pole in the water, or himself shouting. Everyone in the village was simply, properly dead. When the news reached Lastgate, half the country wept with envy. Seventeen more Hushes have been found since, some no bigger than a room, every one within a day of a Hand-line estate.

### The Grey Plague

In the hot Plenty of 512 a Fatlands drover brought a fever up the border road. In the south it killed one in ten. In the south fen of Sallowreach it killed no one, and Kept a third of everyone it touched in a single month: they burned, they sweated, they died in their beds, and they sat up in the morning still burning, still sweating, with the fever going on inside them forever. Brinemoss and the stilt-hamlets around it are still full of the fever-Kept, flushed and shivering under their blankets in the heat, their skin hot to the touch a hundred and twenty-nine years later, their teeth chattering in the dark like a weir-trap knocking. They are the only Kept who are warm. Nobody wants to hold their hands either.

### The Restitution of 639

By the spring of 639 there were eleven Hushes and the Court had Provost Sedge's first maps, and the Leaden party, which wanted the Course-stricken walled in lead, found itself outvoted by a stranger notion. It came from the Confessors of the Theft, whose whole doctrine is that what the north lives in is a bill for a stolen god. If the Hushes were the god coming back for what was taken, said Confessor Ambrose Weal before the Court in Grace of that year, then what was taken might be given back. The hands had been stolen. Return a pair of hands.

The Court debated it for nine days, which is fast for the Court. It settled on Lord Pellam Fallow, of a lesser Hand-line, forty-four, at the Course, the son of the Lady Imber Fallow who had closed her father in 588; and Pellam, who had grown up in that house and knew what his mother's hands had done, said yes before he was asked twice. The Seamers' Guild was ordered to take the hands. Thessaly Mort refused to do it herself and sent her senior Master of the Seam, who did it in the Hall of Hands at dawn with a bone-saw, before the whole Court. The witnesses agreed on one detail more than any other: the saw blade went cold and grey where it touched the wrist, and the Master had to change blades three times, because each one the hands touched was finished, and would not cut again.

The hands lived. Everything in Sallowreach lives. Severed, they lay on the cedar tray and opened and closed, slowly, nine joints to a finger, and the clerk who carried the tray found his breath smoking and his ears full of silence. They were sealed in a lead box. A procession of the whole Court, with the Confessors singing the Hymn of the Open Hands, carried the box by punt to the edge of Dunmere Hush. At the grey line Lord Pellam, his stumps bound in lint and pitch, opened the box with his teeth, and the Master of the Seam tipped the hands out over the line.

They fell into the grey and lay palm upward in the ash-coloured reeds, about ten feet from the edge, where everyone could see them. They did not move again. The Confessors sang the seventh verse and sat through the eighth in silence, as is the custom, and the procession poled home.

The next month Dunmere Hush grew forty yards, which was twice its rate in any month before. That season two new Hushes opened, one at the Fallow estate at Wending Mound. The Leaden party has not raised the Restitution since and the Confessors do not preach it. Lord Pellam lives at Wending, handless, in a house where the orchard has gone grey. He does not leave his rooms. His stumps, his servants say, have not healed, because nothing heals on the Kept and in the end not much on the living either, and lately there is something at the end of each of them, small, grey and smooth, like the first buds on a branch, with tiny joints in it, that closes when it is touched.

The hands are still where they fell. On a clear evening you can see them from the Verge with a spyglass, open, among the grey reeds, as if waiting to be given something else.

## Rule, Law and Politics

Sallowreach is governed by **the Mortuary Court**, a council of the Hand-lines seated in the Hall of Hands, under a Regent, Ansel Grue, who has held the post since 341 A.G. The Court makes law, levies the fly-tax and the stitching-tithe, licenses every Closing, and judges every capital crime, of which there is only one penalty that matters.

**Property never passes.** Owners never die. The oldest Kept, called **the Unvacated**, hold every title, deed and office in the country. A house built in 150 A.G. belongs to the man who built it. A seat on a guild council is held by whoever first sat in it. A living man of seventy is legally a minor if his father is still upright, and cannot sign a lease, marry without consent, or vote in a guild. The living are tenants, clerks, apprentices and servants of the dead, and will be until they join them.

**Death is the only currency of power.** A Closing costs more than a house. The Court grants Closings as rewards (to loyal officers, to the parents of useful men), withholds them as punishment, and sells them by license to those who can pay. The Court's worst sentence is the opposite of death: to be **jarred**, Kept forever in pieces in the Jar Room. Lesser crimes are punished by fines, by flogging (it heals badly on the Kept and never closes), by *unstitching* (the removal of the Seamers' work, a public humiliation), and by **refusal**, the striking of a name from the Court's Closing ledger forever.

Courts in Sallowreach hear cases between parties who were wronged three hundred years ago and are still present to give evidence. Statutes of limitation do not exist. Murder, as a crime, is hard to define: the Stitching Laws class it as *grievous unmaking* and punish it by the cost of repair. The only true murder in Sallowreach is an unlicensed Closing, and it is punished with the jar.

### The Only Inheritance

There is one family in Sallowreach in which property passes, and everyone knows which, and nobody says why.

The Hand-lines inherit. Not often, and never in public, but over six centuries the great houses of Grue, Vane, Sedge and Hethe, and the dozen lesser lines, have each seen their seats pass from parent to child perhaps four or five times, while in the rest of the country nothing has passed at all. The Court calls these occasions *retirements*. A Hand-line lord or lady, Kept a century or so, grows tired of the seat, retires to a wing of the estate, and is not seen again; and the heir, who is always at the Course, takes the seat; and the household, for a week afterward, wears grey ribbons and speaks quietly; and the wing is shut up and its windows bricked.

It was put into law in 588. Lady Imber Fallow, then thirty, at the Course, went up to her father's rooms at Wending Mound in the evening with a basin of warm water and a towel, as she had every evening for twenty years since he had lost the use of his arms; and Lord Odo Fallow, Kept since 471, Unvacated master of Wending and eleven weirs, was found the next morning in his bath, finished, the water cold and grey, his face sunk and peaceful and smelling of nothing at all. Imber said only that he had asked her. There was no license. No one else had been in the room. By the law of 301 it was unlicensed Closing and the sentence was the jar.

The Court sat on it for a year. Then Regent Grue, in a ruling of nine lines that is still read aloud in every Hand-line nursery, held that a Closing performed *within the line, upon a member of the line, by one of the line's own hands* was a private matter of the house, *licensed by blood*, and not a matter for the Court at all. The doctrine is called **domestic license**. It applies only to the Hand-lines, because only the Hand-lines have hands. Since 588 forty-three Hand-line heads have retired. Every one of them was succeeded by a child or a niece at the Course. Not one of the forty-three is recorded as having asked a Closer of the Court.

Imber Fallow sat in her father's chair for fifty years, raised her son Pellam in the house where she had done it, and was herself retired in 638, at Pellam's hands, a year before the Court took them off him. The Heirs' broadsheet printed the whole case in 640 under the headline *THEY DO IT TOO*. That issue sold more copies than any other, and the Court jarred the printer's tongue.

## Culture and Daily Life

**Keeping.** Families are responsible for their dead. Six or seven generations share a house: the living on the ground floor and in the kitchen, the Kept upstairs in the cool rooms with the shutters closed. Grandmothers are restitched each spring in Restitching Week. Kept relatives sit at table, mind the children, mend nets, complain. The quarrel over whose turn it is to keep great-great-grandfather is the standard subject of comedy; the play *Uncle on the Stair*, in which three brothers try to lose the old man in Lastgate and he keeps coming back, has run since 340.

A Kept attic has its own climate. The shutters are kept closed for the flies and the stairs are kept closed for the smell, and so the cool rooms at the top of a Lastgate house are dim, still and sweet, hung with drying lavender and fly-paper black with catch, and furnished with the chairs of the dead: high-backed, deep-seated, upholstered in oilcloth that wipes clean. The floorboards under each chair are stained dark in a ring. Under the window there is always a basin of vinegar and a stack of clean cloths, and in a drawer the family keeps the parts that have come away and have not yet been sewn back, each in its labeled twist of paper, *Gran's ear, Plenty 639*, so that nothing of anyone is lost.

[pull] Three generations to a bed, four to a chair, nine to a stair, and Gran on the landing, telling you to mind your step. | — Weir Row housekeeping chant

**Smell.** Everyone carries perfume, vinegar or smoke. Scent is class. The rich smell of cedar, myrrh and ambergris, burned in braziers at their doors and soaked into their clothes. The middling smell of lavender and vinegar. The poor smell of peat smoke, and when they cannot afford it, of themselves. A pomander is a gift for a betrothal. Sniffing in company is extremely rude.

**Dress.** High collars, long sleeves, gloves and veils, to cover seams. Linen is preferred because it can be boiled; wool takes the smell and keeps it. The Kept favor wrapping: strips of waxed linen, wound tight, which hold things in. The Hand-lines wear mittens at the Appetite and gloves after, black kid sewn at the wrists. Never offer to shake a Sallowreacher's hand.

**Food.** The living eat eel (lively), reed-bread, fen-mushroom, pickled everything, and imported Fatlands meat at feasts. Vinegar is in every dish. Stilled meat, closed by a licensed butcher, is a luxury. A dish of lively eel is eaten quickly, with a strong jaw, and washed down with *sour*, a peat-smoked beer. The Kept do not need to eat. Many still sit at table and chew, for company, and spit into a dish.

**Marriage.** Vows in Sallowreach end with *until finished*, which once meant something. Widowhood does not exist. A spouse who died is still a spouse, and remarriage is bigamy. Many marriages carry on between the living and the Kept, and what passes between them behind the shutters is a matter the Court does not regulate and no one discusses; the word for it is *coldbed*, and it is said only by the rude. A living person may petition for a *setting-apart*, which is not a divorce but a court order permitting them to live in another house from their Kept spouse. It requires the spouse's consent. Henna Farrow has filed forty-one times.

**Children.** Living children grow up surrounded by the dead and are fearless about it. They are trained young to hold a lamp for a Seamer, to tie a wrap, and never to stare. Children who die stay the age they died, forever. The Smalls, the child-dead of the Drowned Storeys, have their own society and their own queen. The living do not speak of them at dinner.

The children of Lastgate also play *Closer*, in which one child wears their mother's mittens and the others line up to be "finished" with a tap on the forehead and lie down in the road very still, and the winner is the one who can lie still the longest while the others tickle. Parents on the Upper Boards forbid it. Parents in the Sinks do not, and the Heirs say the Sinks children are better at it.

**Setting Aside.** When someone is too far gone to stand, they are wrapped, labeled and shelved in the Lofts, still aware. This is the nearest thing to a funeral. The family gathers and the wrapper winds the linen from the feet up, and the family speaks to the face until it is covered. Families visit on feast days and read aloud: letters, news, the old one's favorite books. Many do not visit. The Wardens read to those.

**Proverbs.** *May you finish* is the polite greeting. *Long life* is the worst curse. *Mind your stitches* means be careful. *He'll keep* means a problem can wait forever. *Fresh as a Glad Century fiddler* means hopelessly out of date. *The Court has hands* means you cannot escape it. *Close the book* means end an argument by force.

**Oaths on the dead.** Sallowreachers swear by their dead in the literal sense. The strongest oath is sworn with the right hand placed inside the mouth of a Kept parent, who must consent and must be able to bite. To break such an oath is to invite the parent to close their jaw, and the oldest jaws in Lastgate are wired shut until a promise falls due. Debts sworn this way are the only ones the moneylenders trust.

The jaw-oath has teeth in the most literal sense, and the Court of Wrongs keeps a box of the evidence. When Hamo Rudd of Tanner's Boards swore in 597 on his Kept mother's jaw to pay a debt by Carving and did not, his mother, who loved him, closed her mouth in the Court before witnesses as the law required, slowly, with her eyes on his, and he did not take his hand out until the bone gave. She wept afterward, they say, for nine years. She keeps the two fingers in her shut-niche, beside his milk-teeth. That is what an oath on the dead is in the north: not a curse sent from somewhere else but a mother's mouth, and she is obliged to use it, and she does.

Blasphemy, in a country that ate its god, is a matter of where you put your hands. To clasp them open on your knees, as Ossel sat in the carvings, is the gravest insult a Sallowreacher can offer, and drunk Heirs do it in the doorways of Hand-line houses to start fights. To shut someone's eyes who is not asleep is a mortal offence. To *finish a song* that was not written to be finished, by inventing a last verse to one of the old fen-songs, is the commonest blasphemy of the young, and the Finishers have made a liturgy of it.

[pull] Never close the book on someone else's finger. | — Lastgate proverb, meaning: do not take a death that was not yours to take

**Crime.** Theft, fraud and debt are common. Violence is cheap but pointless, since it kills nobody; Sallowreach's criminals specialize in leverage. The worst crimes are part-theft (see Marrow Jack), unlicensed Closing, and *stilling*, the illegal sale of closed meat.

**Tablenight.** On the longest night of Lack, the living cook a banquet for their dead, who cannot taste it, and everyone pretends. The table is laid with the best plates. The Kept are dressed, lacquered and seated. The living serve. Everyone eats, the living swallowing and the Kept chewing and spitting. Speeches are made praising the food. At midnight the eldest present says "May we all finish," and the room drinks. In the old houses of the Upper Boards one chair at the end of the table is always left empty, and nobody can say for whom. When asked, the Unvacated say only that it was always so, and that it would be rude to sit there.

### The Closing

The only good death in Sallowreach is bought. A licensed Closing follows a rite fixed by the Court in 301 and never altered.

[fiction]

## Factions

### The Mortuary Court

The Hand-lines nobility: Grue, Vane, Sedge, Hethe and a dozen lesser houses. Sellers of death, hoarders of it, and increasingly afraid of their own hands. The Court believes in order: that a country of the undying must be governed by the oldest, that death is too precious to give away, and that the Hand-lines were chosen by the god to ration its mercy. What it wants now is to survive its own success. It knows the Hushes follow its blood. Its factions are split between those who would wall up the Course-stricken in lead (the Leaden party), those who would sell the Hushes as estates (the Quiet Market), and a very few who suspect that being closed like a book might be the god's kindness to the land.

#### The Proving of Hands

The Court licenses every Closing in Sallowreach but one kind, and that kind it does not record. When a Hand-line heir reaches the Course, the house must know that the hand works, and the hand must be taught. This is done in the family chapel at dusk, in a rite older than the Licensing, and the Hand-lines call it the Proving.

A living person is needed: a whole one, unready, with a pulse. Never a servant of the house, because servants talk, and never one of the line. The house's steward buys a wage-pledge from the debt-rolls of the Sinks, where there is always a laborer or a spinster whose wages are owed to an Unvacated landlord until some year in the next century, and the house pays off the debt, and the debtor is told they have been taken into service. They are brought to the estate by punt. They are washed and dressed in grey linen and given the best supper of their lives, and the household is kind to them all that day, very kind, the way you are kind to the beast before a feast. At dusk they are seated in the single chair in the chapel. The household stands along the walls and sings the first six verses of the Ferrying Song. The heir's gloves are unpicked.

The first touch is laid flat on the breastbone, and it takes the living person as Ossel took the fen's dying for a thousand years, gently: the heart stops, the breath goes out, the head drops. Then the household waits, in silence, as long as it takes, usually less than an hour, for what everyone in Sallowreach waits for: the Proof opens its eyes again, Kept, and sees where it is, and understands. The second touch is laid over those eyes. That one finishes. Two deaths in one hour, the Hand-lines say, one to teach the hand to take and one to teach it to end; and the heir who can do the second with the Proof's eyes open and pleading is judged ready to sit at the Court.

The Court, which knows, has never forbidden it. The Licensers keep a quiet tariff. The sacks go into the Sump without names, and the Sackmen who drop them are paid not to say the words. There are, by the Heirs' reckoning, some five hundred Proofs under the black water since the Licensing, and not one of them has a strip. Ebba Corn is the only one whose name anyone knows. The Heirs have painted it on the Ferrying Post.

[box: The Hollowe Tree]
Every Hand-line keeps its Cut strong by marrying within the Hand-lines; that much is public, and the genealogies on the walls of the Hall of Hands are a thicket of Grue-Vanes and Sedge-Hethes. One line keeps it stronger. The Hollowe-Vanes of Hollowe Mound, a cadet line of the Vanes three hours north of Glovehall, have married no one but Hollowe-Vanes since the Licensing. The Vanes count the night the eldest Vane boy spent with his hand held in the god's hand as a kind of wedding, and say so at their weddings; the Hollowes say that the line has been married to the hand ever since, and that it would be a kind of faithlessness to marry out. Provost Sedge's ledgers record the Hollowe family tree on a single page, because it does not branch. It folds.

The house keeps a nursery wing with no windows. Its children are said to be born quiet, not crying, with fingertips already grey, at the Taste in the cradle. More than one Hollowe nurse has left service and gone to the Sinks and would not say why, except that the household was very close, and that in a house where everyone is cousin, aunt, brother and husband to everyone else at once, nobody can be sure which word to use at table, and nobody tries. There are three Hushes within a day of Hollowe Mound, the densest cluster in the north. The young cousin Lord Casimir Hollowe-Vane, who wrote to Osric Vane every month for years, ran south across the Finishing Post in 638 and has not been heard of since. In the Sinks they say he ran to marry somebody, anybody, else.
[/box]

### The Seamers' Guild

Stitchers, embalmers, lacquerers and wire-workers who hold the population together. Every Kept body in Sallowreach owes its shape to the Guild, and every family owes it money. The Guild believes in craft and in continuance: that a well-kept body is a dignity and a duty. What it wants is pay, respect, and to be left alone. A Seamers' strike would be a national collapse within a month, and the Guild has threatened one only once, in 459, when the Court tried to tax wire. The tax was withdrawn in nine days, by which time the Sinks were knee-deep in what had fallen off people.

### The Finishers

A death cult in the plainest sense, and the fastest-growing faith in the north. The Finishers believe that the Hushes are Ossel's mercy returning, that the god never wanted to be eaten and is calling its people home, and that to walk into a Hush is to be closed by the god's own hand instead of a Court official's. They make pilgrimage to the Hushes in long, singing processions, mostly of the Kept, carrying candles and the names of the dead, and they walk in. The Court calls it theft of a licensed good and has posted guards at the larger Hushes. The guards are Kept, and some of them have walked in too.

The Finishers' songs have last verses, which in Sallowreach is a revolutionary act. They are sung at the edge of the grey reeds, and the singers go over the line one by one, and the song gets thinner and thinner as it goes, until there is no one left outside to finish it. Those who remain behind are the worst of it: the living children of Kept parents who went in, the husbands who held their wives' hands to the edge and could not follow, because the living who enter a Hush die too, and do not come back, and the Finishers do not want them yet. The Court records the number of pilgrims. Nobody records the number of people who stood at the edge and watched someone they loved go quiet.

#### What the Edge Takes: the Coldharrow Carving

The Court's figures say that fifty-nine of the living of Coldharrow walked into Dunmere Hush between Carving 637 and the end of Lack 638. The Court's figures are correct. They are a list of names, a list of dates and a bill. This is what they do not record.

[fiction]
*Petition of Agathe Rusk, living, aged sixty-one, of Coldharrow, to the Clerk of Fees of the Mortuary Court, contesting a bill of eight hundred lacks for "two deaths not paid for." Written for her by the Coldharrow schoolmaster.*
Sir, I have your bill and I will pay it. I write only to tell you who it is for, because the bill says *Rusk, W.* and *Rusk, B.*, and they had names.
My husband's mother went first. Old Mam Rusk, Kept since 519. She had been at the Settling a hundred years and she hurt every day of them, and when the Finishers came through with their candles in the Carving she put on her good shawl and went with them, and I helped her down the ladder into the punt. I am not sorry for that. I would do it again. She kissed me on the forehead and her lips were cold and she said, "You've been a good girl to me, Aggie." I had not been. She said it anyway.
My son Wendel went after her to bring her back. He was thirty-four. He was always the one who went after things. He got to the Verge the next night and she had gone over already, and he stood at the grey line, they told me, from dusk until the morning, and then he came home.
He came home quiet. That is the only word for it. He did his work. He cut reed. He ate what I put in front of him. But he hummed, sir, all the day, under his breath, the same tune, and when I asked him what it was he did not know he had been doing it. It was the last verse of the Finishers' song, the one that is not written down. I know that now. I did not know it then.
Nine days after, he was gone in the night. His boots were by the door, side by side, the toes pointed south-east, toward Dunmere. He had not taken them. He had cleaned them first.
After Wendel it was Hobb Lister's girl, who had been sweet on him. Then old Lister. Then the Coopers' two. By Lack there was humming in every house in Coldharrow. People did not know they did it. You would be pulling reed with a neighbour and she would be humming it and you would find that you were humming it with her, and then the both of you would stop and not look at each other. People started to say they could hear their dead out there in the quiet, waiting, and that it was very kind, the quiet. Children drew grey lines in the hearth-ash. Men left their boots by the door at night pointed south-east, and in the morning they did not remember doing it, and their wives turned the boots round, and the next night they were pointed south-east again.
We tried. You should know that we tried. We put bells on the doors. We slept in pairs. I tied my daughter Bryony's wrist to mine with a ribbon every night, the blue one from her christening, and she let me, and she said, "I'm not going anywhere, Mam," and she meant it. She was twenty-nine. She was the cleverest of us.
On the first night of the Shutter Days I fell asleep. I had not slept properly for forty nights. When I woke the ribbon was untied and folded on the pillow, very neat. I went to the Verge. I asked every Lamp and Singer at the edge. Nobody had seen her. Her name was on no strip. But I stood at the line, sir, where the grey starts, and I could feel it, how kind it was, the quiet, and how it would not hurt. I understood her. I want the Court to know that I understood her completely.
I did not go over. Someone has to pay the bill.
I will pay it. I ask only that the Court write their names on the receipt, the whole names, Wendel Amos Rusk and Bryony Agathe Rusk, because nobody else has written them anywhere.
*(Endorsed by the Clerk: Fee confirmed. Names noted. Receipt to be issued as requested. No further action.)*
[/fiction]

Coldharrow is the worst of it, but it is not alone. Wherever the Finishers pass, or a Hush grows near enough to hear its silence, the same thing begins among the living: the humming first, then the boots by the door, then the talk of how kind it is, then one, and then more, and always those who had already lost someone to the edge. The Kept walk in because they have suffered too long. The living go because grief has made the quiet sound like home. The Finishers say they do not want the living yet and turn them back where they can. They cannot always tell, in the dark at the edge, who is breathing.

[box: Running the Contagion]
Treat the Verge-hum as madness spreading by the god's influence, and treat it with care. A PC who spends a season within a day of a Hush, or who stands at the edge while someone they love walks in, makes a Dread 3 check; on failure, beyond the Fray, they find the hum in their mouth. A PC who Breaks within sight of a Hush should take **The Pull** as their Derangement, and those who witness that Break make the usual Dread 2 check to share it. Never stage the walking-in of a living character as a scene to be watched; cut to the morning, to the boots, to the ribbon on the pillow, to the bill. The horror of Coldharrow belongs to Agathe Rusk. Let the players meet her, and let her offer them tea, and let them hear her not humming.
[/box]

[box: Handling the Finishers]
The Finishers are a portrait of despair in a land that has no exit, and the pull of a god that offers one. Play them as tragedy. Their pilgrims are tired, frightened, often kind, and they have been suffering for longer than any human being was meant to. The horror is not the walking in; it is what it says about a country that this is the only door, and who it leaves behind. Never present a pilgrimage as triumphant or a Hush as an attractive solution for the living. Focus scenes on the people at the edge.
[/box]

## Sallowreach Now

Sallowreach has started to end. The Hushes are multiplying, eighteen now, and they appear fastest near Hand-line estates. The Court has realized what that means: the nobility is the god's way back, and the country will not be freed from undeath so much as closed like a book. Provost Carrion Sedge can prove it. The Court's answer, so far, is lead mittens, locked wings and silence.

Meanwhile the Vigil's envoy, Castellan Dray, has taken rooms on the Upper Boards and is offering Regent Grue the oil of a nation for the secret of Keeping. The Finishers have tripled in a year. A Scrapling girl in the Sinks can close things with her hands. The forty thousand in the Lofts have begun, all of them, to whisper the same word. And the Heirs have noticed that, for the first time in six centuries, an elder can be made to die.

### Adventure Hooks

- **The Deadwatch.** Castellan Dray hires the PCs as guides and bodyguards while he negotiates. The Regent is tempted. The secret of Keeping is not a secret: it is the god in the soil, and the Vigil would have to move to Sallowreach to use it. Dray knows this. His real commission is to find out how many Vigil citizens Sallowreach could house.
- **The Glovehall Garden.** The Hush behind Glovehall grew three yards last week. Lady Corrow Vane hires the PCs to measure it secretly, nightly, and to find out whether it is growing from her brother's wing or from her own bedroom.
- **The Little Closer.** The Court, the Finishers, the Cutwrights and Marrow Jack all want Pim Sorrel. Her mother hires the PCs to get her across the border, where the Closing touch might stop. It might not. And the girl does not want to leave.
- **Sack Seventeen.** A Sump dredger has hauled up a sack whose occupant claims to be a Regent of Sallowreach older than Ansel Grue, sunk for treason in 340. If true, Grue's appointment was illegal and every license he ever signed is void.
- **The Last Verse.** Bram Hollin has been hired to mourn the first proper funeral in Lastgate in six hundred years, for a living woman closed by accident. The family wants it done right. Halfway through the rite, the corpse begins to whisper the same word as the Lofts.

**Ansel Grue, the Unvacated Regent.** Four hundred and twelve years old, three hundred of them in office. Grue died in 371 of a fall from a horse, and has been mostly wire, lacquer and habit since. He sits in a high chair in the Hall of Hands with his jaw wired shut and his face lacquered to a deep glossy amber, like a violin. ## Playing a Sallowreacher

You grew up with your great-great-grandmother in the attic and the smell of cedar in your clothes. You have held a lamp for a Seamer. You have never been to a funeral. You say *may you finish* to strangers and mean it kindly.

### Who's in Your Attic?

Every Sallowreacher grew up under somebody. Roll or choose the Kept elder who shaped you; they are still there, and they still have opinions about your life.

| d10 | Your Kept elder | What they want from you now |
| 1 | A great-grandmother at the Leathering, sharp as vinegar | To be restitched by you and nobody else |
| 2 | A drowned uncle who still goes to the weir each morning | For you to come with him, just once |
| 3 | A father, Unvacated, who owns the house and your wages | Obedience, until about 760 |
| 4 | A fever-Kept aunt from the Grey Plague, always hot | A cool cloth, and for you to sit with her in Plenty |
| 5 | A sibling who died young and stayed young | For you to stop growing older than them |
| 6 | A grandfather at the Long Grey who no longer speaks | Nothing you can hear; he taps the arm of his chair |
| 7 | A mother on the shelf in the Lofts | Visits on feast days; the end of a book you started reading her |
| 8 | A great-great-grandfather who fought in the War of the Heirs | To know whether you are with the Heirs |
| 9 | A grandmother in a barrel at Brinemoss | For you to pay the visiting fee, and to tell her it is still spring |
| 10 | A parent who walked into a Hush | Nothing, ever again; you have only the name-strip |

**Attribute bonus:** +1 HAND. Sallowreachers are stitchers, menders, eel-catchers and punt-men, and the Hand-lines rule them.

**The Sop:** close something that was meant to stay open. Shut the eyes of a sleeper; close a book someone is reading; snuff a lamp someone needs; end a conversation mid-sentence with a hand on the speaker's arm; shut a door on someone outside. It must matter to someone. The Sop removes 1d6 Hunger, and the Want to Close grows: for the rest of the day, resisting it is one step harder.

**The Tooth:** when a Sallowreacher Partakes, the fingers go cold to the second knuckle, the nearest flame leans away, and for a heartbeat every sound in the room drops, as if a door had shut somewhere. When the Tooth shows a 1, the god bites back: the character's hand closes involuntarily on whatever it holds, hard enough to crack a cup, and stays closed for a minute.

### Gifts

**The Taste (Regrowth 1–3).** *Lidsense:* you always know whether a door, lid, eye or book within ten paces is open or shut, even in darkness, and gain +2 to Search and Instinct rolls to notice concealed openings. *The Quiet Hand:* you can close any door, lid or shutter in perfect silence.

**The Appetite (Regrowth 4–6).** *The Shutting:* once per scene, lay a hand on a speaker and make a Tongue + Rites roll against Hard; on success their current argument, threat or song ends, and they cannot recall where it was going. Costs 1 Hunger. *Cold Fingers:* +1 to Stitching (cold hands are steady), but you must wear mittens or gloves in company, and any bare-handed touch on a living person is a Dread 1 experience for them.

**The Course (Regrowth 7–9).** *The Closing Touch:* bare hand, Hand + Rites against Routine to close a flame, a wound's pain, a fly-swarm (one cloud) or a Kept person. Closing a Kept person finishes them forever; it costs 1 Hunger and is murder without a license. Against a living target outside Sallowreach, the touch is an attack (Hand + Rites vs Guard) dealing 4 damage that ignores Armor. Inside Sallowreach, a living target touched with intent must succeed at a Grim Caul + Resolve check or fall to Ruin and rise Kept. *Long Hands:* your hands grow extra joints; +1 Filch and Stitching, and everyone who sees them bare makes a Dread 2 check.

**The Brink (Regrowth 10–11).** *Hushing:* by Partaking, you may let silence spill from you for a scene: no sound carries within ten paces, flies die, flames lean and dim, and every Kept person in the radius must make a Hard Caul + Resolve check or kneel and plead to be closed. Costs 2 Hunger. *Closing Gaze:* a full minute's stare finishes a candle, a small animal, a conversation or a Kept person, without touch. Small Hushes begin to appear where you sleep: the GM marks one on the map each season.

### The Wants

- **Close.** Triggered by an open door, an unclosed eye, an open book, an unlidded jar within reach. Resist with Caul + Resolve vs Routine at the Taste, Hard at the Appetite, Grim at the Course, Dire at the Brink.
- **Finish.** Triggered by an unfinished thing in your presence: a song, a story, a quarrel, a life. You must end it, one way or another. Resist as above.
- **Quiet.** Triggered by laughter, crowds, bells and children playing. At the Course you must leave or silence the source. Resist as above, one step harder in Lastgate.

### The Seating

At Regrowth 12 the character becomes a Hush. They do not die and they do not leave; they stand still and the silence spreads from them, slowly, for the rest of the campaign. Everyone and everything inside it ends. The player retires the character, and the GM places the new Hush on the map. Their friends may visit the edge. If the Hush is near the party's home, it will reach it in a year or two.

**Your last sound.** When a PC is Seated, the player describes the last sound the Hush ever let through: a door closing, a name, a laugh cut off, the Ferrying Post bell. Every PC who later stands at the edge of that Hush hears it, once, faintly, from just behind them, and must make a Dread 3 check.

### Playing One of the Kept (optional)

A PC who falls to Ruin in Sallowreach and takes further damage does not die. They become Kept. Players may also begin play Kept, by choice.

- **No more death.** A Kept character no longer loses Flesh to dying. At 0 Flesh they suffer Ruin and collapse as normal, but further damage adds **Rot** instead (1 Rot per Ruin, or per 5 damage taken while Ruined). They need no food, drink, breath or sleep, and are immune to poison, drowning and suffocation. Cold and heat they feel, but cannot be killed by.
- **Rot (0–10).** Rot also rises by 1 each season without Seamer upkeep, and by 1 for any fall, fire or soaking that is not tended within a day. At Rot 3 (*Ripe*) the smell is unmistakable: −2 to Stealth and to Persuade with the living. At Rot 5 (*Loose*) parts come away: −1 to Lung and Hand rolls, and every scene of exertion has a chance to shed something (GM's call). At Rot 7 (*Ruinous*) the character is a Dread 2 horror to the living and must be carried more often than not. At Rot 10 the character is Set Aside: retired to the Lofts, still aware, and the player makes a new one. They can still be visited.
- **Seamer upkeep.** A Seamer's restitching (Hard Hand + Stitching, an hour, 30 lacks in materials) removes 1 Rot; a full spring restitching in the Guild's hands removes 2 and costs 120 lacks. A Kept character can Stitch themselves at one step harder.
- **The border.** A Kept character who leaves Sallowreach drops dead at once, completely, all their unfinished rot arriving in a heartbeat. There is no save. A Kept PC cannot follow the party south.
- **The Regrowth continues.** A Kept Hand-line still Partakes and still accrues Hunger and Regrowth. A Kept PC who is Closed is finished forever.
- **Fray.** The Kept still feel. Every season of Rot 5 or more adds 1 Fray. The commonest Derangements among the Kept are believing that one is still alive, and believing that everyone else has died.

#### Waking Kept

The first waking is the worst hour a Sallowreacher ever spends, and the player should be given time to play it. The new Kept opens their eyes cool, still, with no breath and no heartbeat and every sense sharpened, and hears, before anything else, the absence of their own pulse. They make a Dread 3 check. They choose one thing they will try to keep doing as if alive (eating at table, sleeping in a bed, breathing to speak, blushing with lacquer) and keep it up as long as they can; while they do, they gain +1 to Persuade with the living. They also lose any living Knack that depends on warmth, appetite or breath, at the GM's judgment. And the family, if there is one, has to be told, and will fetch a Seamer, and will begin, gently, from that morning, to speak of the PC as one speaks of the elders upstairs.

#### What Comes Loose

When a Kept character at Rot 5 or more exerts themselves and the GM calls for it, roll a d10. Any part may be stitched back by a Seamer within the week (Hard Hand + Stitching); after that it is the Guild's stock, or Marrow Jack's.

| d10 | Shed |

#### Kept Courtesies

The Kept have manners that the living do not teach, because the living do not need them. A Kept PC knows to sit downwind; to keep a handkerchief for what leaks; never to sit on another's good chair; to carry their own spitting-dish to table; to wear gloves to a Closing so that no one thinks they are begging; and never, ever, to stand near a Hand-line with their face turned up, as if asking. A Kept PC who breaks these courtesies before other Kept loses face; one who keeps them before the living is treated, in the old houses, with the slightly frightened courtesy owed to an elder, whatever their age.

### Backgrounds

- **Restitched Apprentice.** A Threader of the Seamers' Guild who knows every family on their street by its smell, and who has begun to notice which bodies are missing parts.
- **Disinherited Heir.** Forty-eight, living, legally a minor, owner of nothing; your father is upright and the Heirs have offered you a punt and a sack.
- **Glovebearer of a Closer.** A clerk who unpicks the gloves of a Hand-line Closer and has seen what that hand does, and whose own fingers have started going cold.
- **Sackman's Child.** You have dropped the rope and said the words a thousand times, and you know the patterns the bubbles make, and lately you have started to understand them.
- **Reader of the Lofts.** You read to four hundred shelved strangers every night and know them by their whispers; one of them has started whispering your name.
- **Walker Who Came Back.** You carried a candle and a name to the edge, and watched the procession go in, and turned home; the hum has been in your mouth ever since.
- **Hand-line Cadet.** A minor cousin of a lesser line, in mittens since you were nine; your house has bought a Proof for the day your hands go grey, and you have met her, and she is kind to you.
- **Tam Ruddock's Passenger.** A foreigner smuggled north to escape a death that would have been quick, now Kept, unable ever to go home, slowly learning the language of the people who have been living like this for six hundred years.
