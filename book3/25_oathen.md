# Oathen: What the Witness Heard

> Say less.
>> — the whole of Oathen's law, as taught to children

>> — inscription over the Lesser Gate of Tacit, carved without a single verb

> I have been listening. I have not stopped.
>> — scratched into the lead of the third Gilded Cell, and crossed out, and scratched again

RECORD OF A LAWFUL DISSOLUTION, kept by the undersigned clerk of the Bench of Clauses, in the Ninth Room beneath the Ledger, on the eleventh day of Carving, 640 A.G. The condemned: Aubin Sele, cooper, convicted of selling the same cask twice under oath. Present: the condemned, the Breaker Jude Crake, the clerk (bitted, writing only), one Surety-witness (bitted). The hour-glass is turned at the first word.
CRAKE: Good evening, Master Sele. No, sit, the chair is for you. I brought figs. You needn't eat them. I only thought the room wanted something sweet in it.
SELE: (says nothing for the space of nine grains)
CRAKE: You've been told what I am. That's all right. People always have. I'm not going to touch you, and nothing in this room is sharp, and the door is not locked. You may check it. (The condemned does not check it.) I'm told you were a cooper. My father bent staves. I remember the smell of the steaming shed, the oak going soft. Is it true you can hear a bad cask?
SELE: If it's dry. If you knock it. (He stops himself. He has said nothing binding. The clerk notes his relief, visible in the shoulders.)
CRAKE: That's lovely. A ringing note, I'd guess, and a dead one where the split is. (Some minutes on staves, pitch, his wife's cooking, the late rain. The condemned laughs once, at a story concerning a goat. The clerk notes that it is a good story.)
CRAKE: May I ask you something small? Your daughter. Wenna, isn't it? She's to be fostered with your sister in Low Dray. Would you like her told anything?
SELE: Tell her (stops) Tell her, if you're able, that her father thought of her.
CRAKE: That's beautifully put. The "if you're able" is mine, not yours, so it costs you nothing. You're careful. I admire careful. (The glass is at its quarter.) You're shaking. Are you cold?
SELE: No.
(The clerk notes that the condemned is shaking, and that the room is warm, and that "No" in answer to a question of present fact is an assertion. A small sound, like a knuckle cracking, from the condemned's jaw. A thin line of blood at the corner of the mouth. The condemned understands. He weeps. He does not cry out, which would be foolish.)
CRAKE: Oh, Aubin. I'm so sorry. It's only a little lie. Everyone tells it. Here, the cloth. You'll want to hold it under. Let's not talk about cold. Let's talk about Wenna. She'll be frightened of me when she's older, I expect. They'll tell her stories. Will you do one thing for me, so I can tell her you were brave?
SELE: (nothing for a long time; the glass is at its half) What thing.
CRAKE: Only stop crying before the glass runs out. That's all. So I can tell her.
SELE: I (stops) I'll try.
CRAKE: That will do.
(The clerk notes that "I'll try" is an oath of effort, the lightest there is, and that it is kept by trying. The condemned tries. He breathes slowly. He looks at the figs. The clerk notes that at the three-quarter mark he has very nearly stopped weeping, and that the Breaker is telling him, softly, about a girl he once saw in Low Dray who had red ribbon on her bit, and that perhaps it was Wenna, and that she looked well, and that she was holding the hand of a man who was not her uncle. The condemned asks what man. The Breaker says he didn't see the face. The condemned asks again. The Breaker says it was probably no one. The condemned begins to weep again.)
CRAKE: Aubin. Aubin. You promised you'd try.
SELE: I am trying, I am, I swear I am
(The clerk records that the condemned has sworn to a present fact, which was true, and that the true oath holds. The clerk records that the condemned then stopped trying, because he was thinking only of the hand that was not his daughter's uncle's, and that a man cannot try and not try in the same breath, and that the god heard which one it was.)
(At this point the record becomes difficult to keep. The clerk sets down only what is required. The first Breaking was the tongue's, for the lie about the cold, and it worsened, the jaw separating at its hinge with the sound of a green branch. The second was apt to "I'll try" and opened him along the effort: the long muscles of both arms and the back, which split lengthwise like staves without a hoop. The third was apt to "I swear I am": the chest, at the breastbone, so that the clerk could see the condemned's heart moving and observe that it was trying very hard. None of these was fatal. The condemned remained aware throughout. The Breaker held his hand.)
(The glass ran out at the forty-first grain past the hour's mark. The condemned had ceased by then. It is the Breaker's first hour to exceed the hour. He asked that this be noted. He asked that it be noted also that he did not see any man in Low Dray, and that Wenna Sele is well, and that he lied about it, and that the lie cost him the nail of the smallest finger of his left hand, which came away in the cloth, and which he left with the figs.)
Sealed in silence. Witnessed. Filed.
[/fiction]

## The Meal

[sigil: oathen]

### What Tolm Was

Before the Gorging, Tolm, the Witness, sat at the head of the Gullet, the deepest gorge in the country, and listened. It was the size of a hill, shaped like a seated person drawn by someone who had only heard one described, with a bowed head as long as a barn and ears without number: great folded ones like bracket fungus on a dead oak, and small ones down its throat and the insides of its arms, opening and closing like the gills of fish. Its mouth was small and always closed. Its tongue, the chronicles say, was the color of raw liver and as long as a street.

Tolm did not command and did not bless. It heard. A vow spoken in its presence became a fact the way a stone dropped becomes a fall. Marriages were made at its knee; boundaries were walked in its hearing; a liar standing before it found the lie had been true all along, or found himself face down in the red dust with blood coming out of his ears. The people of the canyons loved it the way a court loves a scrupulous judge: with fear, with gratitude, and with the steady low resentment of those who can never quite be off the record.

The old canyon folk did not pray to it so much as report to it. Every household sent someone up the Gullet at the turn of each season to kneel at the god's shin, which was as broad as a barn door and rough as bark, and say aloud what the family had done: what it had sold, whom it had married, which child had died and of what. They called this the Telling. It was not confession, because nothing was forgiven, and it was not worship, because nothing was asked. It was the keeping of a record. The ears nearest the speaker would turn and unfold as they talked, wet and pink inside, and a thin clear fluid would well at their rims, and that was how you knew you had been heard. Children were brought to the Telling as soon as they could stand, to say their names. The Ledger's oldest carving says that the god once leaned down, slowly, over the course of an afternoon, to hear a girl who was whispering, and that its head blotted out the sun over the Gullet, and that the girl was not afraid, and that what she whispered was the price of a goat.

[pull] We did not love it. You do not love the ground. You walk on it, and you are careful where you put your feet. | — the herders' saying of the upper gorges, recorded in 14 A.G.

Boundaries were made at Tolm's knee by two neighbours walking the line together, saying aloud at every cairn *this far, and no farther, for me and mine*, and the lines held better than walls. Debts were made there and paid there. A man who could not pay walked up the Gullet and said so, and the god heard, and what happened next depended on how he said it. A people who live in the hearing of a god that hears everything learn three things early: to say little, to mean it, and never to mistake silence for privacy.

[box: The Hearers]
Before the Gorging, Tolm had no priests. It had **Hearers**: men and women of the upper gorges who sat at the god's feet in shifts, night and day, for life, and kept the record of what was said to it on knotted cords and later on clay. They were chosen from children who could sit still the longest, and they were not permitted to speak in the god's presence except to repeat, word for word, a vow someone had made, so that the god could hear it twice. Their descendants are the Keeners of the Witnessing Vaults and, some say, the Echo-Wardens. The last Hearer's cord, a rope of knots as thick as a man's thigh and longer than the Gullet is deep, is kept in the sealed rooms of the Ledger. No one now can read it. The Bench keeps it anyway, because it is the record, and the record is never thrown away.
[/box]

### The Long Lack in the Canyons

The Lack came to Oathen as it came everywhere: the figs failed, the goats went dry, the green cheese would not ripen in the river caves, and the bread went to ash on the hot stones. But in Oathen the Lack had a second shape. In the third year of it, the Hearers noticed that the god's ears had begun to close.

Not all at once. A few of the small ones on the inner arms first, the rims drying and puckering shut like the mouths of old wounds. Then the throat-ears. By the fifth winter the great folded ears on the god's head were furled tight, grey, crusted at the edges, and the Hearers sitting at its knee reported that vows made before it no longer drew the clear fluid to the rims. The canyon people did what frightened people do in front of a god that has stopped listening. They talked louder.

#### The Hundred Vows

In the seventh winter of the Lack, the Hearers declared that Tolm was starving for words, and that a people who had always given it their record must now give it something heavier. They demanded vows. Not boundaries, not prices: sacrifices of the self, sworn at the god's shin, at the greatest Weight the canyons knew. The account on the Ledger, carved in 12 A.G. by a hand that shook, lists a hundred of them. A herdsman swore never to eat meat again, so that the god might eat. A mother swore she would never again speak her children's names, so that the god would have them, and keep them alive. A young man swore to walk into the Sheath and not come out until it rained. Forty of the hundred swore silence for life. Nine swore away their sight, and the clerks of the Ledger record, without comment, that they gave up their eyes at the god's knee, so that Tolm would see they meant it, and that the screaming was done afterward, at home, with the doors shut.

The god did not move. The ears did not open. The Lack went on for two more years. The herdsman starved with goat-meat hanging in his neighbour's smoke-house, because he had sworn. The mother's children died that winter, all three, and she could not say their names over the graves, and the Ledger records that she stood at the cairn opening and closing her mouth on nothing for an afternoon, and then went home and did not come out again. The young man in the Sheath was found at the spring melt, still walking, his feet worn to the bone, in a narrows so deep and so dark he had not been able to tell if it was raining overhead. The Hundred Vows were kept, every one, and they bought nothing. Then the canyons, having given the god everything they could promise, came back up the Gullet with knives.

[pull] We swore it everything. It took everything we swore. Then we took it. | — the last line of the account of the Hundred Vows, on the Ledger's western end

The Bench of Clauses has never ruled on the Hundred Vows, and the Bench rules on everything. The Plain Readers, the Bait-heretics of the Breakdowns, say that is because the answer is unbearable: that the god heard every vow, and that its ears were not closing in hunger but in patience, and that the Hundred were not food. They were seasoning.

### The Silent Supper

In the ninth winter of the Lack, the canyon people came to the Gullet with knives, and they came in silence, because they knew exactly what would happen if they did not. Anything said in front of the Witness would count. A cry of pain would become a sentence. A prayer would become a contract. "Forgive us" would become a debt. So they did not speak. They had spent a month practising, and the practice had a cost: a generation of hand-signs for butchery, a set of whistles agreed and then forbidden, the dry-throated discipline of a whole people learning to hold their tongues. Those who could not learn it were left at home, tied, with rags in their mouths. Some of the tied ones died in the cold while the rest were at the Gullet. They are the first silence anyone made on purpose in Oathen, and they are not on the Ledger.

They began at dusk. The old account, carved on the Ledger in the year 12 A.G., records that Tolm did not move, but that every ear on its body turned toward the knives as they came, the great ones and the little ones, a sound like ten thousand pages turning at once. The furled ears, closed for six years, opened. The first incisions were made at the throat, where the flesh was softest. What came out was not blood but something thick and clear that smelled of wet slate and tasted, those who caught it on their lips said afterward in handtalk, of the moment after you have said a thing you cannot take back.

They worked for three nights. The god's skin was tough as harness leather, lined with a fat that mouthed the shapes of words when cut: a yellow fat, granular, that puckered and pursed under the blade as though it were trying to say *knife*. Butchers wore wax in their ears and still came away with nosebleeds. The ears were taken first, because no one could bear to cut while they listened, and laid in rows on the canyon floor, where they went on twitching toward every footstep for a day and a half. Someone stepped on one. The sound it made was the sound of the person who had stepped on it, and it went up the canyon walls and came back, and that was the first echo anyone in Oathen was afraid of.

The butchery signs survive. Every Oathen child learns them, because they became the first words of handtalk, and the oldest signs in the language are still the signs for *hold*, *cut*, *pull*, *more hands here*, *it is looking at me*, and *do not stop*.

On the second night a woman named Deverel Ashe slipped in the slick and laid her forearm open to the bone; she knelt with her teeth clamped on her sleeve until her jaw cracked, and made no sound. Near the fires, a small child woke in its mother's arms and began to laugh at the flames. The carving says only that the mother held the child close for a long time, until it was quiet; that those nearest saw and did not stop her; that she sat with the bundle in her lap until dawn and then went back to work. Her name is not on the Ledger. Her line is: the Hushwell Tongue-line, among the highest in the land, whose crest is a closed hand over an open mouth.

[pull] The Hushwells do not tell the story of the Supper at their table. They do not have to. It is on their crest. | — a lady's maid of the Heights

The tongue was taken on the third night. It took forty people to draw it out from behind the teeth, hand over hand like a hawser, and as it came Tolm's mouth opened for the first and last time anyone remembered. The inside of the mouth was ridged like the roof of a cave and lined, the account says, with more ears, small and pale, all of them turned outward. The god spoke once. Every person present heard it in a different voice, the voice of whoever had first taught them to talk, and every account gives the same two sentences: "Go on. I am listening."

They went on. The families who ate the tongue, raw, in slices that kept curling in their hands, became the Tongue-lines, the nobility of Oathen. The slices were cold and very heavy for their size, and moved on the palate, and those who ate them said afterward that for a moment their mouths were full of words that were not theirs, like a crowd talking in the next room. The root went to the Daths, the tip to the Vanthes, the long middle to the Hushwells, the Esks, the Sarrows and a score of lesser houses. The ears were boiled and eaten by the canyon clans of the upper gorges, whose descendants are said to hear too well; the great slow heart went to the Rain-keepers of the high basin; the scraps and the clear fluid went to the poor, who licked it off the stone and were Scraplings ever after. The bones were rolled into the Gullet and are there still, under a floor of red sand, and the canyon above them is the quietest place in a quiet country.

In the morning the Lack ended, as it ended everywhere. In Oathen, the first thing anyone said aloud was said by a herdsman's son who found green shoots on the canyon rim and shouted, "It's over, I swear it's over!" It was. The boy's word was the first oath of the Long Grace, and it was kept. He lived to be ninety and never said anything again with quite that much confidence.

[box: The Two Sentences]
Theologians on the Bench of Clauses have spent six centuries arguing the grammar of Tolm's last words. "Go on" is permission, or command, or encouragement, or a sigh. "I am listening" is a statement of present fact, and the god of true speech could not make a false one, which means that at the moment it was being eaten it was listening, and the question that has never been settled is when, if ever, it stopped. The Theft doctrine reads it as a sentence: we are watched until we pay. The Gift doctrine reads it as forgiveness: go on, eat, live. The Bait doctrine, illegal in Oathen since 290 A.G., reads it the plainest way of all. The god said it was listening. It has not said that it has stopped.
[/box]

## The Rule of the Land

[sigil: oathen]

### Words Bind Flesh

In Oathen, a promise is a physical fact. Keep it and nothing happens. Break it and the body breaks to match: at once, with a sound, in a shape that fits the word. A man who swore to stand by his brother and ran had both legs fold under him backwards, the knees bending the wrong way like a heron's, and he lay in the road with his brother walking on without him. A woman who promised her heart to one man and gave it to another was found with her chest opened like a cupboard, the ribs swung outward on their cartilage like doors, the heart inside beating, entirely visible, entirely hers.

The Breaking is always apt and never by itself fatal. Tolm was a witness, not an executioner. What the god does to the forsworn is not punishment so much as testimony: it makes the body tell the truth that the mouth failed to. The broken live on, often for decades, as the Forsworn, and you can read a person's moral history from across the street. A merchant with no hands cheated someone. A woman whose lips have split back to the ears told a lie that mattered. A soldier with a spine bent like a hook swore to bear something and set it down. The canyons are full of these histories walking.

It does not have to be solemn. "I'll be back before dark" is an oath. "I'd die for you" is an oath. "I'll never forgive you" is an oath, and the man who swore it to his father and then, at the old man's deathbed, wept and took his hand, had his own hand turn to the consistency of wet bread in his father's grip. A child's "I promise" binds exactly as hard as a king's. The god does not weigh station. It weighs meaning.

[pull] A wise man in Oathen is not one who keeps his word. Any fool can keep a word. A wise man is one who never gave it. | — Sorrin Vael, Chief Clausewright, in a lecture to Slate-Clerks

### The Body Speaks for the Mouth

The Bench's physicians have been cataloguing Breakings since 3 A.G., and their Book of Ruptures runs to nine volumes, each organised by the word broken rather than the part. What the catalogue teaches, and what every canyon child knows without being taught, is that the body does not merely break. It *says*. A Breaking is a sentence written in flesh, in the god's own grammar, and its grammar is the plainest reading of what was sworn.

A man who swore to his partner *I'll give you my right arm if this deal goes bad* did not lose an arm: he was found the morning after the deal went bad with the arm intact and entirely useless, hanging from the shoulder like a sleeve with nothing in it, the muscle gone soft and the skin cold, because he had promised to give it, and it was given, and it was no longer his. A woman who swore to a lover *I will never let you go* broke two years later, when the lover went, and her fingers clenched shut and did not open again; the Bench's surgeons tried, in 588, to open them with screws, and found the tendons had grown into the palms. A drover who promised his wife he would *keep his eyes on the road* came home by a different road and lost the use of his neck: it locked forward, and he spent the rest of his life staring at the ground in front of his feet, and walked into walls.

The sound is always the first thing. Oatheners who have heard a Breaking describe it by its kind: the green-branch crack of a long bone going; the wet rip, like a sleeve torn off a shirt, of skin parting along a line it was never meant to part on; the hollow pop of a joint leaving its socket; the soft, almost polite sound of a heart's cupboard opening, which the Bench's physicians liken to a ripe fig pulled in half. A Breaking in the Felt Market stops all trade, because the sound carries through felt, and everyone in earshot knows at once that someone nearby has said something they did not mean, and every one of them, without exception, runs back through what they themselves have said that morning.

Then the smell: iron, and opened bowel when the oath concerned courage, and very often a smell of wet slate that has no source and that the Breakdowns call *the god's breath*. Then the blood, which in a Breaking comes oddly slowly, as if the body were not sure it had been given permission. Then the scream, if there is one, and in Oathen there often is not, because the broken know better than anyone that a scream can say things.

[box: The Book of Ruptures]
The nine volumes of the Bench's medical catalogue are kept in the Archive of Readings and copied for the Murmuring House and the Strap-House of the Breakdowns. A sample entry, from the fourth volume, *Of Oaths of Carrying*: "Item 1,114. A porter of the Galleries, sworn to carry his mother's coffin to the Vaults 'on my own back, all the way down', set it down on the ninth stair to rest. Rupture: the spine at the ninth vertebra, the body folding at that point to the angle of the stair. He completed the carry upon his knees, the coffin on his folded back, and the oath was then held to be kept, and the rupture did not reverse. Note in the margin: *the god does not take back.*"
[/box]

### What Counts

The Bench of Clauses has spent six hundred years defining what Tolm counts, and its definitions fill the four miles of the Ledger and a library besides. In practice, the canyon folk know the rule in their bones, because the ones who did not know it are no longer whole:

- A promise binds. Any statement that commits the speaker to a future act, a future state, or an abstention ("I will", "I won't", "I'll be there", "never", "always", "you can count on me").
- A lie breaks at once. A spoken assertion the speaker believes to be false is a broken oath the instant it is finished. The Breaking for a lie is nearly always the mouth: the lips, the tongue, the jaw, the teeth.
- A boast is a promise. "I could carry that alone" is heard as "I will carry it if asked." "Nothing could frighten me" is heard as a vow of courage, and the next thing that frightens the speaker will cost them.
- Meaning outweighs grammar. A sentence built to sound like a question but meant as a promise binds as a promise. Tolm heard what people meant, and it still does.
- Ignorance is no defense. An honest mistake about a fact (you said the well was dry, and you believed it, and it was not) is not a lie, but it is still a statement, and how the god will weigh it is a matter of furious debate. Most Oatheners do not risk it. They say "I believe the well is dry," which is true about them, rather than "the well is dry," which is a wager about the world.
- Sleep counts. A vow sworn in sleep binds as if waking, though at lesser Weight, and the Bench's records hold three hundred years of marriages that came apart over what one spouse heard the other say in the dark.
- Drink counts. The Bench has ruled six times that wine is not an Exit. This is why Oatheners fear it more than any other people on the Table, and why the Breakdowns, who have nothing left to break, drink fig-spirit by the jug.
- Echoes count. An oath said once in a place that throws it back has been said as many times as it comes back (see Echoes, below).

[pull] Do not say "always". Do not say "never". Do not say "I swear" unless you would cut off the hand that is lifted. Do not say "I'd die". You might. | — the Four Don'ts, chalked on the wall of a Galleries schoolroom

### How Oatheners Speak

So Oatheners do not chat. They speak rarely, slowly and in conditionals, and they never, ever exaggerate. A polite sentence is mostly exits: "If able." "As I now intend." "Barring death, illness, or weather." "Unless the Bench rules otherwise." "In so far as I can see." A shopkeeper asked whether the bread is fresh says, "It was baked this morning, I am told." A mother asked by her child if she will come back says, "That is my wish," and the child, who is bitted and cannot reply, learns from the sentence everything the mother cannot promise.

Foreigners on the Rim Road find Oatheners cold and evasive. Oatheners find foreigners obscene: people who say "I'll kill you" in jest and "I'll love you forever" at the end of an evening, and walk away whole. To an Oathener a talkative stranger is a man juggling knives in a nursery.

The Oathen voice is distinctive anywhere on the Table: low, even, very slightly slow, with a pause before every verb, as though the speaker were checking the ground before stepping onto it. Oatheners do not interrupt. They do not finish other people's sentences, which in Oathen is a kind of theft. They do not answer a question until it has been asked in full, and then they wait a breath longer, because the asker might yet add a clause. Laughter is rare and mostly silent: a shaking of the shoulders, a hand over the mouth. A loud laugh in Tacit makes people turn around, because a laugh is not a word, but it is very close to one.

[box: The Common Exits]
Every Oathener carries a pocketful of these, and uses them as other peoples use "please".
- **If able.** The universal exit. Covers death, injury, the weather and the Bench. Used for goodbye.
- **As I now intend.** Binds the speaker only to their present intention, which is a true fact about them and cannot be broken.
- **Barring death, illness, or weather.** The Three Bars. Used in contracts and courtship.
- **In so far as I can see.** For statements of fact. A blind man in Oathen can say almost anything.
- **I am told.** Shifts the weight of a fact onto whoever told you. Rumour in Tacit travels entirely in this form.
- **Unless the Bench rules otherwise.** Used for anything legal, and as a joke, which Oatheners find very funny.
- **For today.** A child's exit, learned before the Unbitting: "I'll be good, for today."
- **God willing.** Never used. It is a promise on Tolm's behalf, and the Bench has records of what happened to people who made one.
[/box]

[box: GM Advice — Running the Rule at the Table]
Oathen changes how players talk, and that is the point. Make it clear before play that in-character speech in Oathen is mechanically live: what a character says aloud is what the character has said. You are not trying to trap the players with grammar; you are letting them feel what it is to live in a country where every sentence might be a contract. Be generous in the first session. When a player says something binding by accident, pause and ask, "Is that what your character says, in those words?" Once they have felt the first Breaking land on someone else, stop asking. The best Oathen sessions are quiet ones, with long pauses before anyone answers a question, and players writing their character's sentences down before they say them.
[/box]

### The Forsworn: Bodies Like Broken Words

The broken are everywhere in Oathen, and not only in the Breakdowns. Most Oatheners carry a small mark or two: a crooked finger from a childish boast, a split lip healed white, a toe that turned in when someone was late home. These are not Forsworn. The canyon speaks of the Forsworn only for those whose Breaking was grievous, whose mark is plain from across a street, and whose word, therefore, no whole person will take.

A Forsworn body is a sentence that cannot be revised. The Breakings heal, after a fashion: the skin closes over what opened, scar-tissue grows along the lines of the rupture, bones knit at the angle the god set them. But they knit *as broken*. A chest opened for a marriage vow closes over the ribs where they now stand, outward, so that the skin stretches between them like a tent, translucent, and the heart can be seen moving under it in good light. A jaw that unhinged for a lie heals unhinged, and the Forsworn learn to eat with their fingers, pushing food back past the slack bone. Legs folded backward heal backward. The Breakdowns are full of people walking on knees that bend the heron's way, quick and jerking and oddly graceful, and the children of the Breakdowns, who are born whole, imitate the walk in their games until their mothers slap them for it.

There is a second breaking that the Book of Ruptures does not record. It happens a season or a year after the first, and it is in the mind. The Forsworn call it *the reading*. It is the moment the broken person understands that every stranger who looks at them knows exactly what they did, in what words, and that this will be true until they die. Some Forsworn go down into the Breakdowns and find the Showing and the common pot and the plain loud speech, and live. Some go the other way, inward, and sit in a back room with the shutters closed and their broken part wrapped in cloth, and say nothing, and eat nothing, and are found by the neighbours when the smell reaches the gallery. The Strap-House in Back-Before-Dark lane keeps a book of those, too. The Breakdowns read the names aloud at the Gathering of the Broken every Carving, loudly, because they can, and because no one on the Walls ever will.

[pull] You think the god punished me? The god did me a kindness. I was a liar for nine years with a whole face. Now I have the face I earned. Nobody has to wonder. | — a Forsworn of Honest Measure, mouth open to the ears

The Forsworn have a word for whole people: *Unread*. It is not a compliment. It means a person whose sins are still hidden, which in the Breakdowns is understood to mean a person who has simply been lucky, or careful, or rich enough to hire a Surety to take their Breakings for them. The broken have a saying: *every Unread is only a promise away*. It is the closest thing the Breakdowns have to a creed.

#### Broken Ground

A part lost to a grievous Breaking cannot break again. This is the Forsworn's single, terrible freedom. A woman whose hands came off for short weight can swear on the scales from now until her death and never lose another finger. A man whose chest stands open can tell any woman in the Lantern Lanes that he loves her and walk away without a mark. The Breakdowns call this *standing on broken ground*, and they have made a whole economy of it: liars-for-hire whose mouths are already split, loud-criers whose throats already opened, smugglers whose legs already folded and who will swear to any Watch patrol that they are going nowhere. The Bench has spent two centuries trying to draft a law that a broken mouth cannot evade. It has not succeeded.

### Echoes

[pull] Listen twice. | — the oldest proverb in Oathen, and the only one taught to foreigners at Treaty Stair

The great danger of the canyon country is echo. An oath repeated is an oath renewed, and a canyon will repeat anything. A man who swore in the narrows of the Sheath to pay a debt by the new Plate heard it come back nine times, and when he failed, he broke nine times, each Breaking landing on the last. He is buried at Sheathmouth in nine pieces. The Bench's ruling of 77 A.G., which has never been amended, holds that an echo is the speaker speaking again, because the god hears the voice and not the wall: every repetition the speaker hears is a fresh oath, of the same Weight, made by the same mouth, and broken at the same moment.

Whole gorges are posted as silent, with stone hands carved at their mouths, palm out. Wind is feared for the same reason: a vow overheard is still a vow, and when the dry Carving wind called the Tattler comes up from the south, families sit in their inner rooms in silence until it drops. The Tattler carries words. Herders on the plateau have heard, on its gusts, a sentence spoken in Tacit ten miles off, and the person who spoke it, by the Bench's ruling of 412, has made that oath again at the moment it was heard, and broken, if they broke it, twice.

Echoes do not always die. The Echo-Wardens divide them into four kinds, in their own clipped handtalk: *the short* (a word that dies in a bend or two, harmless except to the speaker); *the long* (a word that runs the length of a narrows, binding anyone who walks through it in its life, usually an hour or a day); *the kept* (a word caught in a hollow of the rock, a cave or fault, that comes out again whenever the wind or the river pushes air through it, for years); and *the living* (a word that has learned to move, and follows people). Every Warden knows the stories of the kept: the cave above Low Dray that says *I'll wait for you* in a young man's voice every evening when the river breeze turns, and has said it for a hundred and forty years, and the women of Low Dray who go up to listen to it because whoever it was meant it. The living are hunted.

Of the oath in Sworn Gorge, sealed since 420 A.G., the Wardens do not speak, even in handtalk.

[box: Why the Canyon Folk Lie Down to Speak]
In the open canyon country, people who must say something important (a marriage vow, a sale, a confession) lie down on the ground to say it, face to the dirt, with a fold of felt or a cupped hand over the mouth, so that the words go into the soil and not into the air. The soil does not echo. Travellers take the sight of two drovers lying face down in the road, murmuring, for a prayer, or a fit. It is a contract. Some of the oldest Oathen marriage-houses in the upper gorges have no beds at all, only a deep felt floor, because a couple who will spend forty years saying things to each other in the dark had better say them downward.
[/box]

### The Witness Is Inside

Tolm was the god who listened. It is still listening, from the inside: from the blood of those who ate it, from the rock over its bones, from canyons that throw every word back. The Rule is not a law of nature but the attention of a god, and attention can sharpen and wander. It has begun to count things it never used to count: handtalk, a scribe's chisel, perhaps, for the highest bloodlines, the silent words a person tells themselves at night.

And because it is listening, it can be spoken to. Blasphemy in Oathen is not an abstract offence. To curse Tolm aloud is to address a god that is present in your own throat, and the Bench's records hold four hundred years of cases of what happens to people who do. Most of them went quiet afterward. Not all of them by choice.

#### Blasphemy Against a Listening God

Elsewhere on the Table, a blasphemer insults a god that cannot hear, or is too busy growing back to care. In Oathen, the god is the one being spoken to, and the god is the one listening, and the god is the Witness by whose attention every word has weight. A curse against Tolm is therefore not an insult but a statement made directly to the record, and the record answers.

The Bench does not punish blasphemy. It records it, in a slim book bound in grey goatskin and kept in a felt-lined box in the Archive of Readings, called the Register of Address. The Register holds forty-one entries since the Muzzling. Thirty-nine of them end the same way: the blasphemer was found the next morning with their tongue gone, not cut but missing, as if never grown, the floor of the mouth smooth and pink and seamless, and the mouth full of fine red sand from the Gullet, three miles from any of them. The sand is warm. Some of them were found still breathing it. The other two entries in the Register were Sayers, and their entries are blank below the date, and the Bench has never entered what they said.

Oatheners curse, of course, as all people curse. They curse each other, the weather, the Bench and the price of figs. They curse in handtalk, which was safe until last year. What they do not do is curse Tolm, or address the god in anger by any of its names, or say *damn you* within hearing of an Ear Shrine. A child who stamps her foot and signs *I hate the god* is picked up by the nearest adult and carried away from any echoing wall before she can be tempted to say it.

The Plain Readers, the Bait-heretics of the Breakdowns and Knot Canyon, practise a blasphemy of their own, and it is worse because it is reverent. They do not curse the god. They *feed* it. At the Ear Shrines, after dark, they whisper to the stone everything they have ever said, from earliest memory: every promise, every lie, every word of love, every name, in long murmuring catalogues that take whole nights. They believe they are giving Tolm back the record it lost when it was eaten, and that when it has heard everything again, it will be whole. They are not always wrong about which shrines have begun to whisper back.

## The Regrowth in Oathen

[sigil: oathen]

Tolm is growing back, and what grows back is a witness: an ear that hears too well, a tongue that says too truly. In the Tongue-lines it grows fastest. The Bench of Clauses is the most godsign-ridden body of people in the country, and the more powerful a family, the more carefully it chooses who is allowed to speak.

### The Taste

It begins with the echo. You say something and, half a second later, you hear it again in your own voice, close behind your ear, slightly flatter, like someone reading it back for the record. At first it happens only in quiet rooms. Then everywhere. Children at the Taste whisper into cupped hands to hear whether it comes back. Adults learn to wait for it, and to listen to what they said with the god's attention, and to wince.

Other tells follow: a taste of wet slate when a promise is made nearby; the ears ringing faintly whenever someone in the room lies; a habit of mouthing other people's words along with them. Tongue-line children at the Taste are bitted early and watched closely, because the Taste is not dangerous, and the Appetite is.

[pull] It's like having a clerk behind your ear. A tired one. He doesn't judge. He just writes it all down, and reads it back, and you hear how it sounds. | — a Galleries weaver at the Taste

### The Appetite

The compulsion to swear. It comes on like a sneeze: a pressure in the throat, a heat behind the teeth, and then the words are out, vows on nothing, on everything. "I will walk to the sea." "I will never eat bread again." "I will have your eyes." Sufferers wake in the night having sworn in their sleep and lie rigid, trying to remember what they said, while the one who shares their bed lies equally rigid, trying not to have heard it.

The Appetite-struck must be gagged for their own safety, and in Oathen this is no metaphor. The iron bit of the Appetite is a heavy thing, hinged at the jaw, with a flat plate that presses the tongue down and a small grille for breath and broth. Families keep their swearers at home, bitted, hands bound when the urge is strong, because a determined swearer will try to sign or scratch vows into the plaster. The Murmuring House in Tacit holds three hundred of them, in rows, and the sound the place makes, three hundred people trying to promise through iron, is where it gets its name.

What the Appetite wants is not any particular promise. It wants the *weight*. Sufferers describe the urge as a hunger with no object, a pressure that eases only when something heavy has been said and witnessed, and the vows that escape them grow heavier as the Appetite deepens: from "I'll fetch the water" to "I will never leave this room" to "I will give my children to the god." A swearer who gets that last one out before the bit goes back is a family tragedy, and the Bench has no clause for undoing it. The parents of the Murmuring Tier tell their children never to stand in front of a swearer whose bit is off for washing. The children do not need to be told why.

### The Course

At the Course, speech stops needing to be a promise to bind. Plain statements begin to come true. "The door is shut," said of an open door, shuts it, or shuts something. "It's cold," said on a warm day, brings frost to the speaker's lashes and nobody else's. Figures of speech become fatal: a man who said he was starving was dead by evening, in a full pantry, his stomach shrunk to the size of a walnut. A Tongue-line girl who told her sister "you look like death" was a mourner within the week.

The body changes with it. The tongue thickens, darkens and lengthens until it rests against the bit even in repose, mottled the color of raw liver, and grows heavy and cool and faintly ridged, like the god's own. A second voice develops beneath the first, a half-step lower, and sometimes it says the words a moment before the speaker does. The small ears come: puckers in the skin of the neck and shoulders and the inner arm, at first like scars from a pox, then opening, pink and folded and perfectly formed, turning toward sound. They weep the clear fluid when someone near them makes a vow. Course-sufferers cannot bear crowds. Too much is said in them, and all of it is heard.

[pull] My husband has an ear on the inside of his wrist now. When I take his hand, it turns toward my mouth. | — a Tongue-line wife of the Heights, to her Cutwright

### The Brink

At the Brink, the gap between saying and being narrows to nothing. A Brink-sufferer's ordinary speech reshapes the room they stand in: a careless "of course" makes a thing inevitable; a sigh of "everything's falling apart" has, on record, brought down a house. The bit is no longer optional. Brink Tongue-lines are taken from their families by the Bench and fitted with silver bits, and their hands are kept gloved and occupied, and they are not permitted pen or chisel. They go mad, often, because a mind full of things it may not say is a mind with no door out, and in Oathen a Break at the Brink is a public danger: a madman whose delusions are binding.

The Bench's files on the Brink-mad make terrible reading. A Vanthe cousin who became convinced that her family had been replaced by strangers wearing their faces, and who said so, once, at breakfast, before her bit was refitted: the family has not been the same since, and the servants say they eat differently. A Sarrow uncle who believed he had died in the night and was being kept alive by a clerical error, and who said, "I am dead," and was correct by dinner, though he went on walking about the house for a day afterward because nobody had told his legs. An Esk heir who decided his own hands belonged to someone else, and said whose, and the man he named, a bridge-rigger on the Shadeward who had never met him, woke with two left hands.

#### The Stopping

[pull] We keep a stone by the door of his room. Not for the door. | — the mother of a Brink-mad son, to a Keener

There is a thing the families of the Brink-mad do, and the Bench has a ruling for it, and nobody speaks of either aloud. When a Brink-sufferer slips the bit and begins a sentence that would end in ruin (a curse on a city, a wish for a death, *I wish you had never been born*), a kinsman in the room is permitted by the Ruling on Interrupted Speech of 451 to stop the sentence before it ends, by any means. The means are always the same: the speaker must stop breathing before the last word. It is called the Stopping. The ruling requires that the act be done by blood kin, so that no stranger carries it, and that the kinsman swear afterward on the dead one's bones, before a Keener, *the sentence was grave*. If it was not grave, the oath breaks them.

Oathen does not count the Stopped as murdered. Their families count them. In the Witnessing Vaults the Stopped stand in their own gallery, faces outward, each with a slate at the foot of the niche on which the half-sentence they did not finish has been chalked by the Keeners, word for word, as far as it went. *The canyon will.* *Mother, I see you are.* *Let every child in Tacit.* *I wish you would all.* The Keeners rechalk them every Tablenight. Nobody knows who first insisted the half-sentences be kept, and nobody has ever proposed rubbing them out, and the families who come down to that gallery come alone, and stand longer than they stand anywhere else, and do not read the slates.

In 588, at the height of the Esk fever, Tobin Esk stopped his father in the library of the Esk house on the Sunward Wall. The old man had said "I wish you'd never been", and Tobin had a pillow, and then had nothing in his hands at all. He swore on the bones that the sentence was grave, and did not break, and the Bench sealed the matter. He went to the Quiet the following spring and took the knife, and lived on Hollin's Stair for thirty years, and never once in that time was seen to sleep lying down. The current heir of House Esk, Lord Varro, is his great-nephew, and carries a pillow-stone on his watch-chain: a smooth red pebble, said to be the one the family kept by the old man's door, in case.

### The Seating: the Sayers

A Tongue-line in the last stage does not describe the world. What they say, is. They are called the Sayers, and they are the closest thing the canyons have to Tolm walking again: not the god's ear but its mouth, the place where its witness turns into its word. The first was a girl in the Hushwell house who said "I wish it would stop" during a storm in 598 A.G. It stopped. So did the wind, the river, the birds, and the hearts of everything in the valley of Low Shale, which is still there, intact, with nothing in it moving.

The few Sayers alive are kept bitted in gold, which is the one metal Tolm's mouth will not bite through, and locked in the Gilded Cells beneath the Ledger. They are unmuzzled rarely, on the Bench's vote, to say a single sentence the Clausewrights have drafted for months. The Bench calls this governance. The Sayers do not call it anything. Their faces, behind the gold, have grown long and smooth, and their eyes have closed over with skin, and the small ears have covered them like moss.

Three cells are occupied. In the first, since 598, is the First Sayer herself, the Hushwell girl of Low Shale, now an old woman whose face no one living has seen without its gold; the Bench has not unbitted her in forty-three years, because she has never yet been drafted a sentence anyone trusted her with, and because in the first year she wept through the grille so constantly that her keepers feared what the weeping might mean. In the second is the Sayer of 611, whose name the Bench struck from the Ledger after the night of the copper bit, and who has not been unmuzzled since that one word in the dark. In the third, since 638, is Lady Imre Dath, who is unbitted once a year, and who has begun to write.

[box: The Sayer's Gold]
A Sayer's bit is a work of terrible craft: soft gold over a core of lead, cast to the exact shape of the mouth, with a tongue-plate that has to be resized every few months as the tongue grows. The Bitmaker Garl Tome fits them himself. The gold must be pure. A bit cut with copper was found, in 611 A.G., to have been bitten clean through overnight, and the Sayer it held had said one word into the dark of the Cells before the guards reached her. No one knows what the word was. The Gilded Cells were walled off for six weeks afterward, and the guard who came out first had no ears, and smiled.
[/box]

[pull] They are not prisoners. Prisoners have a sentence. These have only ours. | — Brother Ansgar Mote of the Quiet, on a slate left at the Ledger Foot

## Land and Weather

[sigil: oathen]

Oathen is dry red canyon country, deep and narrow, under a hard white sky. The plateau above is scrub and scoured stone; the life is below, in the cracks. Rivers run in the canyon floors, thin and green and fast, and the towns are cut into the cliff walls in tiers: dwellings, granaries, shrines and tombs, connected by ladders, rope-lifts and narrow stairs that wind up the rock like the thread of a screw. It is hot, still and extremely quiet. Sandals are soled with felt. Doors are hung on leather. Cart wheels are wrapped in rag. You can stand on a balcony in Tacit and hear a woman across the canyon draw water.

The heat is a dry heat that cracks lips and shrinks leather, and the canyon walls hold it into the night, so that a hand laid on the rock at midnight finds it warm as a sleeping animal. The colours are red, ochre, rust and bone, with the thin green stitch of the rivers at the bottom and, in the low valleys around Low Dray, the dusty grey-green of fig orchards. The smells are hot stone, goat, dung-smoke, cedar oil, and, on the canyon floors, the cool mineral breath of the rivers. The light at noon falls straight down the canyons like water poured into a well, and the people of the lower tiers see it for two hours a day and spend the rest of their lives in a red half-dusk.

The great danger, as has been said, is echo (see the Rule of the Land), and the canyon country is a country of mouths. Every bend is a throat. Every overhang is a palate. Travellers who come down from the Rim Road and hear, for the first time, their own cough come back off a wall a quarter-mile away, then again, then again, fainter, say afterward that it was the moment they understood they were inside something.

It rains once a year. Before it died, Tolm promised the canyons rain on the first day of Grace, and a god's word outlives the god. The Kept Rain has fallen on that day for six hundred and forty years: a single day's downpour, warm and heavy, that fills the cisterns, floods the canyon floors, and drowns the careless. The whole country lives on that day's water. The rain smells, those who have stood in it say, of wet slate and of skin, and it is warm as blood when it first hits, and the canyons roar with it, the only day of the year when Oathen is loud, every gorge a cataract and every wall throwing back the thunder of water, so that lovers make their vows in it and know the god cannot pick their words out of the noise. This year it came three days late.

### Places in Oathen

#### Tacit

The capital, carved into both walls of the deepest canyon. Half a million people, and you can hear a sandal drop. The Sunward and Shadeward Walls face each other across two hundred rope bridges; the Tongue-lines live high on the Sunward side, and the Bench sits at its heart in the Hall of Conditions, a felt-lined chamber floored with sand so that nothing said there can echo. Markets run on handtalk and tally-sticks. The city smells of hot stone, goat, and the cedar oil the rich rub into their bits. (*Seven Tables* walks Tacit gallery by gallery, from the Heights to the Murmuring Tier.)

#### The Breakdowns

The slum of the Forsworn, on the canyon floor below Tacit, where the river runs green and the sun reaches for two hours a day. Here live the broken: men without hands, women with mouths opened back to the ear, people walking on legs that bend the wrong way, people whose ribs stand open under oiled cloth. It is the loudest place in Oathen, because the people in it have nothing left to lose. They swear constantly, defiantly, on broken parts that cannot break again. They sing. They mock the Walls overhead. Their lanes are named for the oaths their founders broke: Fair Weight Row, Back-Before-Dark, the Never Alley, I'll-Stand-By-You, Till-Death Court.

[pull] LIES TOLD — 3 L. UNDER OATH IN A COURT — 10 L. LOVE, SWORN — 2 L, AND YOU BUY THE DRINK. NO CHILDREN. NO DEBTS. — chalked on a board at the corner of Honest Measure

#### Sworn Gorge

A sealed ravine east of Tacit where an oath shouted in 419 A.G. has never stopped echoing. Anyone who hears it is bound by it. Nobody knows any longer what it says. The mouth of the gorge is walled with dressed stone forty feet high, and the wall is packed behind with wool, and still, on the stillest nights, a man pressing his ear to it can hear a murmur in the rock like a crowd a mile off, rising and falling. The Echo-Wardens keep a post at the wall, beside the dead hamlet of Sowe. They wear wax and lead plugs in their ears and communicate by touch, and they rotate every nine days, because the murmur gets into you anyway.

#### The Ledger

A cliff face four miles long into which every vow of public record has been carved: treaties, marriages, sentences, Rim Road contracts, the oaths of kings. The oldest carvings at its western end are worn nearly smooth; the newest, at the eastern end, are still pale with fresh dust. Scaffolds hang from the cliff top, and the carvers work on them in all weathers, in silence, at a lack a letter. Behind the face, inside the rock, lies a warren of record-rooms and copying halls, and below that, the Ninth Room, where executions are conducted, and below that, the Gilded Cells.

#### The Gilded Cells

Where the Sayers are kept: a vault of nine chambers cut deep under the Ledger and lined in beaten lead and felt, with doors of gold-faced iron. Three of the cells are occupied. The guards are bitted and deafened by surgery. A Clausewright of the Bench visits once a week to inspect the bits and does not stay long. The air is close and warm and smells of lead, broth and something sweeter beneath, like the inside of a mouth.

#### The Gullet

The gorge where Tolm was eaten, at the head of the river system west of Tacit. Its floor is deep red sand over the god's bones, and it is the quietest place in Oathen: sound does not carry here, but sinks, as if into a pillow. Pilgrims walk the Gullet barefoot on Tablenight eve. Those who put their ear to the sand hear, they say, a slow sound like breathing; the Bench rules that what they hear is the river. The sand is hot all year, and it is getting warmer. The Quiet of Hollin's Stair look down on it from their cells and say that on still nights the whole floor of the Gullet rises and falls, very slightly, like a sleeper's blanket.

#### The Unmade Road

A switchbacked track down from Wending Pass, in the high eastern canyons, where Captain Renn Hold's regiment came apart in 627 A.G. Four hundred soldiers swore to hold the pass to the death, obeyed an order to retreat, and broke on the road behind their captain. Their remains were never cleared. The bone along the road is still sorted, roughly, into the shapes of men lying where they fell, and the drovers who use the road walk their animals in single file down the center of it. Renn Hold is still at the top of the pass. He has to be.

#### The Murmuring House

A hospital and asylum on the Shadeward Wall of Tacit for the Appetite-struck and the Brink-mad who have no family to keep them. Three hundred patients, bitted in iron, in long low wards. The nurses work with wax in their ears and feed their charges thin broth through the grille of the bit. The sound of the place is a ceaseless, thick, wet humming: three hundred vows attempted and not finished. On Tablenight, when the patients fall silent with the rest of the country, the nurses say the quiet is so heavy they can hear the patients' hearts.

#### The Witnessing Vaults

The catacombs beneath Tacit, where the dead stand upright in niches, faces outward, to witness. In the dry air they do not rot so much as cure, the lips drawing back from the teeth so that every face wears the same look of having just heard something. Families come here to swear on the dead, and the Vaults' keepers, the Keeners, are the only people in Oathen who have heard the dead speak (see Oaths on the Dead).

#### Quillhouse

The Rim Road Company's notarial house on the Oathen stretch of the Rim, three days' ride north of Tacit. A fortress of pale stone, gated, garrisoned by Company guards, and home to Jessamy Quill. Every Company contract on the Table is carried here by courier, read aloud before her, and sworn. The waiting-hall is always full: Fatlands renderers, Brinehollow admirals' factors, Vigil oil-buyers, all made to sit in silence in a long felted room while an Oathen clerk explains, in handtalk and on slate, that anything they say from here on counts.

#### Low Shale

The valley the First Sayer stopped in 598 A.G. Everything in it stopped: water standing in the riverbed in a single hard green ribbon that will not run if you cup it, birds hanging in the air a foot above the ground, a herdsman mid-stride with his crook raised, his eyes wet and open after forty-three years. Nothing rots. Nothing moves. The air is warm and has no smell at all. The Bench has posted it as silent and forbids entry. Pilgrims of the Bait doctrine sneak in to leave written prayers at the herdsman's feet. The herdsman's eyes have been seen to follow them.

#### Hollow Cistern and the High Basin

At Highwell, above the Gullet, where the Rain-keepers ate the god's heart, the Rain Shrine stands at the centre of a bowl of red rock ringed with cisterns: six hundred and forty standing stones, each carved with a year and its rainfall. Every household's cistern-share is sworn here or at a share-stone on the first day of Grace. This year a new stone stands outside the ring, uncarved, the height of a young woman. The basin has not yet agreed what to write on it (see Oathen Now).

#### Knot Canyon, Sheathmouth and the Small Places

South of Tacit lies **Knot Canyon**, a labyrinth of slot-canyons where sound gets lost, home to smugglers, unlicensed Sureties and Grandam Cess. Downstream of the **Sheath**, the fourteen-mile narrows where an echo runs nine times, the guides of **Sheathmouth** rope travellers together and lead them through gagged. **Low Dray**, two days down the Hushwater, is the gentlest town in the country, all fig orchards and fostering-houses. **Treaty Stair** is where the Rim Road comes down off the plateau and every foreigner is taught, by slate, to shut up. **Hollin's Stair**, on the upper Gullet, is the vertical monastery of the Quiet. (*Seven Tables* describes them all.)

#### The Stilled Gallery

A short gallery on the eleventh tier of the Shadeward Wall, forty doors long, sealed at both ends with felt curtains since the Lack of 640. It was called Corder's Gallery once, after the rope-makers who lived there. Nobody lives there now. What happened in it is told under Oathen Now.

### The Honest Age

For two centuries after the Supper, Oathen was the conscience of the Table. Other lands discovered they had swallowed monstrous gifts; Oathen discovered it had swallowed honesty, and sold it. A treaty sworn in Tacit could not be broken without the signatories' bodies breaking too; a merchant house that swore its weights at the Ledger could be trusted to the crumb. Oathen grew rich on notarizing the world. The canyon cities filled with foreign envoys learning to keep quiet.

It was not a kind age. Children broke constantly on promises they did not understand, and the cemeteries of the period are full of small, oddly shaped bones. In 215 A.G. the Bench passed the Muzzling, and the canyons have been quiet in a new way since: the silence of the nursery.

[pull] The bones in the children's galleries of the old Vaults are not arranged by family. They are arranged by what broke. Nobody alive remembers why. Everybody who sees it understands. | — Old Sabra Wends, Eldest Keener

### The Mute Succession

The old kings spoke, and every word bound them. By 330 A.G. King Orrel the Fourth had sworn so many coronation oaths and treaty-clauses that he ruled his last years from a litter, his body folded into what his physicians called an exact map of his reign's contradictions: one arm bent to the shape of the Brinehollow treaty, one to the Vigil's, his spine twisted between them, his jaw locked half-open on a promise of mercy he had broken in 322. Thereafter the crown passed only to children who had never spoken. Nine such kings have reigned, and all died with their silence intact.

### The Vanthe Oath

The Vanthes ate the tip of Tolm's tongue, the part that touches the teeth, and they have always been the quickest-spoken and the soonest-broken of the high houses. In 312 A.G., when a Vanthe daughter married into the Esks and the Cutwrights reported that her children had the stronger godsign of the two houses, the Vanthe matriarch, Lady Orsa, went down to the Witnessing Vaults with her whole house behind her, laid her hand on the bones of her husband, and swore, at Weight 5, on the dead: *The tongue shall not leave this house.*

The Bench has argued ever since about whether a living woman can bind her unborn descendants. The Vanthes do not argue. Every Vanthe who has tried to marry outside the house since 312 has broken at the mouth on the wedding morning, the tongue splitting to its root, and the Testimony of Lady Orsa's husband has been heard from his niche in the Vaults eleven times, each time saying the oath in a dead man's voice, loud enough to be heard on the Sunward Wall. So the Vanthes marry Vanthes. They have married cousins, and then the children of cousins, and then, the Ledger's register of marriages implies in a column nobody reads aloud, nearer than cousins. One entry, for 589, records the marriage of Lady Ammet Vanthe to Corvel Vanthe, and beside it, in the Cutwrights' small grey marginal hand, pressed so hard the nib went through the page, two words: *same mother*.

The Vanthes are beautiful in the same way, all of them: narrow, very pale, with the house's grey eyes and the house's habit of tilting the head when listening. Their children reach the Taste in the cradle. Many are born with their mouths sealed over with a membrane of skin and must be cut open before they can feed, and the house physician does it in silence with a silver knife and stitches nothing, because the god's tongue must have its door. Some of the sealed ones do not take the knife well. The Vanthe children's gallery in the Vaults is long. The house does not receive guests for supper, and its servants are hired from Sheathmouth, where people are used to not saying what they have seen.

[pull] You never ask a Vanthe who their father is. Not because it's rude. Because they'd have to think about it. | — overheard on the Tenth Bridge, in handtalk, and the hands were broken a month later; nobody knows if it was the god or the house

### The Oath in Sworn Gorge

In the drought summer of 419 A.G., something was shouted in a narrow ravine east of Tacit, and the ravine has never stopped saying it. The records of that year are incomplete: a page is cut from the Bench's minutes and a stretch of the Ledger has been chiselled smooth, very carefully, in a way that took weeks. What is known is that the people of the hamlet of Sowe, at the gorge's mouth, were all found broken the following spring, every one of them, in the same way, and that the shape of the Breaking was so terrible that the Echo-Wardens' charter forbids its description. The gorge was walled. The murmur goes on.

### The Forsworn Riots

By 506 the Breakdowns held forty thousand broken people, and the Bench had passed, in that year, the Ruling of Visible Marks, which required every Forsworn to bare their broken part in any public place above the third tier, so that no whole Oathener could be deceived by them. The broken came up the stairs. They came on crutches and wheeled boards and backward-bending legs, singing their oaths, and they swore on broken ground that they would burn the Galleries, which they could swear without cost, and the Watch, who could not swear anything that was not in their oath, could not answer in kind. Then they cut the bridges. Nine of the lowest spans went down into the river in a night, with people on them. The Bench rescinded the Ruling of Visible Marks by noon, in a sentence drafted in under an hour, which no one living had seen done. No Bench collector has gone down into the Breakdowns to ask for a tax since.

### Wending Pass

In 627 A.G. Reckoners out of the Fast came through the eastern canyons, and the four hundred soldiers of the Fourth Regiment of the Canyon Watch swore, in the old form, to hold Wending Pass to the death. Captain Renn Hold looked at the raiders and at his men, most of them nineteen, and ordered them to fall back. He kept his own oath by staying. They broke theirs by obeying. The sound came down the road like a rockfall, four hundred Breakings in a breath, each soldier opening at every joint by which he had bound himself to the place. They did not die of it, not at once. The Reckoners found the captain alone at the top with his sword drawn, looked at the road below him, and went back the way they had come.

[bigquote] I ordered them down because I loved them. I have had fourteen years to work out whether that was an oath. | — Captain Renn Hold, to a drover, at the top of Wending Pass

## Rule, Law and Politics

[sigil: oathen]

Oathen is ruled by the Mute Kings, chosen in infancy, bitted for life, and raised to rule by gesture. The present king, Ket the Ninth, has never made a sound. Beneath him sits the Bench of Clauses, forty-one lawyer-priests called Clausewrights, whose art is the drafting of promises that can survive contact with reality. In theory the Bench interprets the king. In practice the king nods, and the Bench spends a month in forty-page conditionals deciding what the nod meant, and then does what it would have done anyway.

There are no prisons. There is no need. A convicted person is made to swear the terms of their own sentence: "I will not leave the district of Low Dray for nine years." "I will pay the widow one lack a day until my death." "I will not speak again." Breach the sentence and the body enforces it. The Bench's chief tool of government is therefore not the sword but the clause, and its great fear is the sentence badly drafted, which leaves a convict free, or the sentence too well drafted, which kills him in a way the court did not intend. For the gravest crimes there is the Breaker, Jude Crake, who carries out death sentences with nothing but conversation.

Trials are simple. In a Breaking trial, presided over by Judge Thane Urrow, there is no evidence and no testimony. The accused repeats the promise they are alleged to have broken, aloud, in its original words, and the court watches. If they kept it, nothing happens. If they broke it, it breaks them again, now, in front of everyone. For crimes that are not oaths (theft, murder, arson), the accused is asked a single question: "Did you do it?" and must answer. A Forsworn who has nothing left to break in the mouth is a problem the Bench has never solved, which is why the Breakdowns are, to Tacit's lasting horror, the one place in Oathen where a person can lie.

Oathen's power abroad is trust. Every other government sends its treaties east to be made real, and the Rim Road Company swears every contract before its notary, Jessamy Quill. Oathen charges a tithe on every sworn page, and has never needed an army larger than its notaries. It has one anyway, the Canyon Watch, small and very well disciplined, because a soldier who has sworn to obey cannot do otherwise. Since Wending Pass, the Watch's oath has been redrafted. It now runs to eleven pages, and the phrase "to the death" has been removed.

### The Ninefold

In the upper gorges, where the ear-eating clans live and the Bench's writ runs thin, there is an older justice, and it is a ritual killing. The Bench outlawed it in 377. The gorges did not notice.

When a kinsman kills a kinsman in the upper gorges and will not answer the question, the clan takes him to a narrows that throws back nine (every upper-gorge clan has one, and knows its count) and stands him in the place where the echo is cleanest. The dead one's nearest kin stand above, on the rim. They call down to him the question, *did you do it*, and he must answer, and whatever he answers comes back nine times. If he says *yes*, the clan lets him live, sworn to exile on the bones. If he says *no*, and it is a lie, his mouth breaks, and then breaks again, and again, nine times, each Breaking landing on the last, and by the ninth there is no jaw, no tongue, no lips, and the throat has split down to the breastbone, and the clan on the rim watches in silence until the canyon is quiet. Then they go down and bring him up and lay him in the dead one's niche, facing in. If he says nothing, they wait. The longest anyone has ever stood silent in a Ninefold narrows is four days. The clan stood on the rim for all of them.

The Ninefold is not cruel by the gorges' reckoning. Nobody lays a hand on the accused. The god does everything. That is what the clans say, and the Bench, whose own Breaker sits down with the condemned and talks until they promise something, has never found a reply that it could make without hearing itself.

[pull] Down here we don't kill a man. We ask him a question, and let the walls ask it again. | — an elder of the Tallow-clan, upper Gullet, to a Bench inspector who did not return to Tacit with his tongue

## Culture and Daily Life

[sigil: oathen]

### Speech and Silence

- Handtalk. A sign language used for everything that matters little: prices, directions, the weather, gossip, jokes, flirtation. It is held not to bind. That belief is being tested, and fingers have started breaking. Oatheners sign with the left hand where they can, because it is the hand they can spare.
- The bit. Children wear a soft leather muzzle until twelve, decorated by their parents with beads, ribbons and family marks. Having it removed is the coming of age, the Unbitting, and the first words spoken afterward are chosen with a Clausewright present. They are usually a family motto, or "I am here," which is safe.
- Conditional speech. "If able." "As I now intend." "Barring death, illness, or weather." A polite sentence is mostly exits.
- Insults. The worst thing you can call someone is "talkative." The second worst is "certain."
- Proverbs. Oathen proverbs are short and verbless where possible: "Fewer words, more fingers." "A full mouth, an empty hand." "The canyon keeps the change." "Listen twice." "Say it to the dead, pay it to the living." "Better bitted than broken."

### Food, Drink and Dress

Oatheners eat goat, canyon lizard, flatbread baked on hot stones, dried figs, and a sour green cheese aged in the river caves. Water is life and law: every household's cistern-share is sworn yearly at the Rain Shrine, and stealing water is the only theft the Bench treats as a crime against the god. Meals are eaten in silence; the grace "Lack keep away" is signed, not said, unless there are foreigners at the table, in which case it is said very slowly with three exits attached. Wine is rare and feared, because drunkenness makes promises.

Dress is plain and close-fitting, in dust-red and ochre wool, with long sleeves to hide what broke. The Tongue-lines wear high collars and veils across the mouth, white for the unwed, black for those whose vows are many. Rings are worn on the thumbs as reminders of oaths outstanding: a ring per vow, so that a man's hands tell you how much he owes. The Forsworn go bare where they broke, as a point of pride.

### Marriage and Kin

Marriage vows are negotiated for months and run to forty pages, covering fidelity, property, illness, absence, the care of each other's dead, and what counts as "cherishing." Divorce does not exist: a marriage vow cannot be unsworn, only broken, and its Breaking is the chest opened to the air. What the faithless do, and what the vows forbid in their thicket of clauses, the canyon decently does not discuss aloud. The Tongue-lines marry cousin to cousin to keep the god's tongue in the family, and some old houses have married so close for so long that their children are born with mouths sealed over with skin, and must be cut open before they can feed.

### Children

Children are bitted from the first tooth until twelve. They grow up in handtalk, which they speak faster and more inventively than adults, with a whole private slang of nursery signs. The bit is soft and does not hurt, but it shapes the face: Oathen adults have a groove worn at the corners of the mouth, and some of the old bits, family heirlooms, have been worn by six generations of children and taste of all of them. Every parent in the country carries one fear above all others: that their child will say something before twelve, in the instant the bit is off for cleaning. Some do. The Bench records the Breakings of children only by number. The number this year is higher than last.

The bitted have games of their own: the counting game of the ear and the knife; a game called *Closed Hand* in which the loser is the first to answer a question; and a cruel one, played in the lanes of the Galleries, in which one child signs a promise very slowly to another, and everyone watches to see if the fingers will break. It used to be a game about nothing. This year it is a game of courage, and mothers are beating it out of their children with the flat of their hands, silently, in doorways.

### Death and the Dead

The dead are entombed upright in the Witnessing Vaults, faces outward, so that they may witness the oaths of the living. Before a body is set in its niche, the family gathers and speaks every promise they made to the dead person in life, and the Keener asks the corpse, formally, whether each was kept. The dead do not answer. Usually. A death in Oathen also releases the dead person's oaths, which is why heirs sometimes wait at a bedside very patiently, and why the Bench has drafted a clause, standard in all wills, binding the heirs to have hoped for recovery.

Oaths sworn on the dead are the heaviest in the land: a hand on the bones, the dead one's name, the vow said into the niche. They are used for blood feuds, for marriages across feuding families, for debts too large for any Surety. When such an oath breaks, the dead person answers for Tolm, and what the dead do then is the stuff of every Oathen ghost story, and all of them are true.

#### The Desecrators

Where there is an oath on the dead, there is someone who wants the dead gone. The trade is old and the Keeners hate it above all others: **bone-lifting**, the theft of a witness from its niche, by an oathbreaker who hopes that a dead one who is no longer in the Vaults can no longer Testify. It does not work. Bones lifted from the Vaults Testify from wherever they are taken: from the bottom of the Hushwater, from a lime-pit in Knot Canyon, from under a hearthstone in the Galleries, where in 602 a family sat at supper above the grandmother they had sworn on and heard her say their oath up through the floor. The Keeners know this. The desperate do not, or will not believe it.

The worst of the desperate go further. There is a belief, old in the upper gorges and spreading in the Breakdowns, that a witness swallowed cannot speak: that an oathbreaker who grinds the bones of the dead they swore on into flour and bakes it into bread and eats it, alone, at night, in silence, has taken the witness inside, where the god cannot hear it. The Keeners call the practice *eating the witness*. They know of nineteen cases since 500. In every one, the dead Testified anyway, from inside: their voice coming up out of the oathbreaker's own belly, muffled and wet, speaking the oath aloud in a crowded place, while the oathbreaker clutched at their stomach and the people around them backed away. One of the nineteen, a cloth-merchant of the Sunward Wall, tried to stop the voice with a knife, from the outside, and the Keeners were sent for, and Old Sabra Wends will not describe what she was asked to put in the niche afterward.

[pull] We tell the families, the dead will speak from wherever you put them. If you put them in your mouth, that is where they will speak from. | — a Keener of the Witnessing Vaults

The Keeners also guard the widowers' galleries, where the dead of marriages are set facing each other across a narrow aisle, and where, once in a generation, a niche is found empty, and the empty niche belongs to a wife whose husband swore her a vow of the bed, and was a very literal man. The Keeners go up to the house with lamps. What they find there is never entered in any record, and the Bench's ruling of 503, that a vow of the bed ends at death, was drafted in a single afternoon after one such visit, and is read aloud now at every Tongue-line wedding.

### Festivals

- The Kept Rain (first day of Grace). The whole country stands in the open, faces up, mouths bitted, and the rain comes. Children take their first swim. Lovers make the year's vows in the downpour, when the noise of it hides the words from the canyon. This year, for three days, the whole country stood and waited, and the sky stayed white.
- The Unbitting (Plenty). Twelve-year-olds have their bits removed in public, in the Hall of Conditions, and speak their first chosen words. Families weep, silently.
- The Carving of the Year (Carving). The new vows of public record are cut into the Ledger. Carvers work through the night by lamplight, and the town watches from the canyon floor.
- The Night of Ears (the eve of Tablenight). Pilgrims walk the Gullet barefoot and lay their ears to the warm sand.
- Tablenight (longest night of Lack). Twenty-four hours in which no one in the country makes a sound. Shops close. Bells are tied. Babies are nursed constantly. It is the only day Oatheners feel safe, and the happiest day of the year. Couples who have not touched in years hold hands in the street. Enemies sit at one table. Nothing can be promised, so nothing can be broken, and for one day, the canyons say, everybody is whole.

### Crime and Punishment

Every crime in Oathen is either an oath broken or a question waiting to be asked: a convict swears a sentence, a debtor a schedule, and a murderer, if the crime is grave, receives Jude Crake. Blasphemy, the cursing of Tolm aloud, is not punished by the Bench at all; it is merely recorded, as Tolm will see to it. Of forty-one blasphemers entered in the record since the Muzzling, thirty-nine were found the next morning with their tongues gone, not cut but missing, as if never grown, and their mouths full of fine red sand. The other two were Sayers. No one has entered their sentences in the record.

### The Bench of Clauses

The Clausewrights: rulers in practice, and the finest contract lawyers who have ever lived. They train for twenty years in the drafting of vows, in the reading of Tolm's past judgments on the Ledger, and in the art of speaking a whole day without committing to anything. ## Oathen Now

[sigil: oathen]

The rain was late, which means a god's promise has slipped, which should not be possible. Handtalkers are starting to break. A scribe has found evidence that written words now bind. If the Regrowth is widening what counts as speech, soon there will be nothing an Oathener can safely say, sign, write, or perhaps think. And the Bench is debating, in forty-page conditionals, whether to take the bit out of a Sayer and have her fix it.

Meanwhile the Forsworn are counting. The Breakdowns have doubled in five years, swollen by handtalkers with broken fingers and scribes with split palms, and Brakk now speaks for a fifth of the capital. The Company has tripled the guard on Quillhouse. Lady Imre Dath, in her gold, is writing, and no one is quite sure on what.

### The Girl at the Stone

On the first day of Grace in 641, the high basin of Highwell stood bitted and faced the sky, and the sky stayed white. On the second day it stayed white. An old man died on his feet and his sons held him upright. On the second night, in the Rain Shrine, by lamplight, the elders of the eleven Rain-keeper lines met without their Rainwarden, because Yusra Thole had stopped speaking and would not sign to them about anything but the cisterns. They talked. They had a Lack-era rite in the oldest of the Tally-books, from before the Gorging, when the canyon had tried everything to make the god's ears open again: the **Heart's Due**. When the rain will not come, the rite says, the god's heart wants a heart.

Before the lot was drawn, every unwed Heart-line adult of the basin between eighteen and thirty, forty-one of them, was made to swear at the Shrine, at Weight 4, before the elders: *If the lot is mine, I will go to the stone.* It was the old form. Nobody refused, because refusing in front of the whole basin, with the cisterns cracking, was a thing nobody there could do. The lot fell to Hallie Corr, twenty-two, a shepherd, Heart-line on her mother's side, whose pulse was so slow that her friends used to lay their fingers on her throat as a game, to count how long they had to wait.

She went to the stone at dawn on the third day because she had sworn to. The elders did it with the bronze knife of the Shrine, in silence, as the rite required, and laid her heart in the dry bed of Hollow Cistern, and stood back. Yusra Thole stood at the edge of the ring through all of it, and watched, and did not move, and did not sign. Six hours later, the first cloud came over the basin rim. By noon it was raining as it had always rained, warm and heavy, the canyons roaring, the cisterns filling, the water running red and then clear out of Hollow Cistern, and the whole of Highwell knelt in the flood and wept with relief and gratitude, and the elders said to each other in handtalk, *she brought it*.

She did not. Yusra Thole knows, from the Tally and from her own slowing pulse, that the rain was never late; that Tolm's year has grown three days longer; that the rain was coming on that third day whether a girl went to the stone or not, and to the hour. The sacrifice was demanded and given, and it bought nothing. And Yusra cannot say so, because to say so is to say what the god's longer year means, and she has not been able to make herself say that to anyone. So she says nothing, and the basin has raised a new uncarved stone outside the ring, the height of a young woman, and wants it carved *Hallie Corr, who brought the rain*, and the elders have begun, quietly, drafting the oath for next year's lot. The Heart's Due is in the Tally again, after six hundred and forty years. If nobody tells Highwell the truth, it will be in it every year the rain is late, and the rain will be late every year from now on, a little later each time.

[pull] Her mother has gone up to sit by the stone every day since. She doesn't say anything. Nobody up there says anything now. You'd think they'd be happy. They got their rain. | — Ash Merrow, Rain-Deputy of Highwell, to a stranger

### The Stilling of Corder's Gallery

[pull] It wasn't a curse. That's what I want you to understand. Nobody did anything to them. They just stopped. | — Dacey Furl, night-sister of the Murmuring House

Corder's Gallery was forty doors on the eleventh tier of the Shadeward, rope-makers and their families, poor and close and kind. In the Grace of 640, a rope-maker named Isk Corder, at the Course, woke in the night and said to his wife, in the dark, half asleep: "I can't say anything any more without it being true." It was true. It had been true for a month; the Taste and the Appetite had run through him fast that year, as they were running fast through everyone. But now he had *said* it, at the Course, and it was more true than before, and in the morning he found that he could not say anything at all, about anything, that did not immediately happen.

He stopped speaking. That was sensible. His wife stopped speaking too, to keep him company, and because she was afraid that whatever was in him was in her. Their neighbours, who loved them, began to keep a silence around their door, and then in the gallery outside it, and then, because the god was widening that year and everyone in Tacit could feel it, in their own homes. By Plenty the whole gallery spoke only in handtalk. By Carving, after Mirren Osk's fingers broke before four hundred people, it had stopped handtalking. People in Corder's Gallery sat in their doorways and looked at each other and did not move their hands. Children who had been unbitted that spring asked for their bits back. The gallery smelled, neighbours from the tier below said, of unwashed bodies and unemptied pots, because to wash a child you must tell it to lift its arms, and nobody would.

Then they stopped eating. Nobody could say afterward why, because nobody in the gallery had said anything. Dacey Furl, who went up from the Murmuring House with broth when the tier below sent word, thinks it was that eating is a kind of answer: you are offered, you accept, and to accept is to agree to something, and they could no longer agree to anything. She fed some of them by reed, as she feeds her patients. Some let her. Some turned their faces to the wall. Isk Corder sat with his back to his door for eleven days with his eyes open, and his wife beside him with her hand on his wrist, where the small ear was, and on the twelfth morning Dacey found them both still and cold, and the ear on his wrist turned toward his wife's mouth, as if waiting.

Through the Lack of 640 the gallery went one door at a time. Not all of them were the god's doing. Some went out of their doors one night and did not come back. Dacey will not say more than that, and the families on the tier below, who heard nothing, because nobody in Corder's Gallery made a sound even then, say only that the Keeners came for them in the morning, and that the Keeners, too, said nothing. Others were found sitting up. The children were carried down by the Murmuring House and the Quiet, and some of them live, and some of them have not yet said a word, in any hand, to anyone. Of the hundred and sixty people who lived in Corder's Gallery in the Grace of 640, nineteen are alive. The Bench sealed the gallery with felt at both ends and posted it silent, as though it were a gorge. It recorded the matter as *a contagion of discretion*.

It has happened since in two other galleries, on a smaller scale, and once in a village above Low Dray. It begins the same way each time: someone at the Course says, aloud, that they can no longer safely say anything; and those who love them, listening, believe it.

[box: Running the Stilling]
The Stilling is not a monster and should never be played as one. It is grief and fear moving through people who love each other, in a country where the god has made silence the only safe answer. Use it as aftermath: a gallery behind felt curtains; a child at the Murmuring House who will not move her hands; a mother who comes to the PCs because her husband has stopped answering, and wants them to make him talk. If a PC spends a day in a household beginning to still, have them make a Dread 2 check at dusk; on a failure, the player may not have their character speak aloud until they succeed at Caul + Resolve (Hard 14) in a later scene, and if a Break occurs in a stilling household, every witness who shares the delusion shares this silence. The cure, where there is one, is simple and hard: someone the stilled person trusts must speak to them, aloud, plainly, for a long time, and keep every word.
[/box]

### Adventure Hooks

- The Late Rain. Yusra Thole, the Rainwarden, has not spoken in a month, because she cannot lie about the rain and will not tell the truth. The Bench hires the PCs to find out what she knows without asking her a question. The answer lies in the Gullet, where the sand over Tolm's bones has grown hot enough to blister, and something beneath it has begun, very slowly, to clear its throat.
- The Second Lot. Highwell's elders are drafting the oath for next year's Heart's Due, and the forty-one who swore last Grace are being asked to swear again. Hallie Corr's mother hires the PCs to prove the sacrifice bought nothing before the lot is drawn. The proof is in Yusra's Tally, and in her pulse, and the PCs must decide what the basin should be told, and whether they can say it without breaking.
- The Surety's Last Default. Ona Fell can survive one more default, and one of her two hundred and eleven principals is about to break: a Tongue-line merchant who swore a Company contract he cannot meet. The PCs must find which one before it happens, and the merchant has hired them too, to make sure Ona breaks first.
- The Hold at Wending. A Company caravan must cross Wending Pass, and the man at the top will not let anyone through who has not sworn to hold it. Renn Hold is not mad; he is bound. Getting past him means understanding what he swore, and the four hundred on the road below have opinions too.
- Sworn Gorge Has Gone Quiet. For the first time in two hundred years the murmur behind the wall has stopped. Halvar Stane believes the echo has found a way out. Three herders in the eastern canyons have broken in the same way as the hamlet of Sowe, and the PCs must track a word through the rock before it reaches Tacit.
- The Notary's Hand. Someone is trying to make Jessamy Quill break. Agents of the Second Table want the Company's contracts loosed so that the Rim Road falls into chaos before Tablenight; the PCs are hired as her bodyguards, and every conversation in Quillhouse is a battlefield.
- The Bread of the Witness. A Keener of the Vaults has found a niche empty and a trail of bone-dust to a bakery on the Galleries. Somebody in Tacit is eating the witness, and somebody's grandmother is about to Testify from inside a stranger at the Felt Market, on market day, in front of everyone.
- A Vanthe Wedding. House Vanthe has, for the first time since 312, betrothed a daughter outside the house, to an Esk, on the Second Table's instruction. The PCs are hired by the bride to find a reading of Lady Orsa's oath that will let her marry and keep her tongue. The house is hiring someone else to make sure no such reading is found.
