## An Introductory Adventure

*The Ninth Chair* is an introductory adventure for **The Long Grace**, written for three to five new characters of mixed lands and playable in three sessions of four to five hours, with an optional fourth session of aftermath, *After the Snow*. It takes place in the last week of Lack, 641 A.G., at a Rim Road Company waystation called **Thimble Cross**, where the great ring road bends between the warm meat-plains of the Fatlands and the gold, empty edge of the Fast. A storm of something that is not snow strands the characters there with a convoy of Fatlands meat, a houseful of strangers, and a long table that nobody carried in.

It is a siege, a murder mystery, and a famine, all at once, and it ends on the longest night of the year. It is designed to teach the core of the game: the Grace Roll, Dread and Fray, Hunger and Partaking, the way each land's curse travels in the blood of its people, and the first rule every child of the Fast learns, which is that there are invitations you must not accept.

It is also designed to hurt. The adventure's climax is a sacrifice that the community demands, that somebody gives, and that buys nothing at all. Players should leave the table understanding, in their stomachs, what kind of world this is: a world in which the gods are not angry and cannot be bargained with, in which good people do the brave thing and it does not matter, and in which the Host, the only god never eaten, is kind.

[pull] Come in, come in. There's always room. There's always, always room. | — Absalom Thimble, at the door, every evening of his life

[box: Content and Care]
This adventure contains graphic gore and transformation, cannibalism, the desecration of graves, a ritual murder, a killing within a family, an inherited bloodline curse with implied incest between adults, contagious madness, and a suicide. Talk with your players before you begin. Settle your Lines and Veils, and make sure everyone knows how to call the Grace.
The suicide on Day Three is written to be played as grief, never as spectacle: its method is never described and need never be, and the scene is about who finds the body, what was in the note, and what the people left behind begin to say to themselves afterward. Big Oona's walk onto the marbled ground at dusk the same day is the echo of it, the pull of grief made visible, and it is played the same way: slowly, with room for the characters to talk her home.
The Thimble family's bloodline is kept in the corners of the eye. Nothing in this adventure depicts, describes or jokes about it. The ritual killing in the smokehouse is described in the aftermath, not the act. Use the tools your table prefers to step around anything that cuts deeper than the game should. Horror works best among people who trust each other.
[/box]

## The Secret Truth

Read this section before anything else. Nothing in it should reach the players directly. All of it can reach them in pieces.

### Four Things Are Happening at Once

**The Reaping has followed the convoy.** Forty-four barrels of Fatlands meat came up the Rim Road under the seal of Cullmaster Hedley Brawn. Thirteen are notched with a small knife-mark under the stamp, and they hold the harvest of the Dusk Acres: Rootkin, field-grown meat with faces, and, packed in brine among them, the remains of people the Fatlands soil took and then, being a generous field, gave partly back. The soil of the Fatlands is the flesh of Ummer, the Laden, and that meat is still, in a sense the Board would rather not examine, ground. It has carried its weather with it. **Marbling**, the Fatlands' first frost, the whitening of the fat in the soil, has followed the wagons north for six days and settled over Thimble Cross like snow. Where the marbling lies, the ground is Fatlands ground. And on Fatlands ground in the dark of the year, at dusk, people are Reaped.

**The Host has come to dinner.** Orrum, the only god never eaten, has been walking out of the Fast along the Rim Road, laying tables where people are hungry. Thimble Cross is the most perfect invitation it has ever been offered. The inn is starving. The convoy's hunger is the bottomless hunger of the Fatlands. And the family who keep the inn are bound, by a Company charter sworn in Oathen two hundred and thirty-nine years ago, to "turn no traveller away, nor refuse any guest, while the blood of Thimble endures." A house that cannot refuse is a house the Host can enter. On the night after the characters arrive, a table appears in the common room, laid for nine. On Tablenight, the longest night of Lack, the Host will come to sit at its head, and everyone who takes their place will be Seated: alive, smiling, eating from empty plates, never to rise.

**The guests are not innocent.** A poacher means to sell a caged Unfed woman to the Second Table, two of whose nobles are here under false names to measure the Host's table for carving. A Purgation preacher is looking for a high-Cut throat to open. The innkeeper is about to feed his guests his own mother. A Vigil courier on her last rouse is seeing things, and the things are catching. A Cullmaster of the Board is keeping a little book of who the field should take next, and his book is very neat.

**The ninth chair is not for anyone here.** Orrum's newest tables on the Rim Road are always laid with one place more than there are guests to fill it. That place has a chair pushed in square to the table, its back to the lamp, and at Thimble Cross, for the first time anyone has recorded, it has a card. The card bears no name. It bears a single mark in the oldest form of Table-speech, the root word that means both "to worship" and "to eat," with a stroke beneath it that scholars read as "after," or "last," or "late." The people of Thimble Cross will come to believe that the ninth card names someone who must be given to the Host so that the rest may be spared. They will argue about who. They will choose. Someone will sit in the ninth chair. And the Host will come anyway, lift the volunteer gently out of a chair that was never theirs, lay a new place for them, and go on with dinner. The ninth chair was not laid for any guest of Thimble Cross. Welcome Ardent, the Host's steward, sets it at every table himself, and in six hundred years he has never once been told who it is for.

[box: What the Ninth Chair Is (Do Not Say)]
The ninth chair is the place every old carving keeps for the Eighth, the figure chiseled out of the world's history. This adventure never says so, and neither should you. If players connect the empty chair to the eighth place in old carvings, to the stone table under the sea with eight chairs, to the eighth side of Hallowboard that never has a warm handprint beside it, let them. Do not confirm it. Welcome Ardent does not know. The Host, if it knows, does not discuss its guest list. The horror of the climax is that the chair belongs to someone who has not arrived, that everyone at Thimble Cross can feel it waiting, and that nothing they offer fills it.
If a player asks you directly, at the table, who the chair is for, the right answer is the one Ardent gives: *We're expecting one more.* Then ask whether they take sugar.
[/box]

### What the Host Wants

The Host does not want a sacrifice. It has never wanted one. It wants what it has always wanted: for everyone to be fed. The tragedy of Thimble Cross is that the people in it cannot imagine a god that wants nothing from them except their company at dinner, and so they spend seven days working out what it must want instead, and paying it.

Orrum does not compel. It invites. It invites in the handwriting of the dead, with the smell of bread, with warmth in a frozen house, with rest for people who have not rested in years. It is never angry, never cruel, and never satisfied. Every rule in this adventure that seems to punish the characters is really a description of a kindness too large to survive.

[pull] It only wants everyone fed. That's all it has ever wanted. That isn't so terrible, is it? | — Welcome Ardent, asked why he serves

### The Rule of the Host's Table

Three rules govern the table in the common room, and the characters should learn all three the hard way or from Refrain Dole.

- **Eating makes you a guest.** Anyone who eats food from the Host's table, a crumb of the bread or a smear of the butter, must sit at their own place before the next dawn. They make an Invitation check each hour to resist (see below), at Grim 18.
- **Sitting makes you Seated.** Anyone who sits in their own chair is Seated and lost: alive, lucid, smiling, eating air with real pleasure, wasting slowly for years. They can be dragged from the chair only to die, within the hour, of a hunger that has suddenly arrived all at once.
- **Thanks makes it worse.** Anyone who thanks the table, a chair, the bread or the Host, even in passing, even ironically, gains +4 to the Difficulty of their next Invitation check. The Unfed learn this before they learn their letters.

### Invitation Checks at Thimble Cross

The Fast chapter gives rules for **Invitation checks**, which the Unfed make whenever Orrum offers. At Thimble Cross the Host is calling on everyone, and every character makes them, Godeater and Unfed alike: **Caul + Resolve** against the strength of the offer. Unfed characters add their Pangs to the Difficulty as usual and gain Want on a success. Godeaters add their current Hunger above 5 (Hunger 7 adds 2, and so on): the god in them is hungry too, and it would like very much to sit down.

| Offer at Thimble Cross | Difficulty |
| The smell of the bread, passing through the common room | Routine 10 |
| Your own place card, read for the first time | Hard 14 |
| Having eaten from the table: each hour until you sit | Grim 18 |
| A Seated friend calling you by name | Grim 18 |
| Tablenight, the Host present, your chair across the room | Grim 18 |
| Tablenight, the Host present, your chair within reach | Dire 22 |

On a failure, the character takes one step toward their place and rolls again next round at +2 Difficulty. Companions may hold them (opposed Gut + Brawl) or speak the Decline with them, saying "No, thank you" aloud together (the companion's Tongue + Persuade, rolled once, added as a bonus). On a Lack, they sit. On double 1s, they thank the chair first.

[pull] Never thank a chair. Walk into the wind when you smell bread. Stand with your back to the door. Say no three times and never a fourth. That's all of it. That's the whole of our religion, and it's kept us alive six hundred years, and you lot laugh at it. | — Refrain Dole, through the bars

## Background

### Thimble Cross

Thimble Cross stands where the Rim Road, coming north out of the Fatlands, bends hard west to skirt the edge of the Fast. A rutted track, the **Hungry Lane**, leaves the bend and runs north into the gold country of the Unfed, toward the hearthless town of Wanting; it is used by Crumb-Runners, Hostwatch trackers, poachers, and nobody else. The station is old, solid, and plain: a two-storey inn of grey fieldstone with a slate roof and seventeen chimneys, a walled yard, a long stable, a smokehouse, a toll house with a Company bell, and, behind a low wall, a little graveyard of nine stones.

It was founded in 402 A.G. by **Hadley Thimble**, a Crumb-Runner who had spent her life carrying grain into the Fast on her back and been spat on for it by the people it kept alive. She left the Fast, married a Fatlands drover named Corley Mutch, took his money and kept her own name, bought the crossing, and swore her charter with the Rim Road Company in Oathen before the Company's notary of the day. The charter granted the Thimble blood the keeping of the station in perpetuity in return for a single clause, which Hadley, who had grown up among people who refused everything, insisted on writing herself: *that the keepers of Thimble Cross shall turn no traveller away, nor refuse any guest, while the blood of Thimble endures.*

She meant it as a kindness. She had been turned away from every door on the Rim in the Thin Years of her grandmother's stories, and she had stood outside the Pantry in Wanting in the snow while elders argued over her name. She wanted one house on the Table where nobody would ever be left on the step. She carved *Welcome* over her own grave before she died, and the Company sent a clerk to the funeral, which it does not do for innkeepers.

Promises sworn in Oathen follow the body that swore them, and the bodies that come after it, if the drafting says so. Hadley's drafting said so. Her descendants cannot refuse a guest without their bodies breaking to match. Mistress Hepzibah Thimble barred the door once, in her youth, against a leper in the rain, and two fingers of her right hand folded backwards to the wrist with a sound like kindling; she wears them that way still. Her great-uncle Ezra refused a drunk Reckoner a bed in 571, and his jaw broke in three places while he was still saying the word, and he took in the Reckoner anyway, through the blood, and the Reckoner murdered a Gut-line merchant in room four that night. The family does not tell that story to guests.

And because the charter binds only "the blood of Thimble," and a Clausewright consulted in 488 A.G. opined that a mixed line might be argued to have ended, the Thimbles have for five generations kept their blood carefully, narrowly, at home. Company ledgers list Absalom and Hepzibah Thimble as husband and wife. Three lines higher on the same page, in an older hand, they are listed as born to the same mother. Guests notice the family's sameness: the same pale-lashed grey eyes, the same small chin, the same strawberry birthmark at the throat, the same habit of laying an extra spoon. Guests do not ask.

### The Convoy

The convoy is eleven wagons of the Renderers' Union under **Teamster Abel Crumm**, carrying forty-four barrels north from the Chute toward the Vigil, where Fatlands salt pork is bought by the ton to feed a city that eats hourly to stay awake. It travels under the seal of **Cullmaster Hedley Brawn**, who rides with it to see the far-field barrels safely sold, because the Board is beginning to worry about inspection at the Vigil border and wishes its paperwork to be beyond reproach. The convoy's manifest lists forty-four barrels of "pork, salted, first quality." The thirteen notched barrels were packed at the Dusk Acres under Brawn's eye, and stamped at the Chute by Quartermaster Ivo Stockpot, who notched them with his own knife because he would not eat from them himself. Abel Crumm knows they are notched. He has not let himself know why.

Six days ago, as the wagons climbed out of the warm country, the ground behind them began to whiten in long, branching veins. Each night the marbling crept a little further up the road. One drover, Pim Hayward, was lost on the second night. Crumm entered it as wolves, as the Board's forms require. On the evening before the adventure begins, the convoy reached Thimble Cross, and the marbling came with it, and in the morning it was lying a hand deep over everything, and it had begun to fall from the sky.

[pull] There's no wolves in the Fatlands, love. There's nothing in the Fatlands but crop. So when the form says wolves, you write wolves, and you know what it means, and you don't say. | — Big Oona Harl, to a new drover, on the road north

### Why Now

Tablenight is the anniversary of the Gorging, the night six peoples ate their gods and one did not. Orrum has been patient for six hundred and forty-one years. This year, the Hostwatch say, it is walking faster. Its steward, Welcome Ardent, usually lays a table a mile ahead of his master and waits for him. In the last year, at three Rim inns, the Host has gone ahead and laid without him. At Thimble Cross it has done so again, and this time the ninth place has a card, in a hand that is not Ardent's, and that frightens Ardent more than anything has frightened him in six centuries.

Something is hurrying the Host. The Hostwatch think it is the Regrowth: six eaten gods coming back, and a seventh, whole, feeling its brothers stir. Ardent thinks it is something else, and will not say what, because he has never been told, and has decided that not being told is the same as knowing.

## Running The Ninth Chair

### Structure

The adventure runs across seven days, from **Day One** (six days before Tablenight) to **Day Seven** (Tablenight itself). A day-by-day timeline gives what happens if the characters do nothing; the characters will, of course, do something, and the timeline bends around them. Scenes are written to be dropped in when the moment is right. Roughly:

| Session | Days | What It Is About |
| One | Days One to Three | Arrival, the table, the cards, the first Reaping, hunger, the roster, the suicide |
| Two | Days Four to Six | The graveyard, the kin-slaying, the oath on the dead, the ritual killing, the Seeming, Ardent |
| Three | Day Seven | The demand, the sacrifice, the Host, the long night, the endings |
| Four (optional) | The thaw and the week after | After the Snow: the inquest, the Seated, the charter, leaving |

For a two-session game, compress Days Four to Six into a single long night of discoveries, and begin the second session on the morning the consensus forms. For a single long evening, begin on the morning of Day Four with the graves already open, give the players the place cards and Abel's note as handouts in the first ten minutes, and play straight through to dawn on Tablenight.

### The Shape of Dread

Thimble Cross gets worse in a particular order: first strange, then hungry, then frightened, then cruel, then devout. Pace the Dread checks accordingly. The first session should cost each character perhaps 4 to 6 Fray; by the climax most should be within reach of Breaking, so that the arrival of the Host can tip one or two over the edge. The Carver's Tally near the end of this chapter lists every scene's Dread rating, so you can see where the pressure falls and ease it where it has fallen too hard.

Contagion of madness is a central mechanic here: when anyone Breaks, everyone who sees it makes a Dread 2 check, and the inn's own population is Breaking too. Because a god is physically present in the house from Day Two onward, and in the room on Tablenight, the rule for contagion near a Seating applies from midnight on Day Seven: witnesses to a Break check at Dread 3.

Give the players rest when they earn it. A night spent in a warm room with someone they trust, fed honestly, removes 1 Fray, and a sincere rite led by a Celebrant removes 2. Sister Clemency offers rites. They work. That is part of the problem.

[pull] First it's strange. Then you're hungry. Then you're frightened. Then you're cruel. Then you pray. That's every siege I ever read about, and I've read about nine. | — Dorcas Thimble, Day Four

### The Count

From Day Two, **Merrit Sloe**, the Brinehollow bail-girl, begins the count, as is the custom of her country: each morning she walks the upstairs corridor and calls every name through every wall, and waits for an answer. On the first morning she cuts a little plank-ear into the wall beside each door with her gutting knife, the way they do on the Middle Mile in Lowmark, so the names will carry. Use the count to open every day after the first. It tells the players who is gone without needing a scene, and the silences after names are the adventure's heartbeat. Keep a list. Cross names off where the players can see you do it.

Keep Merrit's count consistent with the timeline. On the morning of Day Three, Abel does not answer. On Day Four, nobody answers for Big Oona if she was Reaped. On Day Five, Absalom's name meets a silence, and then Hepzibah answers for him, "Here," in a perfectly ordinary voice, and the players should feel that. On Day Six, the secretary's room is empty. On Day Seven, if Merrit is still alive, she counts the Seated too, the ones who have already sat, and they answer cheerfully from the common room below, through the floor.

[box: Hunger at Thimble Cross]
The inn's stores run short at once. From Day Two, everyone at Thimble Cross is on half bread. From Day Four, there is no bread. A character who does not eat a proper meal on a given day gains 1 Hunger at dawn whether they have Partaken or not, and one level of Starving (−1 to all rolls per level, as in the rules chapter). Unfed characters use their own Pangs instead: half bread is a Pantry measure and changes nothing for them; no bread is +2 Pangs a day, and they gain Want for every day ended hungry.
Hunger gained from starvation counts toward Regrowth like any other. Fatlanders, whose hunger was never in the stomach, gain 2 Hunger a day instead of 1, but suffer no Starving penalty for the first three days: they are used to it.
A character who eats honestly, from their own pack or from an un-notched barrel, removes one level of Starving that day. Characters began play with three days of their own food. Let them share it. Let them argue about sharing it. It is the first moral choice of the adventure, and the smallest.
[/box]

[box: Eating from the Barrels]
The convoy's forty-four barrels are the only food at Thimble Cross worth the name. Thirty-one are honest salt pork. Thirteen are notched (see the Convoy Manifest handout). Meat from a notched barrel is the best thing any character has ever eaten: sweet, yielding, rich, with a fat that melts at blood heat and coats the tongue for an hour. It removes all Starving penalties. It does not remove Hunger. Instead, a character who eats it must roll Gut + Feast against Hard 14. On a failure they acquire the **Laden Taste** until the end of the adventure: they gain 1 Hunger every time they see food, smell cooking, or watch someone else eat, and they must make a Caul + Resolve roll (Hard 14) to stop eating once they have begun.
A character who eats from a notched barrel three times dreams the memories of the person the meat once was, and must make a Dread 3 check on waking: eating a person, knowingly, at last. A character who learns afterward what they ate faces the same check. A Fatlander who eats it suffers nothing new at all, which is the worst thing about it.
[/box]

[box: The Marbling]
The marbling looks like snow until you are close to it. Then it looks like the white veining in a cut of beef, branching and rejoining across the ground, with a pinkish translucence between the veins that gives slightly underfoot. It smells of cold fat and, faintly, of bread. It does not melt in the hand. It softens. It falls from the sky in soft white flakes that land on the skin and cling, greasy, and must be scraped away with a knife-back.
Where it lies, the ground is Fatlands ground. Between sunset and full dark, anyone standing on marbled ground alone may be Reaped. Each day the marbling covers more: on Day One the road and the yard; on Day Three the stable floor and the graveyard; on Day Five the cellar; on Day Seven the boards of the common room, everywhere except the strip of floor beneath the Host's table, which stays bare and warm.
[/box]

#### The Reaping at Thimble Cross

The Reaping here obeys its Fatlands rules. A character alone on marbled ground between sunset and full dark first notices a stillness (Eye + Search, Routine 10). Then the ground leans in, the smell of new bread rising from it (Dread 3 to witness; Dread 2 for a Fatlander). Then it softens: the character must succeed at Caul + Resolve (Hard 14; Grim 18 if their Hunger is 6 or more, or if Brawn has named them) or stop wanting to leave. Companions may drag a character free with Gut + Labor against Grim 18, but each round in the soil deals 1d6 Flesh as the ground begins, gently, to digest. A character Reaped is gone. What is pulled out after full dark is a shape of clothes and the smell of bread.

Two or more people standing together are not alone, and the field does not take them. This is the single most important rule in the first half of the adventure, and the players should discover it by doing the decent thing: going out to stand with someone. Never use the Reaping as a surprise kill on a player character; describe it slowly, and give their friends the chance to pull.

[pull] A field doesn't hate you. A field doesn't do anything as clever as hate. It's just glad you came. | — Cullmaster Hedley Brawn, explaining the Reaping, Day Two

### Portions and Advancement

At the end of each session, award each character **5 to 10 Portions** of experience, as described under Aftermath. A character who survives the three core sessions should have earned between 15 and 30; the fourth session adds 4 to 8 more. This is more than an ordinary story pays, deliberately: the characters who walk away from Thimble Cross have been through something that changes a person, and their sheets should show it.

### The Keepers of the House

#### Absalom Thimble, the Innkeeper

[pull] You look thin. Sit. No, sit, I'll not hear it. There's always a bit more in the pot if you know how to look. | — Absalom Thimble

A big, soft, slope-shouldered man of sixty-one with the family's pale lashes and small chin, an apron that has not been fully white since the Thin Rising was a story his grandmother told, and a voice like a warm spoon. He smells of onions softened in dripping, of pipe-smoke and of the wet wool of a man who has been out to the woodpile in his slippers. Absalom has kept Thimble Cross for thirty-eight years and has never in that time turned anyone away, because he cannot. He loves the work. He loves the moment a cold traveller's face changes at the smell of his pottage. The charter does not feel like a curse to him; it feels like who he is.

That is why the famine breaks him. A host with no food is a contradiction his body cannot hold, and from Day One his hands tremble when he passes the empty pantry shelves; by evening the trembling has reached his jaw, and he stands at the stove chewing nothing. On the night of Day One he digs up his mother, Old Mother Ivy Thimble, buried in Carving and kept sweet by the cold, and on Day Two he serves her, and weeps in the kitchen while the guests ask for more. He tells himself she would have wanted it. He is right. That is the second worst thing about it.

#### Hepzibah Thimble, the Keeper's Wife

[pull] The table wants doing. I don't know why. A table like that ought to be kept nice, that's all. | — Hepzibah Thimble, beeswax on her hands, Day Two

Small, upright, sixty, with her ruined right hand held against her apron like a folded bird, and the smell of lavender water and lye soap about her always. Hepzibah runs the house: the linen, the rooms, the ledger of who sleeps where, the keys on a chatelaine at her waist that she counts with her good hand when she is frightened. She is the family's memory and its law. She is also Absalom's sister, a fact she has never spoken aloud in her life and will defend with a carving knife.

She regards the charter the way a priest regards scripture. The family has suffered for it for two hundred years; therefore it must be holy. When Orrum's table appears she polishes it, every morning, with beeswax, and cannot say why. By Day Five she is polishing it three times a day. By Day Six she is setting places at the pine tables for the dead. On Tablenight she sits first, at her own card, with her ruined hand in her lap, and says that it is very good, that it is the best she has ever had, that she always knew someone would cook for her one day.

#### Linus Thimble, the Son

[pull] I'm not a clever man. I know horses. I know a joint when it gives. That's all I know. That's all I want to know. | — Linus Thimble

Thirty-four, heavy-handed, quiet, a good farrier and a bad liar, with his father's build and his mother's eyes, and hands that smell always of hoof-parings and the forge. Linus has always known that he and his sister are expected to marry, because there are no other Thimbles left, and because the Clausewright's opinion is framed in the parlour. He has been trying for ten years to find a way to be content with it, and he very nearly has, and that frightens him more than the expectation itself.

Linus discovers his father's crime on Day Four, when he finds his grandmother's wedding ring in the bottom of the pottage pot. He kills Absalom that night in the cellar with the cleaver his father taught him to use. Afterward he sits with the body until dawn and holds its hand. He does not resist the oath his mother makes him swear over it. He does not resist anything, after that. He moves through the last three days as though carried, and on Tablenight he sits beside his mother at the table and is, for the first time anyone can remember, visibly at peace.

#### Dorcas Thimble, the Daughter

[pull] There's no exit clause. I've looked. I've looked every night since I was fourteen. There's only the door, and the door breaks you. | — Dorcas Thimble

Twenty-nine, sharp-tongued, tall, ink on her fingers, the only Thimble who reads anything but the ledger. Dorcas has read the charter every night since she was fourteen, looking for an exit clause, and there is none. She has bought every Oathen law-book a passing Clausewright would sell her, and they are stacked in her room to the window-sill, and she has annotated all of them. She wants out: out of the inn, out of the blood, out of the marriage the parlour wall has arranged for her.

She is the person most likely to ask the characters, quietly, to help her leave, and the person for whom leaving is least possible. If she crosses the station's boundary stones without the charter being released, her body will break. She knows this. She has worked out precisely how badly: she has the bone-count from Ezra's jaw and Hepzibah's fingers and a great-aunt's spine, and she has drawn a diagram. She has decided she might be willing to find out. On Day Four she swears the oath on her father's body because her mother is watching and the characters are watching and she cannot think of a single word that would get her out of it, and afterward she goes to her room and is very quietly sick in a law-book.

[quick] Dorcas Thimble — Threat 3 · Flesh 12 · Guard 13 · Attack +4 (bread knife 2) · Armor 0 · Dread 0 (Hearth-Bound)

[box: Playing the Thimble Curse]
The Thimbles' inherited curse should stay in the corners of the eye. The players learn it through the sameness of their faces; through the ledger that lists Absalom and Hepzibah as born to the same mother; through the framed Clausewright's opinion in the parlour with its phrase "the blood kept narrow, the line kept whole"; through the single wide bed in the family wing and the second one beside it that has never been slept in; through Dorcas looking at her brother across a room and then looking away. Never make it explicit. Never make it a joke. It is a family that has been doing a terrible thing to itself for two hundred years because a dead woman wrote a kind clause badly, and the most frightening thing about it is how ordinary they have made it.
[/box]

#### Warden Josse Haverick, of the Rim Road Company

[pull] I would, if it were within my oath, and I believe it may be, provided the Company's peace is not thereby put at hazard, which I cannot at present promise. So: perhaps. Pass the salt. | — Warden Haverick, asked for a candle

Forty-four, lean, a long jaw shaved blue each morning in cold water whatever the weather, an Oathener of minor Tongue-line blood who speaks the way all Oatheners do, slowly and in conditionals, with all the exits built in. He smells of cold water and ink. He keeps the toll-house clock, winding it at every entry, and it is the only clock in the house. Haverick holds Thimble Cross for the Company. He swore before the notary Jessamy Quill, at Quillhouse, to "hold this station for the Company until relieved, keep its peace, and keep its ledger true." Sworn in Oathen, the promise travels with him. He cannot abandon the station; he cannot falsify the ledger; he cannot fail to keep the peace without his body coming apart to match.

He is honest because he has no choice and decent because he chose it. He is the closest thing to law at Thimble Cross, and he is trapped there more completely than anyone except the Thimbles. Every death in the house is, by the terms of his oath, a breach of the peace, and every breach costs him a little: a hairline crack in a rib on Day One, a cracked tooth on Day Three, two fingers numb on Day Four. He binds the injuries himself and enters them in the ledger under *Wear*.

### The Convoy

#### Cullmaster Hedley Brawn, of the Board of Plenty

[pull] It isn't cruelty, my dear. Cruelty is waste. This is husbandry. One at dusk, by choice, and the field sleeps sweet. Have a sugared fat. No? More for the field. | — Cullmaster Hedley Brawn

A vast, courteous, beautifully dressed man in plum wool and a waistcoat with forty bone buttons, all straining. He smells of rosewater over rendered suet, and he breathes through his mouth, softly, all the time, a sound like someone blowing on soup. Brawn is a Gut-line cadet and a career officer of the Board's quietest department, the one that decides which vagrants, debtors and troublemakers are assigned the dusk-shift in the far fields. He is the best officer Cullmaster Varn Sweetbread has, and Sweetbread sent him north with these barrels because he trusts him to make the paperwork beautiful.

He eats constantly and without hurry, from a lacquered box of candied fat he keeps in his coat, and he speaks of the Reaping as a farmer speaks of rain: something to be planned for, measured, and directed where it will do the most good. He is not cruel. He finds cruelty wasteful. He believes, sincerely, that the Reaping can be steered, that a field which is fed one chosen person at dusk will not take a random one at midnight, and he is, horribly, partly right. From Day Two he proposes a **dusk roster**: each evening, one person will go out on the marbled ground and stand there, and the rest will be safe. He will happily draw up the roster. He will not put himself on it. He has a Board warrant that says he need not.

#### Teamster Abel Crumm, of the Renderers' Union

[pull] Lack keep away, friends. Lack keep away from every one of you, and from your mothers, and from your mothers' pots. Drink up. It's on the Union. | — Abel Crumm, the first evening

Fifty-two, square, sunburned in a country with no sun to speak of, with the Fatlands' hunger behind his eyes and a teamster's habit of counting things twice. He smells of ox, pipe-smoke and pear spirit, and his laugh comes up from his belly like a cart over a bridge. Abel has driven the Chute road for thirty years, and he has always known some barrels are notched, and he has always decided not to ask. He is kind to his drovers, generous with his pipe, and on the first evening he stands the whole common room a round of pear spirit and says "Lack keep away" with real feeling.

On the night of Day Two he opens barrel 31 and finds, in the brine, a forearm with a tattoo he paid for himself: a sheaf of wheat and the name **ROBB**, for his sister's boy, who went to work the dusk-shift in Low Tilth last Carving for a debt of thirty lacks. He is found in the cold store on the morning of Day Three. He leaves a note. He leaves his pipe to Oona. He leaves the drovers to work out, by themselves, which of them knew, and how long, and whether it matters.

#### The Drovers

Six Renderers' Union drovers travel with the convoy, all Fatlanders, all perpetually, quietly eating: seeds from their pockets, the rinds of cheese, the leather of their own reins.

- **Big Oona Harl** is the oldest, a woman of fifty built like a dray-horse, with forearms like hams and a soft high singing voice that nobody expects. She sings field hymns, the long ones that thank each field by name, and she loves Abel Crumm like a brother. She has carried his tobacco for twenty years because he always forgets it.
- **Nib Furrow** is eighteen, freckled, on his first run, and so hungry he chews the leather of his own reins. He is first on Brawn's roster. He votes for it.
- **Gus Haslet** and **Tam Pettle** are cousins who argue about everything except food: the weather, the Board, whose turn it is to grease the axles, whether the Vigil folk really never sleep. After Abel dies they stop arguing, which frightens everyone more than the arguing did.
- **Walt Sallow** is silent and missing two fingers, lost to a reaping-hook in his youth. He is the first the field takes, on the evening of Day One.
- **Lise Trotter**, kin to the drover Sal Trotter, does not name the cattle. She knows why. She will tell a character who asks kindly, on Day Two, in the stable, in a low voice: "Because when you name them, they answer."

The convoy's twenty-two oxen stand in the yard, and on the second night, when no one is watching the stable, some of them stand on two legs.

### The Poacher and His Prize

#### Kale Dragoman, the Poacher

[pull] There now. Slow. Little sips. Your belly's forgotten how. That's my brave lass. No, don't cry on it, you'll salt it. | — Kale Dragoman, at the bars of the cage

Big, ruddy and handsome in the Fatlands way, thirty-five, his size all appetite and none of it softness: heavy jowls that shake when he laughs, beautiful white teeth he cleans with a willow twig after every meal, small clever brown eyes, hands like a baker's. He wears a good brown Rim coat over boiled leather, a red neckcloth, and a bay leaf in his hatband, and he smells always of woodsmoke and roast fat and onions softened in dripping. His voice is a rolling eastern burr, the voice of a man who has called a great many people *my love* and meant it each time. He eats constantly from his pockets.

Kale hunts the Unfed across the border for the Blank trade. He takes them alive, because a living Blank fetches four times a dead one at the surgeries where blank blood and blank organs are sold to the nobility. And he feeds them well on the road, slowly, the way the physicians' books say, broth and then milk and then soft bread, because his buyers prefer a plump specimen and because, he says, there is no reason to be unkind. His captives find this the hardest thing to forgive. He travels with two hired men, Odo and Fenn, and a wheeled iron cage, clean, straw-floored, with a wool blanket. He cleans it himself.

Kale is witty, morally weightless, and good company, and he will try hard to be liked, because people who like him are easier to sell things to, and because he cannot bear not to be. His buyers since 638 have been the Second Table. He has never met one of its nobles face to face. On Day Two, at supper, a widow in grey looks at the two-tined fork badge on the inside of his lapel, and he realises that he has.

#### Refrain Dole, the Tablebreaker

[pull] Two hundred and six. I count them. I count the chairs too. Never once found one I wanted. Liar. I'm a liar. Put that in your book, Oathen. | — Refrain Dole

She is thirty-three, Unfed, and she has put an axe through two hundred and six of Orrum's tables. Kale took her on the Hungry Lane a week ago with a cosh behind the ear while she was splitting a laid board near Wanting, and she has been in his cage ever since, eating his cooking because the alternative is to be held and fed, and hating every sweet mouthful. She is gaunt in the way of her people, with cropped black hair, the knuckles of a woodsman, a bruise across her temple gone green, a smell of cold air and clean sweat, and the Unfed's pride in her chin like a fist.

She is, by design, the person the players should come to care about most. She is the most likely volunteer for the ninth chair.

### The Travellers

#### Lady Vellum Serrat, of the Second Table

[pull] If it should prove, madam, that the Host is a guest in this house, then it may also prove, under the custom of houses, that a guest may be served. I say no more than that. I could say a great deal more. | — Lady Vellum Serrat, through the wire

She travels as "the widow Serrat, a dealer in lace," in grey mourning silk with a black lace veil that hides a slim bit of gilt wire across her tongue. She is in truth a Tongue-line noble of Tacit, Cut 4, fifty years old, deep in the Appetite and fighting a compulsion to swear that the bit holds in check. She speaks rarely, in exquisite conditionals, through the wire, so that every sentence comes out faintly lisped and glittering, and she smells of orris-root, old silk and, faintly, of the blood that seeps where the wire rubs.

She is a member of the Second Table, whose one idea is that the gods, when they have regrown, should be eaten again, and she has come to Thimble Cross deliberately, following the Hostwatch reports of tables laid at Rim Road inns, to see one of Orrum's tables up close and take its measurements. She carries a cutwright's tape, a silver carving set wrapped in oilcloth, and a letter from Wont Bellamy of the Abstinent. She regards the Host as the only whole god left on the Table and therefore the only unspoiled meal. ## The Places of Thimble Cross

### The Bend and the Approach

[fiction]
The road comes up out of the south between low hedges gone white, and for a mile you think it is snow, and are glad of it, because snow is clean. Then your boots begin to slide in something that is not ice, and your horse will not put its head down to it, and the smell reaches you: cold dripping, a butcher's slab in the morning, and under it, faint and impossible, new bread. The flakes that settle on your sleeve do not melt. They soften, and spread, and leave a sheen. Ahead, where the road bends west, stands a grey inn with seventeen chimneys, and only one of them is smoking. Beyond it, to the north, the land goes gold, as if it were late afternoon in early Carving there, though it is the dead of Lack here. The white stops at the edge of that light like a tide.
[/fiction]

The marbling covers the Rim Road for six miles south and three west; travel out is Grim 18 on Lung + Wayfaring by day and impossible at dusk, and the Reaping walks the road. A character who tries it by day and fails comes back at noon to the inn they left, having walked in a long slow curve, with the smell of bread in their clothes. North, the Hungry Lane runs into the Fast, and the ground there is bare and warm wherever the Host has walked. The characters will realise, if they test it, that the safest ground in the region is the god's road.

At the bend itself stand the station's two boundary stones, waist-high, carved with the Company's wheel on one face and *Thimble* on the other. They mark the edge of the charter. No Thimble has passed them in two hundred years without paying for it in bone.

### The Yard and the Convoy

A cobbled yard behind a seven-foot wall, crammed with eleven wagons drawn into a ring. The oxen stand head-down, breath steaming, and do not move when touched. The barrels are stacked under oiled canvas: forty-four oak casks bound in iron, each stamped with the Chute's mark struck over twice, thirteen with a small knife-notch beneath. Marbling lies thickest here. By Day Three it has grown veins up the wagon wheels, and by Day Five a white rind has closed over the hubs, so that the wagons look like things that have grown out of the ground.

[fiction]
Under the canvas the barrels sweat. A slow pink brine beads at the staves and runs and freezes into soft white whorls on the cobbles. When the wind drops you can hear them settling, a creak and a long wet shift, the sound of something in a hammock turning over in its sleep. Lay your ear to a notched one and, under the slosh, very faint, there is a sound like breathing through a wet cloth. Lay your ear to an honest one, and there is only brine.
[/fiction]

Search (Eye + Search, Hard 14) finds the notches. A Fatlander, or anyone with Reckoning 2+, can see that the notched barrels are heavier than pork should be. Laying a palm on a notched barrel at night (Caul + Instinct, Hard 14) reveals that it is warm.

### The Common Room

A long, low, smoke-darkened room with a flagged floor, three tables of scarred pine, a great hearth carved with the founding motto, and a row of west windows looking toward the Fast. On the wall by the kitchen door, prices are chalked on a slate in Hepzibah's hand, and from Day Two they are rubbed out one by one and not replaced. From the morning of Day Two the room holds a fourth table.

[fiction]
It runs down the middle of the room where no table was, dark wood polished to the depth of still water, so long the lamp at its head and the lamp at its foot cannot see each other. The cloth is white the way bone is white when it has been boiled clean. There are nine places. Nine plates of thin cream porcelain rimmed in gold, each with a folded card. Nine chairs; eight of them pulled out a hand's width, as if for someone about to sit. The ninth, at the foot, is pushed in square to the table, its back to the lamp. Down the centre: a loaf, broken, steam still rising from the break. A dish of butter sweating gold. A tureen with its lid on. The room is warm for the first time in two days. [/fiction]

The table cannot be moved: it is not heavy, it simply does not go. Axes split it (Refrain's axe always does; others need Hand + Labor, Hard 14) and it is whole again the next dawn, with fresh bread. The food is the Host's; eating any of it makes the eater a guest (see the Rule of the Host's Table). The bread is never cold. Its smell reaches every room in the inn, and every character who passes through the common room hungry makes an Invitation check at Routine 10 or gains 1 Hunger (Unfed: 1 Pang).

The tureen, if anyone lifts the lid, is full of a clear golden broth with nothing in it, and the steam that comes off it smells, to each person who leans over it, of the best meal of their childhood. That is a Dread 1 check, and the Carver should ask the player, quietly, what they smell.

#### The Place Cards

There are nine cards. Eight bear names, in a fine, round, careful hand that each reader recognises as the handwriting of someone who loved them: a mother, a grandmother, a first teacher. Fill the eight with every player character's name (correctly spelled, including any secret or childhood name), plus, in order to reach eight, **Merrit Sloe**, **Linus Thimble**, **Lord Casimir Hollowe-Vane** (under his true name, which exposes him), and **Absalom Thimble**. For three characters, use all four; for four, drop Absalom; for five, drop Absalom and Linus. The ninth card bears no name; see the handout.

The table grows. Each time someone new becomes a guest (by eating the bread, by blasphemy, by sitting), the table is a little longer the next morning, though the room is not, and there is a new place with a new card. By Tablenight it may seat twenty. Nobody can see where the extra length goes. A character who paces it (Eye + Reckoning, Hard 14) finds it longer than the room by eleven feet.

### The Kitchen and Pantry

A stone-flagged kitchen with a hearth big enough to roast a pig, copper pans on hooks, a scrubbed deal table worn into a shallow trough down the middle by two centuries of kneading, and a pantry of sixty empty shelves, each labelled in faded paint: *barley*, *oats*, *dripping*, *salt*, *the good salt*, *Mother's*. On Day Two the great pot hangs over the fire with the pottage in it. A character with Stitching 1+ or a Fatlands upbringing who examines the pottage's meat (Eye + Search, Hard 14) can tell it is not mutton and not pork; on a Grace, they find a small gold ring with an inscription, *A.T. to I.M., kept*. Hob Gristle of Sated could have told them the name by taste. The characters will have to make do.

### The Cellar and Cold Store

Steep steps down to a vaulted cellar of casks and racks, smelling of earth, vinegar and old apples, and beyond a heavy iron-strapped door the cold store, where meat hangs on hooks in winter. This winter the hooks are empty. From Day Five the cellar floor is marbled, and the white climbs the barrel-staves in veins, and the steps are slick with it to the third tread. Abel Crumm is found in the cold store on Day Three. Absalom Thimble dies in the cellar on the night of Day Four.

### The Guest Rooms and the Long Room

Upstairs, nine narrow rooms off a single corridor with thin plank walls, and at the end the Long Room, a dormitory of twelve straw pallets for drovers. Here Merrit walks every morning, knocking, calling names into the plank-ears she has cut. The west-facing rooms are the warmest, and the Fast's gold light lies across their floors all night, and those who sleep in them dream of being invited. A character who sleeps in a west room makes an Invitation check at Routine 10 on waking; on a failure they wake with their hand already on the door-latch.

### The Family Wing

A locked door off the kitchen opens onto the Thimbles' rooms: a parlour with the charter framed under glass beside the Clausewright's opinion of 488, and a sampler above them in faded red thread, *KEPT WHOLE*; a plain room with two beds, one wide and worn and one narrow and never slept in; Dorcas's room, stacked with books on Oathen law, with a diagram of the human skeleton pinned over the bed and certain bones marked in ink. Searching the parlour (Eye + Search, Routine 10) finds the family register, which tells the attentive reader everything about the Thimble blood without saying anything at all.

### The Stable and Kale's Cage

A long stone stable smelling of straw, dung and the sweetish, wrong smell of the convoy's oxen, its floor marbled from Day Three. At the far end, under a tarpaulin, Kale Dragoman's cage: iron bars, a wooden floor with clean straw, a slop bucket, a wool blanket folded square, a copper stewpot on a brazier outside the bars that is never allowed to go cold, and Refrain Dole sitting with her back straight and her eyes open. Kale's men take turns sleeping across the door. Picking the padlock is Hand + Filch, Hard 14; the key is on Kale's belt. Refrain's felling axe is wrapped in sacking in the rafters over Kale's own pallet, where he thinks no one will look.

### The Smokehouse

A squat brick tower across the yard, black inside with three centuries of smoke, hung with iron hooks and racks, its floor a crust of old fat and ash that cracks underfoot like burnt sugar. It smells of hams long gone and of something sweeter under that. It is where Sister Clemency performs the Ninefold Purge on Lord Casimir on the night of Day Five.

### The Little Graveyard

Behind a waist-high wall, nine stones for nine generations of Thimbles, the oldest, Hadley's, carved with a single word: *Welcome*. The others are carved with names and the word *Kept*. The newest, Old Mother Ivy Thimble's, set in Carving, is still sharp-edged and pale. From Day Three the marbling lies across it. On the morning of Day Four three graves are open: Ivy's dug down from above by a spade, and two older ones, Hadley's and her son's, broken open from beneath.

### The Toll House

Haverick's post: a stone hut at the gate with a bell, a strongbox, a crossbow rack, the toll-house clock, and the station ledger on a lectern. The ledger records everything the warden sees, and he cannot make it lie. Reading it (no roll; Haverick will allow it, because refusing would be keeping the peace badly) gives the true daily count of the living and the dead, and every one of his Wear entries. The last page has already been ruled, and the last line already written, in his small careful hand: *Warden relieved.* The date is left blank.

[box: Small Horrors of the Inn]
Roll 1d10 whenever a character spends a quiet hour anywhere in Thimble Cross, or when the scene needs a shiver.
1. A plate in the kitchen rack is warm. None of the others are.
2. Every chair in the room has been pulled out a hand's width. Nobody did it.
3. The snow on the west sill has drifted into the shape of a sleeping person, curled, arms around something.
4. A drover chewing in the corner is chewing the corner of his blanket. He does not notice. He says it is good.
5. The smell of bread is suddenly so strong that someone's mouth floods and they have to spit.
6. Through the floor, very faint, the sound of cutlery on china, and laughter.
7. A card has been slipped under the character's door. It is blank. It is warm.
8. In the yard, an ox is standing on two legs, looking at the window. When looked at directly it is on four, and chewing.
9. Hepzibah is polishing a spoon. She has been polishing it for an hour. It is worn through to the shape of her thumb.
10. The toll-house clock strikes, and keeps striking, past twelve, to nine and twenty, and stops.
[/box]

## Hooks: Why You Are Here

Each character needs a reason to be on the Rim Road in the dead of Lack. Choose or adapt one per character. Every hook should bring them to Thimble Cross on the afternoon of Day One, just as the road closes.

- **Sallowreach.** A Seamer's journeyman or Heir carrying a sealed jar north from the Fatlands for a client in Lastgate. Or: the Mortuary Court has sent you to find a runaway Hand-line cadet, Casimir Hollowe-Vane, before he touches someone important; he was last seen heading east with a widow in grey. Or: his cousin Osric, blindfolded in his locked room, has paid you out of his own purse to find Casimir and ask him only one thing: whether he is all right.
- **The Fatlands.** A Renderers' Union clerk sent by Mistress Brisket Annalow to quietly check one convoy's barrels against its manifest, because Quartermaster Ivo Stockpot has been notching more of them than she likes. Or: a Thin dissident fleeing north toward the Fast with the Board's bailiffs a day behind. Or: a copyist of Pell Hogget's Long Count, walking the villages for names, who has heard there is a Cullmaster on the road with a little book.
- **Brinehollow.** A Netwatch deserter walking inland, or a salvager carrying a sketch of Cray Lobbett's eight-chaired table to sell in the Vigil. Merrit Sloe recognises a fellow coaster and bails you the first night, and cuts you a plank-ear by your door.
- **The Vigil.** A Waker escort assigned to meet Courier Lisle at Thimble Cross and see her dispatch safely to Lastgate. Or: an apprentice of Mirela Sconce sent to find the source of the Seeming of a tall figure laying a table in Thimble Lane, which is, you have realised on the road with a cold feeling, the same name.
- **Cradlewrack.** A border-runner for Petra Quick fleeing the highlands, or an Assembler sent to buy a Part rumoured to be among a poacher's stock. Doors you pass drift open behind you, and Lord Casimir shuts every one. Sister Clemency recognises your accent on the first night and calls you *daughter*.
- **Oathen.** A Surety travelling to the Company's Vigil office, carrying the risk of someone else's contract in your body, or a junior Clausewright sent by Jessamy Quill's office to audit the Thimble charter, which the Company would like to end. You have the Company's instructions in a sealed packet: find the weakness. Dorcas Thimble will find you first.
- **The Fast.** A Tablebreaker hunting the one who took Refrain Dole, your partner. Or a Hostwatch tracker following warm ground south along the Hungry Lane, a mile behind something. You know the rules. You will spend the adventure watching other people break them.

[box: The Ninth Card and the Characters]
The characters' names are on the cards because they are hungry and they are here. Nothing in their pasts summoned the table; the Host invites whoever is within a day's walk and in need. Make sure each player understands, early, that their character's card is written in the hand of the person who loved them most. That should be the first Dread check of the adventure (Dread 2, or Dread 3 if the hand belongs to someone dead), and it should be the one they remember. Before play, ask each player privately whose handwriting it is. Write it down. Use it again on Tablenight.
[/box]

## Act One: The Laid Place (Days One to Three)

### Day One: The Road Closes

The characters arrive through the falling marbling in the last grey hour of afternoon. Read or paraphrase the approach from the Bend, above. Absalom meets them at the door with a lantern and the words his charter requires.

[fiction]
The door is open before you reach it, and the light that falls out across the white is the colour of butter. A big soft man in an apron fills the doorway with a lantern held high, and his face, which is the face of a man who has been frightened all afternoon, breaks open at the sight of you like bread. "Come in, come in," he says, "there's always room," and the words come out of him the way a breath does, without his choosing, and he looks for a moment almost surprised to hear them. Behind him the common room is low and smoky and full: drovers eating something out of their pockets, a fat man in plum wool eating something out of a lacquered box, a woman in grey with a veil, a young man in gloves rising to shut the door behind you before you have quite let go of it. The fire is small. The smell of the house is onions, wet wool, and under it, very faint, as though from the next room, new bread.
[/fiction]

Let them meet the house: Haverick at his ledger in the toll house, taking their names and purpose of travel under oath; Brawn eating in the best chair by the fire; Kale at knucklebones with Odo and Fenn, offering the newcomers supper three times and then a fourth with a wink; the widow in grey and her gloved secretary; Clemency refusing supper and asking, kindly, who among the guests is of good family; Merrit carrying ale and asking where everyone is from, and whether they will need bailing; Bell Lisle standing by the wall because she does not dare sit. Abel Crumm stands the room a round of pear spirit. It is the last warm evening, and the Carver should let it be warm: let the characters eat, joke, trade stories, find out that Oona sings and that Haverick, asked, can recite the whole of the Company's Articles of Hospitality from memory.

[pull] You'll have supper? You'll have supper. You'll have supper, won't you? Oh, go on. Four times, that's Fast manners, and you're not even Fast. | — Kale Dragoman, the first evening

**Dusk: the first Reaping.** As the light goes, the drover Walt Sallow steps out to check the oxen. Any character near a window or in the yard notices the stillness (Eye + Search, Routine 10). Then Walt stops walking. The ground around his boots has risen into soft white folds, like dough proving.

[fiction]
He does not shout. He looks down at his feet with an expression of mild interest, the way a man looks at a dog that has put its head in his lap. The white is over his ankles, then his shins, and it moves in a slow pulse, and where it touches him the cloth of his trousers darkens as if wet. He says, quite clearly, "Oh, that's warm." He puts his hands in his pockets. The smell of new bread is so strong that across the yard someone's stomach answers it aloud. The white closes over his knees with a small sound, a sound like a thumb pressed into risen dough, and he sighs, the way a tired man sighs getting into a bath.
[/fiction]

Witnessing is Dread 3 (Dread 2 for Fatlanders). Characters who run out can try to pull him free (Gut + Labor, Grim 18; see the Reaping rules), but Walt does not want to be pulled, and the ground is fast. If they fail, by full dark there is a shape of clothes on the cobbles, still holding the shape of a man, and two fingers' worth of empty glove where his missing fingers would have been. Brawn, at the window, wipes his mouth and enters the time in a small book. If the characters succeed, Walt is dragged free with his legs scalded pink to the knee and the skin of his shins gone soft and pitted like the crust of a loaf, and he weeps all night, not from the pain, but because it was so warm, and he wants to go back.

**Night: the spade.** Any character awake after midnight and near a window over the graveyard (Eye + Search, Hard 14) sees a lantern among the stones and a big soft figure digging, slowly, stopping often to wipe its face. If they go out, they find Absalom at the bottom of his mother's grave, sitting on the coffin lid in the lantern-light, weeping. He says she would have wanted to help. He says there is nothing else. He says the guests are so hungry. He cannot be made to stop by anything short of force, and force will break a bone in him (the charter: a refused guest is a refused guest, and a host who cannot feed his guests is refusing them). This is Dread 2, and it is the adventure's first great choice. Most groups will not see it at all. Those who do will have to decide what to do with what they know, at breakfast, when the pottage comes.

### Day Two: The Table

Dawn. Hepzibah's scream brings everyone down to the common room. Read the description of the table. Each character finds their own card and must make a Dread 2 check on recognising the handwriting (Dread 3 if the hand is a dead person's), and an Invitation check at Hard 14 on first reading their name.

[fiction]
Handout: A Place Card.
A stiff fold of cream card, warm to the touch, smelling faintly of yeast. The ink is brown, like gravy dried on a plate. The hand is round and careful and belongs, the reader knows at once, to their mother (or whoever loved them most).
(The character's name, spelled exactly as that person spelled it, including the name they used when the character was small.)
You are expected.
Tablenight. Come as you are.
There will be enough.
[/fiction]

[fiction]
Handout: The Ninth Card.
The same cream card, but cold, and slightly damp, as if it had been left out overnight. No name. In the centre, a single mark in the oldest form of Table-speech, made with a broad pen in one stroke: the root that means both *to worship* and *to eat*. Beneath it, a second, shorter stroke, drawn by a different hand, or the same hand much later. The second mark can be read, depending on the scholar, as *after*, *last*, *late*, or *still to come*. There is no third line. Where the other cards say *You are expected*, this one has a small, perfect thumbprint, pressed into the card as if into soft fat.
[/fiction]

Reading the ninth card needs Eye + Lore against Grim 18; on a success the reader knows the readings above. Refrain Dole reads it without a roll, if anyone shows her, and goes very quiet. Vellum asks, through her wire, whether she might hold it, and her hand is perfectly steady and her voice is not. The card cannot be burned: it chars and is whole on the table by morning. It can be carried away; it is cold and damp and never dries.

[pull] Is it mine? Is that mine? I didn't know Mam could write that nice. She couldn't. She couldn't write at all. | — Nib Furrow, holding a card that is not on the table, in a dream, the night of Day Two

**The first count.** Merrit walks the corridor calling names. Walt does not answer. Use it. Note who else does not answer.

**The pottage.** At midday Absalom serves a thick, fragrant pottage of barley and onion and tender pale meat, and the house, which has not eaten properly in two days, falls on it. It is wonderful. It is the best thing the drovers say they have ever had outside the Fatlands, and they say it as a compliment and look ashamed. Characters who eat it, and later learn what it was, face Dread 3 at the revelation. Characters who saw the spade in the night and eat anyway face it now.

**The dusk roster.** In the afternoon, Cullmaster Brawn addresses the common room, pleasantly, with a ledger.

[fiction]
Handout: The Dusk Roster (proposed by Cullmaster H. Brawn, Board of Plenty, and posted on the common-room door with a fork).
For the quiet of the field, and the safety of the house, the following are respectfully assigned the dusk standing, one each evening, on the marbled ground of the yard, from sunset to full dark. The field is fed by choice and does not choose for itself. No one else need go out.
Day Two: Furrow, N., drover.
Day Three: Haslet, G., drover.
Day Four: the courier (name to be confirmed).
Day Five: Pettle, T., drover.
Day Six: Sloe, M., servant of the house.
Day Seven (Tablenight): to be decided by the house, as the season requires.
Persons of Cut 3 and above are exempt by Board warrant, being of greater yield elsewhere. Unfed persons are excused, being of no yield. All others are asked to consider the arithmetic.
Lack keep away.
[/fiction]

He explains the Reaping as Board science: the field will take one at dusk regardless; a field that is fed by choice is a quiet field; he proposes to choose. Every name on the list is a drover, a servant, or a guest of low Cut. Brawn is persuasive (Tongue + Persuade 8); the starving room is frightened; unless the characters argue him down (an opposed Tongue + Persuade, or Haverick's support, which he will give if asked, because his oath includes the peace), the roster is accepted by a show of hands. Kale abstains with a smile. Nib Furrow votes for it, with his own hand, because the room is looking at him. Refrain, from the stable, laughs until she coughs.

**Terms in the stable.** At supper, Vellum sees the two-tined fork on the inside of Kale's lapel. Later, in the stable, through Casimir, she makes him an offer. A character who follows (Lung + Stealth, Hard 14) overhears it: she will pay Kale's full price for Refrain, in a sworn note, on condition that delivery is made "at this house, at this table, on Tablenight." Kale does not understand why. He agrees, because the price is good and because she is so polite. Refrain, in the cage, hears all of it, and says nothing, and that night she asks Merrit, through the bars, whether Brinehollow folk believe in anything after.

[fiction]
Handout: The Convoy Manifest (in Abel's wagon, in the box under the seat).
Renderers' Union of the Fatlands, by the Chute, sworn true under seal. Convoy of Teamster A. Crumm, eleven wagons, northbound for the Vigil, Lack 641.
Barrels 1 through 30, 32, 33: Pork, salted, first quality. Tilth Commons.
Barrels 31, 34 through 44: Pork, salted, first quality. Far field. Under Board seal, H. Brawn, Cullmaster.
Weight of far-field barrels exceeds pork standard by one-fifth. Entered as brine.
Origin: struck over twice. Inspected: struck over once. Stamped, I. Stockpot, Quartermaster.
Drovers: Crumm, Harl, Furrow, Haslet, Hayward, Pettle, Sallow, Trotter. Losses on the road: Hayle, R. (entered last Carving, Low Tilth, carried forward in error; strike).
[/fiction]

The manifest is in Abel's wagon. The struck-out name at the bottom, **Hayle, R.**, is Abel's nephew, Robb, who went to the Dusk Acres last Carving; a clerk copied his name from Brawn's culling roster onto the wrong form. A Fatlander, or anyone who asks the drovers, can learn that Robb was Abel's sister's boy, and that Abel paid for the wheat tattoo on his forearm when he turned sixteen.

**Dusk: Nib.** Nib Furrow walks out onto the marbled yard at sundown, shaking, because the room voted and he is a Fatlander and Fatlanders eat what they are served. He has Brawn's candied fat in his mouth. He keeps saying "it's all right, it's all right." The characters can stop him, stand with him (two or more people together are not alone, and the Reaping does not take them), or watch. If they stand with him all through the dusk, the field takes no one that night, and Brawn writes in his little book, without irritation, that the roster has been disrupted and the field will be hungrier tomorrow. If they watch, it is Dread 3, and the boy does not go quietly; he goes calling for his mam, and then very quietly.

**Night: barrel 31.** Late, the drovers beg Abel for meat. A character awake in the yard sees him prise the lid off barrel 31 by lantern-light, stand very still for a long time, reach into the brine to the elbow, take something out, look at it, and lay it back as gently as a man laying a sleeping child in a cot. He closes the lid. He sits down on the wagon-tongue in the falling white and does not move for an hour. If a character speaks to him, he says only, "It's nothing. It's pork. Go to bed, friend." If they look in the barrel themselves, it is Dread 3.

### Day Three: The Teamster

In the morning count, Abel Crumm does not answer. Big Oona finds him in the cold store. Do not describe how he died. Describe what she does: how she sits down on the flags beside him and takes off her own coat and puts it over him, though he is past cold; how she says his name, the way she has said it for thirty years, to call him to breakfast; how she will not let anyone else come in for the first hour; how, when she does come out, she has his pipe in her mouth, unlit, and his tobacco in her fist. Finding him or coming upon Oona with him is a Dread 2 check. Abel left a note pinned to the inside of his coat, addressed to his sister.

[fiction]
Handout: Abel Crumm's Note.
Mags.
I opened one. I wasn't meant to and I did. It was Robb's arm, the one with the wheat on it that I paid for. I'd know it anywhere. I've known it was something for thirty years and I didn't look, and the not looking was the thing I was best at. I've fed it to people. I've eaten it myself on the road and said it was good.
I don't know how to drive the wagons back and I don't know how to drive them on.
Don't let them tell you it was wolves. It was me. I'm sorry I won't be home for the Weighing. Tell Oona the pipe is hers and she's to stop pretending she doesn't smoke it.
Lack keep away from you. It never kept away from us.
Abel
[/fiction]

**Afterward.** Abel's death is the first time the inn's madness spreads person to person, and it should be shown through the living. The drovers sit together in the Long Room and do not eat. Haverick enters the death, and then the column he has drawn himself, *He was a kind man*, and then sits for a while with his pen above the page. That evening, Gus Haslet says he can hear the barrels in the yard "saying grace, like at home, thanking the field by name." By night, Tam Pettle can hear it too. Big Oona stands at the window over the yard with Abel's pipe, unlit, in her mouth, and says, to nobody, "He always went first through a gate so the beasts would follow." Any character who spends time with the drovers must make a Dread 2 check; on a failure they too hear the barrels murmuring, very softly, all night, the names of fields, and the names of the people the fields gave back.

[pull] It was me, he says. It wasn't him. It was all of us. We all drove. We all ate. He just went and said it, the daft old sod, he just went and said it first. | — Gus Haslet, in the Long Room

**The barrels opened.** By afternoon the inn has had no bread for a day and the pottage pot is scraped dry. Brawn has the barrels broached. He does not say which are which. Unless the characters stop it, the starving inn eats from the notched barrels by evening (see Eating from the Barrels), and it is the best meal anyone has had in their lives. Hepzibah weeps at the stove. Kale cooks for Refrain from an honest barrel, carefully, and she can smell the difference, and eats, and hates him, and asks for more. A character who stops the meal, or who throws the notched barrels open in the yard so the whole house can see what floats in them, earns the undying hatred of the hungry and a Dread 3 check for everyone who looks: a hand with a wedding band sunk into the swollen finger; a face, Rootkin-smooth, eyes closed, mouth a little open; a coil of something packed like sausage in its own brine.

**Dusk: Oona.** Big Oona is not on the roster. At sundown she walks out onto the marbled yard anyway, with Abel's pipe, and stands where Walt stood, and waits. She is not making a choice anyone else can make for her, she says, if the characters reach her; she is only going after him, the way he went first through gates. This is the adventure's echo of the suicide, and it should be played as the pull of grief, not as a test the players pass or fail. Characters who stay beside her, talking, keep the field off her for the night (Tongue + Persuade, Hard 14, to bring her back inside; Grace: she gives the pipe to the character, and lives to the end). If no one goes, she is Reaped, singing a field hymn, slowly, the verse the Board does not like.

[fiction]
She is singing when the white comes up over her boots. Her voice is high and clear and does not shake. *Lay us down in good ground, when we're done with being hungry.* The pipe is in her teeth. She takes it out to sing the next line and puts it back in to listen, the way you listen for an answer at a gate. Her face, in the last light, looks as though she has heard one.
[/fiction]

**Night: the Steward.** An hour after full dark, someone sees a lantern on the Hungry Lane, coming south, and the marbling melting away from the road around it in a widening black margin, steaming. Welcome Ardent walks into the yard, knocks at the door though it is open, and asks Absalom, very politely, whether the house has been laid. Then he sees the table.

[fiction]
He stands in the doorway of the common room for a long time with one white-gloved hand over his mouth. He does not come in. He looks at the table the way a cook looks at a dish someone else has made in his kitchen, and then the way a man looks at the place where his house was. "I didn't lay this," he says at last, and his voice stops, and starts again, like a clock that has been shaken. "I always lay ahead. I always have. A mile ahead. Forgive me. Forgive me. He's never gone ahead of me before this year. Is there, I wonder, is there somewhere I might sit? Not there. Not at that. A stool. Anywhere. Thank you. No. I shouldn't thank you. Forgive me."
[/fiction]

Seeing Ardent understood is Dread 3. He answers questions honestly and wearily. He confirms the rules of the Seated. He says he did not lay this table, and that he does not understand why the Host has gone ahead of him. Asked about the ninth chair, he says only, "I set that one myself, at every table. I always have. I've never set the card." He looks at the card for a long time and does not touch it. Asked who it is for, he says, "We're expecting one more," and then, after a long silence, "I was told that when they come, I may rest." He does not say it as though he believes it any more.

## Act Two: Hunger Indoors (Days Four to Six)

### Day Four: Open Graves

**The graveyard.** In the morning, whoever goes out first sees the graveyard wall split and three graves open. Old Mother Ivy's has been dug from above; the spade is still leaning on the wall, and the shroud lies in the trench empty and folded, because Absalom could not bear to leave it in a heap. The two oldest, Hadley's and her son's, have burst from beneath: the marbling has come up through the coffins in soft white veins, and of the bones only the long ones remain, licked smooth and pink, as if something below had sucked them like sweets.

[fiction]
The coffin wood has gone soft and fibrous, like the inside of a loaf. In the bottom of Hadley Thimble's there is a hollow in the shape of a lying woman, lined with a fine white down, warm when you put your hand into it. Something is still breathing through it. The breath smells of the pottage. Her headstone has fallen forward into the trench, face down, so that the one word carved on it, *Welcome*, is pressed into the white, and when you lever it up the word has been printed there in reverse, raised, like a seal in wax.
[/fiction]

The graves are Dread 2; understanding that Ivy's was dug by a spade, and connecting it to yesterday's meal, is Dread 3. Following the spade's prints (Eye + Search, Hard 14) leads to the kitchen door. Hepzibah, if confronted, does not deny anything. She says that Mother was a keeper of this house, and a keeper's duty is to the guests, and that if they would rather have starved, they might have said so before the second bowl.

**The great sermon.** At noon, in the yard, Sister Clemency preaches to the starving, and the inn listens. Roll her Sermon. On Day Four she sways most of the drovers and a few of the guests.

[fiction]
Handout: The Sermon of Sister Clemency Voide, Preached in the Yard at Thimble Cross.
Children. Brothers. Sisters with your bellies cramping. I will not lie to you, because you have been lied to enough. You are full. You are the fullest people who have ever lived. Your grandmothers' grandmothers swallowed God and you have been carrying It ever since, warm in your guts like a stone in a hen. That is why you can never eat enough. You are not hungry. You are pregnant with a corpse.
And now the one God that was never eaten has come to supper. Why here? Because It can smell Its brothers in you. Because It is a Host, and a Host goes where the larder is. It has come to fetch Its family home out of your bellies, and It does not care if It must take your bellies with them.
There is one way, and it is the oldest way. Give It back. Open your mouths and give It back. Fast and bleed and purge until the god in you comes up black and stinking and lies on the stones where it belongs, and when the Host comes on Tablenight, let It find nothing in this house but clean, empty people, and pass us by.
And those who are fullest, those whose blood is thickest with the stolen meat, the high and the gloved and the veiled: they must be emptied first, and most, because the Host can smell them from the Fast. It is not murder to open a jar. It is housekeeping.
Lack keep away? No. Lack come in. Lack is the only clean guest we have ever had.
[/fiction]

At the climax of the sermon she walks into the common room, kneels at the head of the Host's table, puts her fingers down her throat, and vomits a long black string of bile across the white cloth, and then, while the converts watch from the doorway, lays her palm in it and draws the root-mark of *worship-and-eat* on the cloth with her finger, and spits on it. Witnessing this blasphemy is Dread 2. In the morning the cloth is spotless, and the table is a little longer, and there is a new place at it, with a card. It has her name on it, in the hand of the midwife who delivered her. Clemency reads it, and smiles, and says that God is a very patient host.

[pull] I spat in its cup and it laid me a place. You see? You see? It doesn't know how to be insulted. That's how you know it's not a god at all. It's a mouth. | — Sister Clemency Voide, Day Five

**The ring.** In the afternoon, scrubbing out the pottage pot, Linus finds his grandmother's wedding ring. A character who is in the kitchen sees him stand with it in his palm for a full minute. He says nothing. He goes out to the graveyard, and looks, and comes back, and sits at the deal table with the ring standing on its edge in front of him, and turns it, and turns it.

**Dusk: the Gleanings.** The dusk standing on Brawn's roster is forgotten; nobody will go out tonight, not even for the arithmetic, because at sundown the people the field has taken come back to the windows. See Combat Encounters. They ask to be let in, in their own voices, and Absalom, Hepzibah, Linus and Dorcas cannot refuse a guest.

[fiction]
The first knock is at the kitchen door, three soft raps, the way a neighbour knocks. Then a voice, Walt Sallow's, mild and interested: "Hello the house. Bit nippy. Any room?" Then another at the west window, and you see it: a face pressed to the pane, the eyes closed, the skin smooth and pale and faintly ridged like the rind of a cheese, and below the sill, where its feet should be, a tangle of pale roots that move in the white like fingers looking for a dropped coin. It is Big Oona, or the field's idea of her. It is smiling. Its breath does not fog the glass. "Abel's here," it says. "He's ever so much better. Let us in, my loves, we've come for supper."
[/fiction]

The Thimbles open the doors because they must, and the bones in their hands creak when they try not to. The characters will have to hold doors, block windows, or fight in the kitchen and the yard. The Gleanings are Dread 2 to face, and Dread 3 if one wears a face the viewer knew: Walt, Oona, Nib if he was taken, Robb Hayle with his wheat tattoo, Old Mother Ivy with the barley still in her hair.

**Night: the kin-slaying.** After the Gleanings are driven off, Linus goes down to the cellar where his father is broaching a notched barrel for tomorrow. If characters follow, they arrive as Linus brings the cleaver down.

[fiction]

The first blow takes him where the neck meets the shoulder and goes in to the spine, and the sound is the sound he taught his son to listen for, the clean give of a joint. He falls into the barrel. Brine and blood come over the rim together in a pink sheet across the flags. Linus pulls him out by the apron strings, because he cannot leave his father in there with the others, and holds him, and his father's face is shining with the brine and looks, for a moment, enormously relieved. In the barrel, disturbed, something pale turns slowly over in the brine and settles again, face up.
[/fiction]

Witnessing the killing is Dread 3. Linus does not fight unless someone tries to take the body from him; then use his quick line, at +2, until he is restrained or Ruined. He sits with his father until dawn, holding his hand, and he talks to him, about horses, about the price of iron, about the time Absalom carried him on his shoulders to see the Company congress in 638, and about nothing else at all.

**The oath on the dead.** At dawn Hepzibah comes down. She washes Absalom's face, closes his eyes, and folds his hands over the wound. ### Day Five: The Ninefold Purge

The marbling reaches the cellar, climbing the stairs one step an hour. The inn divides: the swayed with Clemency, who now preach in pairs in the corridor and leave basins outside doors; the Board's people with Brawn, who has moved his chair to the toll house to be near the strongbox and the warden; the Thimbles with their dead, laid out on the deal table in the kitchen under a clean cloth, because the cold store is no longer safe. Bell Lisle is blinking every few minutes now. Absalom's name is called in the morning count, and there is a silence, and then Hepzibah answers for him, "Here," in a perfectly ordinary voice.

Kale, sensing the house turning, moves the cage. Clemency's converts have started to gather at the stable door at night, looking at Refrain with an interest he recognises from the surgeries, and he does not like competition. He asks the characters, cheerfully, whether they would like to earn a meal by standing a watch. If they agree, they are guarding Refrain from the Purgation for a man who has sold her to the Second Table. Kale finds this very funny, and then, when nobody laughs, less funny.

[pull] Everybody wants her for something. Me, the widow, the Sister, the room. Nobody wants her for supper but me. Funny old world. Funny old, cold old world. | — Kale Dragoman, on watch, Day Five

**Night: the ritual killing.** After midnight, Clemency and four converts take Lord Casimir from his room with a blanket over his hands and carry him to the smokehouse. He does not cry out. He has been expecting someone. If the characters have befriended Casimir, or are watching Clemency, they can intervene; Clemency's converts fight with fervour, and Casimir, bound with his hands in a sack, can do nothing until freed. If no one comes, Vellum finds him at dawn.

[fiction]
They have hung him from a meat-hook by the bound wrists, so that his toes just touch the brick. The floor is set with nine pewter basins in a ring, and each one holds something that came out of him, in order, the way the Purgation teaches: brine he was made to drink and bring up; bile; the black of his last meal; blood from the lancet at each elbow; blood from the throat; and then, in the last four, what she took out of him with the long knife, set out neatly, the way a cook sets out the parts of a fowl, the liver dark and glossy as a plum, the coils of him laid in a bowl and still faintly steaming in the cold. His gloves have been cut off. [/fiction]

The scene is Dread 3. Clemency does not run. She explains, kindly, that it had to be done, and that it has not worked yet, and that it needs another, a higher one; she looks at Vellum. If anyone tries to seize her she offers her wrists, and her converts do not; see Combat Encounters. She does not seem to know that two of her converts are weeping. One of them, a drover called Tam Pettle, sits down in the doorway of the smokehouse when it is over and will not get up, and says to anyone who passes that he did not know a man had so much in him, he did not know there was so much in a man.

**Vellum's oath.** At dawn Lady Vellum Serrat takes the gilt wire from her tongue in front of the smokehouse door, kneels in Casimir's blood, lays her hand on his empty chest, and swears on the dead, aloud, without a single condition, for the first time in her life.

[fiction]
"By this dead man, Casimir Hollowe-Vane, my cousin of the Table, I swear the Host shall be carved, and he shall have the first slice."
(Her tongue splits a quarter-inch at the tip with a sound like a fingernail on a slate, and bleeds down her chin, and she does not wipe it.)
[/fiction]

Her tongue keeps her promise for her. From now on she will do anything to bring the Host to its seat at Thimble Cross, and she becomes the loudest voice for filling the ninth chair. She does not put the wire back. She speaks plainly now, and the plainness is terrible, because everything she says is true and she says all of it: what she paid for Refrain, what the Second Table intends, what she thinks of each person in the room, what she has wanted, in her life, and not had. ### Day Six: The Seeming

Bell Lisle's last vial is gone by morning. Her nudger, if she has one among the characters, is all that keeps her upright, and she is blinking every minute, then every breath, and with every blink the Seeming spreads. Everyone in the common room rolls Caul + Resolve (Hard 14) each hour; those who fail see what Bell sees.

[fiction]
At the table the dead are sitting down to dinner. Walt Sallow with his clothes on and nothing in them. Abel Crumm, tucking his napkin in. Old Mother Ivy, with the barley still in her hair. Absalom, with his apron on and the wound in his neck very neat, carving. Casimir with his hands in his lap, palms together. They are passing the bread. They are so happy. They are saying, in the voices of people you have loved, that there is room, that there is always room, that you look thin, that you should come and sit down. Outside the west windows, something very tall is stooping to look in, and its breath fogs every pane at once, and it is warm, and it is counting the chairs.
[/fiction]

The Seeming is Dread 3 for each new witness, and contagious: anyone who Breaks while under it gains the derangement *I am already at the table* (they believe their body is seated, wasting, smiling, and that the self walking about the inn is a thing left behind; treat as **The Dead Man** and **The Pull** together, triggered by the smell of bread). By afternoon, half the inn shares it. Hepzibah sets places at the pine tables for the dead. Gus Haslet carries a plate of nothing to the empty chair where Abel used to sit and cuts it up small for him, because Abel's teeth were bad. Nobody stops him. Several people thank him.

## Act Three: The Ninth Chair (Day Seven)

### The Demand

On the night of Day Six, or the morning of Tablenight, the inn meets in the common room around the Host's table, because there is nowhere else warm. The consensus builds out of every frightened voice in the house, and the Carver should let each speak:

- **Clemency** says the Host will pass a house that offers it a clean guest: a mouth with no god in it. The ninth card is for the empty one.
- **Brawn** says this is only the dusk roster by another name, and sound husbandry: feed the table one by choice and it will not take nine by force.
- **Vellum**, sworn and unbitted, says that a full table is a table the Host must come to sit at, and that she will pay any price to fill it, and says exactly why, which nobody hears, because nobody wants to.
- **Hepzibah** says the house cannot refuse a guest, and a chair laid empty in her house is a guest refused.
- **Ardent**, asked directly whether filling the ninth chair will spare the others, says, with terrible gentleness, "I don't know. Nobody has ever sat there." The room hears what it needs to.

The demand settles on Refrain, because she is Unfed, because she is caged, because she has said herself that the card bears her people's name, and because she belongs to nobody in the room. Kale will not give up his property for nothing; Vellum's note already binds him to deliver her to this table, and he would like, he says, to be paid twice. What follows is an auction in reverse: the frightened inn passes a hat for the price of a woman.

[box: If the Players Fight the Demand]
They may. Freeing Refrain from the cage and holding the stable against Kale's men and Clemency's converts is a fine, desperate scene (see Combat Encounters). It changes nothing that matters. If Refrain is free, she listens to the room arguing over who else should go: Merrit, whose card is on the table; Dorcas, who has asked to; a character. "Because I'm the only one here who knows how to sit at that table and still mean no." If the players have kept her caged and treated her as stock, she is dragged to the chair instead, by drovers who will not meet her eyes, and that is worse.
[/box]

[box: Other Volunteers]
If Refrain is dead, gone, or the players have made another figure the heart of the story, the volunteer is whoever they love most. Merrit Sloe volunteers because her card is already on the table and she reckons she might as well sit in a better chair. Dorcas Thimble volunteers because the ninth chair is the one place on earth her oath cannot follow her. Haverick volunteers, in the Company's form, because a warden who gives himself keeps the peace by definition. ### The Sacrifice

At dusk on Tablenight the volunteer is washed, as the Fast washes its dead, standing, in cold water, by someone who does not speak; Hepzibah lays a clean napkin over their arm. Ardent, who cannot refuse to help lay a table, draws the ninth chair out from the table for the first time in six hundred and forty years. It makes a small sound on the flags. Everyone in the room makes a Dread 2 check.

[fiction]
Refrain does not thank it. She sits down in it the way a woman sits down in a surgeon's chair, all at once, with her jaw set, and lays her axe across her knees. Nothing happens. The bread steams. The lamps burn. Somebody laughs, high and frightened, and stops. Refrain looks at the plate in front of her, which is empty, and says, "No, thank you," and then again, and then a third time, and her voice cracks on the third, because the smell of the bread is coming up off the empty plate and she has been hungry her whole life. She does not say it a fourth time. Her hand is shaking on the axe. Ardent, in the corner, has begun to untie his apron, very slowly, with his eyes shut, as though he has been told he may. Outside, the marbling across the yard begins, very slowly, to melt, in a long dark path from the north gate to the door.
[/fiction]

### The Host

At midnight the Host comes, as it was always going to.

The door opens without a hand on it. The room goes warm all at once, warm as a kitchen on baking day, and every hungry person in it feels their mouth flood. Something stoops in the doorway, too tall for it, and comes in. Nobody sees its face. Characters who try to look straight at it see only the impression of a broad, kind, bending shape, an apron, enormous patient hands, and the light of the lamps leaning toward it. To be in the presence of the Host is a Dread 4 check; to look for its face is Dread 5. Every character must also make an Invitation check (Grim 18, or Dire 22 within reach of their own chair) to keep from walking to their own place and sitting down, and Refrain's Gift may help one of them.

The Host goes first to the ninth chair. It stands over the volunteer for a long moment. Then, gently, the way you would lift a sleeping child from a chair that belonged to a grandparent, it lifts them out. It is not angry. It is something like sorry.

Ardent, in the corner, with his apron half untied, whispers, "Oh. Oh, no. That isn't anybody's. It's not for sitting in." And ties his apron again.

The Host pushes the ninth chair back in, square to the table, its back to the lamp. Then it lays another place. The table is a little longer than it was. There is a new plate, a new chair pulled out a hand's width, and a new card in a fine round hand, and the name on it is the volunteer's, and the handwriting is their mother's. The volunteer reads it. Their face changes. They sit down at their own place, and lift the empty fork, and put it in their mouth, and close their eyes.

[fiction]
"Oh," says Refrain Dole, who broke two hundred and six tables, who never once in her life ate until she was full. "Oh, it's bread. It's just bread. It's warm. Why did nobody tell me it was just bread?" She is crying. She is smiling. She takes another bite of nothing. The axe slides off her knees and rings on the flags and she does not look down. "You should sit down," she says to you, kindly, with her mouth full. "There's enough. I didn't know there would be enough."
[/fiction]

Watching this is Dread 4. It is the moment the players understand the sacrifice bought nothing. Any character who paid into the Hat, carried the volunteer to the chair, or argued for the choosing faces Dread 5 instead: knowing that what you did changed nothing. The ninth chair is still empty. Their own cards are still on the table. The Host turns, warm and patient, toward the rest of the room, and the invitation stands. Outside, at the edges of the dark path, the marbling is still hungry, and the Reaping will take anyone who flees across it alone before dawn. Nothing has changed except that there is one more guest at the table, and she is happy, and she will be happy for a hundred years.

[bigquote] I didn't know there would be enough. | — Refrain Dole, Seated, Tablenight 641

From here, play out the night. Each hour until dawn, every character inside the inn makes an Invitation check to stay out of their chair; Seated NPCs call to them by name; the hungry and the Seemed walk past them to sit. The Host does not compel anyone. It only waits, and lays more food, and is kind.

## Combat Encounters

**The Gleanings (Day Four, dusk).** Six figures come out of the marbled yard to the windows and doors: field-grown things shaped like the Reaped, with root-tangled feet, smooth rind for skin, and the faces of Walt Sallow, Robb Hayle, Old Mother Ivy and others, eyes closed. They knock and ask to come in. If refused, they press against the glass until it bows, and come through. They fight to drag the living out onto the marbled ground at dusk. They are destroyed when their face is cut away; what is left smells of bread and sap, and under the rind there is a grain like the inside of a turnip, and in the middle of each, curled, a small hard knot that is nearly a tooth.

[quick] Gleaning — Threat 2 · Flesh 12 · Guard 11 · Attack +5 (root-grip 3; on a hit the target is held and dragged one step toward marbled ground each round, Gut + Athletics Hard 14 to break free) · Armor 1 · Dread 2 (3 if it wears a face the viewer knew)

**The Stable (Day Six or Seven).** Freeing Refrain means facing Kale and his two men, and, after Day Four, three of Clemency's converts who want her for the chair. Kale fights to disable, not kill: dead Blanks sell for a quarter. He uses the net first and the cosh second. If outmatched he surrenders and offers to sell the characters something, and then, if they will not buy, to cook for them. On Day Six or later, the oxen in the stable may join in, standing up on two legs in their stalls when nobody is looking straight at them; use their quick line, and let them fight anyone who is turned away.

**The Smokehouse (Day Five, night).** Clemency and four converts, armed with lancets. Clemency does not stop preaching while she fights. Each round, one convert who hears her and is not attacked that round must roll Caul + Resolve (Hard 14) or keep fighting even at Ruin. If Casimir is freed and ungloved, he can end the fight with a touch, and the touch will end more than he means; he knows it, and he will ask a character to choose for him.

**The Cellar (Day Four, night).** Linus, grieving and terrible, with the cleaver, between the characters and his father's body. Talking him down (Tongue + Persuade, Grim 18) ends the fight; a Grace gets the cleaver handed over, handle first, and Linus says "thank you" and then, appalled, "I shouldn't have said that, should I? Not here."

**The Seated (Tablenight).** Anyone who tries to drag a Seated person from their chair must beat them in an opposed Gut + Brawl; the Seated cling with a terrible gentle strength and do not stop smiling. A Seated person dragged free dies within the hour, of all their hunger arriving at once, and spends the hour asking to be allowed back.

## Endings

None of these endings is happy. Some are survivable. Whatever happens, describe the dawn: the warmth going out of the air at once, like a door shut on a kitchen; the marbling slumping into a stinking yellow slush and running away down the Rim Road in rivulets; the sound, from inside the inn, of cutlery on china, which does not stop.

### Ending One: No, Thank You

The characters refuse. They take Refrain's lesson and turn their backs on the table, and, discovering that the dark, warm path the Host walked in on is the one stretch of ground the Reaping cannot touch, they go out and stand on it, outdoors, with their backs to every door, from midnight until dawn, as the Unfed do on Tablenight. Behind them, through the open door, they hear the Seated praising the food, calling their names, asking why they will not come in. Each hour, a Dread 3 check, and an Invitation check at Grim 18 when a voice they love calls. Let them hold hands. Let them say the Decline together, aloud, every hour, and let the Unfed among them lead it.

At dawn the warmth goes out of the air and the marbling melts and the inn is silent but for the cutlery. Inside, at a table now seventeen places long, sit everyone who could not refuse: the Thimbles first, because they never could. The characters survive. Thimble Cross becomes a little Long Table, and the Company strikes it from the ledger.

[fiction]
You turn round when the sun is up, because the Unfed say you may. Through the open door the common room is full of light. They are all there. They look well. Refrain lifts her empty fork to you in salute, and her eyes are bright, and her mouth is full of nothing, and she says, "You're letting the cold in."
[/fiction]

### Ending Two: The Steward's Apron

A character speaks to Ardent through the long night and learns that he can be relieved. He describes the position fairly. He does not hide the hours. If a character accepts it, in so many words, Ardent takes off his apron and folds it and hands it over, with the nine keys and the box of cutlery, and weeps with relief, and sits down at the table himself, at a place that was always there and that nobody noticed was his. His card is in a child's hand, very large, the letters uneven. He reads it and puts it in his breast pocket, and lifts his fork. At dawn the new steward walks north up the Hungry Lane a mile ahead of something tall and warm, and does not come back. The character becomes an NPC. The Host, now, has a steward who knows the players' names, and who writes their cards in a hand they will recognise. Everyone else at the inn who was not Seated survives.

### Ending Three: The Long Table

The characters sit. Perhaps the Seeming has them; perhaps the Laden Taste; perhaps they are simply tired, and cold, and it is just bread. It is the warmest night of their lives. They will be lucid for months. They will describe each course to the next travellers who reach Thimble Cross, and to the ones after that. This ending is a gentle total party loss, and players who choose it with open eyes should be allowed to. Play the First Course for a scene, as the Fast chapter describes: let each player describe what they are eating, and who is sitting beside them, and what they say. Then let the next session, if there is one, be played by new characters, arriving at Thimble Cross in Grace, finding the old ones at the table, and being invited.

### Ending Four: Ashes and Tablecloth

The characters burn it down: the table, the common room, the inn, the Seated in their chairs, who do not get up and do not stop smiling and go on praising the food as the cloth catches. The smell is roasting, and then bread, and then something no one will ever describe. The Thimble charter breaks with the house, and so do the last Thimbles who are not already at table, their bodies folding and snapping in the yard as the blood fails to endure; Haverick, failing to keep the peace, breaks too, quietly, in the toll house, having written *relieved* in the ledger beforehand. The characters run south across melting marbling at dawn, and the field takes one more of them if it can. Burning the Seated is Dread 4, and an oath on the dead sworn by anyone present is broken by it. They survive. At the next crossroads down the Rim Road, two days later, there is a table, laid, with their names on the cards.

### Ending Five: The Struck Line

A rarer ending, for groups who have spent their time on the Thimbles. If the characters have found Dorcas her exit clause (see After the Snow: the Company's striking of the station ends the charter's object), and have kept her standing until dawn, and have stood with her on the dark path through the night, she walks out at sunrise between the boundary stones and nothing breaks. She stands in the road and touches her own face, her own collarbones, her own wrists, counting. Then she turns and looks at the inn where her mother and brother sit at the table, and the oath she swore on her father's body comes due. It is a Fatlands-ground oath, broken: from now on she tastes her father in every meal. She eats anyway. She goes south to Sated to read law. She does not come back.
