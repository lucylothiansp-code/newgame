# Before You Sit Down

> We were hungry. Say it plainly. We were hungry, and they were there.
>> — first line of every history written on the Table

[fiction]
My grandmother told it to me the way her grandmother told it to her, with the shutters closed and a crust of bread between us that neither of us was allowed to touch until she had finished.
"There were eight of them," she said, "and they lived with us the way the weather lives with us. You did not pray to them. You did not need to. You woke up and they were there, as big as the hill behind the house, and they were kind, and they were warm, and they fed us."
"Then they stopped," I said, because I knew the story.
"Then everything stopped. Nine years. The seed lay in the furrow like teeth in a dead mouth. The nets came up holding only water. The cows went dry, and then the women did. We boiled harness. We boiled the glue out of the chairs. Your great-great-great-grandfather's sister ate the clay from the wall in the corner where the fire had baked it sweet, and it set in her like a stone, and she died holding her belly like a woman carrying." She looked at the crust. "And on the ninth winter we went up the hill with knives."
"Did they fight?"
"No," she said. "That is the part nobody can stand. They lay down. They lay down like a dog lies down for the hand it knows, and they let us."
She told me the rest in a whisper, because it was not a story for children and she was telling it anyway. How the first cut steamed in the snow. How the fat under the skin of a god is yellow as butter and smells of every kitchen you were ever happy in. How the blood did not run so much as well up, slow and warm, and the people knelt in it to keep their knees from freezing. How nobody spoke, and then everybody did, and the sound of a whole village chewing in the dark was like rain on a thatch. How her grandmother's grandmother had been given a piece no bigger than a thumb, from somewhere near the knuckle, and had wept while she ate it because it was the best thing she had ever tasted and she knew she would never taste it again.
"There were eight," I said. "You said eight. But there are only seven lands."
My grandmother picked a crumb off the table and put it on her tongue, slowly, like a woman taking communion, and did not answer, and that was the first time I understood that the story had a hole in it the size of a chair.
She pushed the crust toward me. I said the grace, the way you must: *Lack keep away.* I ate it. It tasted like bread. It tasted like bread for eleven more years, and then one morning it tasted like something that had been waiting for me.
[/fiction]

## The World in One Breath

Six hundred and forty-one years ago, at the end of a famine that lasted nine years, the peoples of the world killed their gods and ate them. Six peoples did. One refused.

It saved them. It also did not stay down. A god cannot be digested, only delayed. In every land, the god that was eaten is growing back: through the bloodlines of the people who swallowed it, through the soil their dead were buried in, through the weather over their heads. Each land is now a culture living inside the slow, wrong resurrection of its own meal.

The poets of the Table have a name for the age that followed. They call it **the Long Grace**: the pause between the blessing and the first bite, the breath a family holds before it is permitted to eat. Six centuries of borrowed time, while the meal sits in the belly of the world and remembers what it was. Everyone alive is descended from someone who ate. The monster is in the blood, and it is waking up. The Grace is ending.

[pull] The grace is the shortest prayer we have, and the oldest, and the only one everybody still means. | — Celebrant Abel Corse, *Sermons at the Rim*

This book is a complete tabletop roleplaying game set in that world. It contains everything you need to play: the history and lands of the Table, the rules for making and ruining characters, a market to buy and sell in, a gallery of the things that hunt and are hunted, guidance for the person who runs the game, and an adventure to begin with.

## The Table at a Glance

The known world is one round continent, a ring around a hole. Six lands that ate their gods sit around the rim; the land that refused lies empty in the middle, and nobody crosses it. Everything that moves between them moves on the **Rim Road**, two thousand two hundred miles of toll highway owned by a merchant company. What follows is the Table as a traveller would sketch it on the back of a toll receipt.

### Sallowreach

[sigil: sallowreach]

Grey fen and black water under a fog called the Breath. The north ate Ossel, the Closing Hand, god of death, and since that night nothing there can finish. A cut throat does not kill; neither does drowning, fire or age. The Kept dead walk, talk and feel every hour of their rot, and outnumber the breathing nine to one. The Hand-line nobility sell the only death available, by license, at the price of a house. Now, near their estates, patches of fen have begun to die all at once, down to the sound.

*Said there:* "Nobody here is waiting to die. We are all waiting to be allowed."

### The Fatlands

[sigil: fatlands]

Warm, wet plains under a yellow sky, where the ground rises and falls underfoot like something breathing. The south ate Ummer, the Laden, god of harvest, and now the soil is the god's flesh and grows flesh: wheat with teeth in the ear, fruit with a bone for a pit. All of it is delicious and none of it fills. The dead are served at their own wakes. The Board of Plenty votes by body weight. Each Carving, people walk into the far fields at dusk and are not there in the morning. The Board says it is wolves. There are no wolves in the Fatlands.

*Said there:* "There is no word in our dialect for full. We had one. We ate it."

### Brinehollow

[sigil: brinehollow]

A grey coast of shingle and cliff, beside a sea that retreats a mile a year. The west ate Dromm, the Fathom, and now the sea is inside the people: twice a day their lungs fill with brine that must be coughed into a bucket. On certain nights a person walks into the surf and keeps walking. Some come back weeks later, heavier and courteous, with someone else behind their eyes, and these Tenanted sit as judges now. The capital is a pier nineteen miles long, chasing water that is not drying up but going somewhere.

*Said there:* "The sea is not going out. It is going in."

### The Vigil

[sigil: vigil]

One great city on a stone plateau, lit by ten thousand lamps, where there has been no night for three hundred years. The people ate Iss, the Lidded, god of sleep, and since 341 A.G. anyone who sleeps goes to one shared dream, a long room with a closed eye at the end, and never wakes. So nobody sleeps, by law, by drug, by needle. Marriage is a promise to pinch. The sleepers lie in sixty thousand numbered beds, breathing in time, and at some number the eye opens. The beds are nearly full.

*Said there:* "Pinch me."

### Cradlewrack

[sigil: cradlewrack]

Red-clay highlands, warm to the touch, smelling of iron. The northwest ate Vey, the Opening, god of birth and doors, and now nothing is born right and too much is born: infants who speak, infants born old, adults born mid-stride, single healthy hands. Men bear. Wells bear. Doors will not stay shut. The midwives of the Attendance rule with a knife called the Second Opinion, and the earthquakes are contractions nine days apart and closing.

*Said there:* "First we ask what it is. Then we ask whether it stays."

### Oathen

[sigil: oathen]

Red canyons under a hard white sky, cities cut into cliff walls, and a silence you can hear a sandal drop into. The east ate Tolm, the Witness, and now words bind flesh: break a promise and your body breaks to match, with a sound. Children are bitted until twelve. Polite speech is mostly exits. Every treaty on the Table is sworn here, because here it cannot be broken. This year, for the first time in six centuries, the god's promised rain came late.

*Said there:* "Say less."

### The Fast

[sigil: fast]

The rich heartland at the center: perfect farmland, mild and gold and almost empty. Its people would not eat Orrum, the Host, and nine in ten starved beside a willing meal. Their descendants, the Unfed, carry no god, eat once a day standing, and are insufferably proud. Orrum got up again. It still walks, very tall, stooping under doorways, laying tables wherever it passes. Those who sit do not get up. Lately the tables have place cards, the names spelled correctly, and some have been found in other lands.

*Said there:* "No, thank you."

[pull] Count the chairs before you count the plates. Count them twice if the room is warm. | — Rim Road innkeepers' saying

## What Kind of Game Is This?

*The Long Grace* is a horror roleplaying game. One player, the **Carver** (the game master, or GM: at a feast on the Table, the Carver is the one who decides who gets which portion), describes the world, plays everyone in it who is not a player character, and adjudicates the rules. Every other player creates and plays one **Guest**: a single person of the Table, with a land, a bloodline, a trade, debts, people they love, and a god in their veins that wants things.

Play is a conversation. The Carver describes a situation; the players say what their characters do; when the outcome is uncertain and matters, dice are rolled. Over a session or a long campaign, the characters investigate, travel the Rim Road, bargain, fight, flee, and make terrible choices, and the story that results belongs to everyone at the table.

It is not a game of heroes. There is no chosen one, no sword that kills a god, no cure that works. The characters are people trying to survive inside a catastrophe that is also their inheritance, and the most important rules in this book measure how their bodies change (**Hunger** and **Regrowth**), how their minds fray (**Fray** and **Derangements**), and what they are willing to do to each other to stay whole for one more day. Characters will be maimed. Characters will go mad. Characters will become something that is not a person anymore. Some of the best sessions you will play are the ones in which that happens.

What the game rewards is attention. The Table is built from small, specific horrors: the smell of cedar on a rich man's sleeve covering the smell beneath it, the neighbor who has stopped calling her name through the wall at dawn, the extra joint in a nobleman's finger, the chair that is warm when nobody has sat in it. Players who notice those things, and who let their Guests love something enough to be afraid for it, will find the game gives back more than it takes. Most nights.

### What You Need

- This book, or at least its rules chapters and the character sheet at the back.
- Two ten-sided dice (d10) per player, ideally of two different colors. Several six-sided dice (d6) in a third color: these are **Teeth**, rolled when a character calls on the god inside them.
- Pencils, erasers, copies of the character sheet, and scrap paper for maps, ledgers, and the names of the dead.
- Three to six people, one of whom has read the rules and at least the chapter on the land where the game begins.
- A card with a crossed fork drawn on it, in the middle of the table, for the Grace (see below).
- A few hours, and a willingness to sit with discomfort.

## Ten Things Every Guest Knows

Every child of the Table learns these before their letters. Guests know them whether or not their players do, and a Carver may simply remind a player of any of them, free, the way a mother would.

1. **Say the grace.** Nobody eats with a stranger without first saying *Lack keep away.* Skipping it is the universal insult, and in some inns the last one you will give.
2. **Never sit at a table you did not see laid.** If the plates are hot and the room is empty, walk out backward. If a chair is pulled out for you, do not thank it.
3. **The god goes where the blood goes.** A Brinehollower in a Sallowreach inn still bails at dawn. A Vigilant in the Fatlands still dares not sleep. You cannot leave your land behind; you are carrying it.
4. **In Sallowreach, "Long life" is a curse.** Say "May you finish," and mean it kindly. Do not ask the Kept how they are keeping.
5. **In Oathen, say less.** Speak in conditionals. Never exaggerate. Never say "I promise" to a child, and never, ever say "I'd die for you."
6. **In Cradlewrack, knock.** You knock to warn whatever is on the other side. Do not ask a child its name before its first year is out.
7. **In the Vigil, accept the pin.** A slap is a favor. Do not yawn where anyone can see. Do not lie down, even to die.
8. **In the Fatlands, clean the plate.** Leaving food is a fine; refusing a meal is sedition. Do not ask what it was. Ask whether it was good. It was always good.
9. **In Brinehollow, do not ask a returned person where they went,** and do not mention how anyone sounds when they breathe. If your neighbor does not answer the morning count, go and look, and take a rope.
10. **Count the chairs.** Every old carving on the Table has a place in it that has been chiselled out. Every old table has one chair more than it has people. Nobody will tell you why. Count them anyway.

[pull] My mother taught me nine of them. My grandmother taught me the tenth, when she was dying, and made me promise not to teach it to my children until they asked. | — a Lastgate stitcher, to a Cutwright surveyor

## Content and Consent

This is a game about eating gods and the gods eating back. It is, deliberately, one of the most disturbing settings ever printed for a tabletop game. Before anyone at your table makes a character, everyone should know what is in it.

[box: Content Warning]
*The Long Grace* contains, described on the page and in sensory detail: graphic body horror and gore (flesh, bone, rot, unwanted transformation); murder, including ritual killing, executions and kin-slaying; torture; cannibalism, both desperate and sacramental; grave desecration and the misuse of the dead; blasphemy and corrupted religion; human sacrifice, including sacrifices that turn out to have been for nothing; self-harm and suicide, presented as tragedy and never as instruction; contagious madness, psychosis, hallucination, paranoia, delusions of being dead or of loved ones being impostors, dissociation and lost time; cult control and gaslighting; starvation; possession; and a cosmology without rescue or redemption.
The setting also touches, thematically and never explicitly, on cursed and incestuous bloodlines, sexual taboo among adults, and desecration of the dead. These elements are always implied, off-page, and adult. Children exist on the Table and suffer its horrors; this book never sexualizes them and never lingers on graphic violence done to them.
[/box]

Horror is a collaboration. The point of all this darkness is not to wound the people at the table but to let them feel, safely and together, the weight of a world that cannot be saved. That only works if everyone trusts that the game will stop or turn when it needs to. Use the following tools. They are not optional extras; they are part of the rules.

### The Pre-Game Questionnaire

Before the first session, ideally before characters are made, the Carver hands round the list below. Each person privately marks each theme **L** for a Line (it does not appear at all), **V** for a Veil (it happens off-screen: the door closes, the scene cuts away), or **W** for Welcome (it may be shown, with care). Nobody explains a mark. The strictest answer anyone gives is the table's answer. The Carver merges the sheets into one list and destroys the originals, so nobody knows who marked what.

### Lines and Veils

From the questionnaire, the group keeps a short written list of **Lines** and **Veils**, visible through every session. Anyone may add to it at any time without explanation. The Carver honours it, including by rewriting this book. Every land and adversary here can be run with any of its themes veiled: a Fatlands campaign in which nobody ever sees the wake, only the clean plates afterward, is still a Fatlands campaign, and perhaps a worse one.

### Saying the Grace

Every meal on the Table begins with the words *Lack keep away*. At your table, the same words, or a tap on the card marked with a crossed fork, mean: *stop this, now.* Anyone may say the Grace at any moment, about anything, with no reason given and no discussion required. The Carver immediately removes or alters whatever was happening, and play continues. Nobody asks why. Saying the Grace is never an insult and never an inconvenience; it is the oldest courtesy in the world.

### Pass the Salt

A softer signal. A player who says *pass the salt*, or slides the salt cellar toward the Carver, asks for less lingering: fewer details, cut to the aftermath. It is a hand on the sleeve, not a stop. A player who wants a scene to stay longer or go darker may ask for a **second helping**, and the Carver honours that too, within the Lines.

### The Open Door

Any player may leave the table at any time without saying why, and nobody comments when they come back. In Cradlewrack the doors drift open at night and nobody is blamed for walking through. At your table the door is open on purpose.

### Checking In and Clearing the Plates

Every few sessions, take five minutes out of character: what landed well, what was too much, what people want more of. Horror that has stopped being fun for one person at the table has stopped being fun for the table. At the end of any session that went somewhere dark, **clear the plates**: each person names one thing they liked about their own Guest's night, one thing they liked about someone else's, and anything they need to put down before going home.

### On Suicide and Despair

The Long Grace contains the suicides of characters, in the setting's history and sometimes in play: the Finishers walking into the Hushes, the Called walking into the sea, the sleepless lying down on purpose. This book treats every one of these as a tragedy with a cost that falls on the living. It never describes how; it describes what it leaves behind. If you or someone at your table is struggling outside the game, stop the game. Real people matter more than any story, and help from people who are trained to give it is worth reaching for.

[pull] Nobody asks why. That is the whole of the courtesy. Nobody asks why. | — Rim Road tavern-keepers, on the grace

## How to Use This Book

The book is divided into four parts.

- **Part One: The Table** describes the world. *The Long Grace* chapter covers the history, the Providers, the Lack, the Gorging, the Regrowth, the Cuts, the Rim Road and the powers that cross borders. Seven chapters follow, one for each land: Sallowreach, the Fatlands, Brinehollow, the Vigil, Cradlewrack, Oathen, and the Fast. Each chapter covers its land's meal, its rules of horror, its history, factions, people, and how to play someone born there. *Threads Between the Lands* ties them together.
- **Part Two: The Rules** explains how to make a character, how dice are rolled, how the god in the blood is measured and spent, how the mind breaks, and how violence is done. It includes seven ready-to-play Guests, one from each land, for groups who want to start tonight.
- **Part Three: The Market and the Things That Eat** contains the full equipment and shopping system, the currencies of the Table, relics made from godflesh, and a bestiary of horrors, human adversaries and Seated gods.
- **Part Four: Setting the Table** is for the Carver alone: how to run horror, how to build campaigns, the secrets of the world, and the introductory adventure, *The Ninth Chair*.

At the back you will find the character sheet and an index.

If you are a player, read this chapter, the chapter on your Guest's land (or at least its *Playing* section), and *Making a Guest*. That is enough. Much of the pleasure of the Table is not knowing what the Carver knows. If you are the Carver, read everything, and then read *The Ninth Chair* twice.

## Words of the Table

In Table-speech, the common tongue older than the Gorging, the words for "worship" and "eat" share a root. Scholars insist they always did.

- **The Table:** the known world, one round continent with a dead heartland. Also any table where strangers eat, which is why the word is said carefully.
- **The Long Grace:** the age since the Gorging; borrowed time before the gods finish growing back. "The Grace is ending" is said at every bad harvest and late rain.
- **The Gorging:** the Night of Seven Tables, when six peoples ate their gods. Year 0 A.G. It is now 641 A.G.
- **Providers:** the eight gods who lived bodily among the peoples. Seven are named. The eighth is not.
- **The Lack:** the nine-year famine before the Gorging; also winter; also poverty, hunger and bad luck, which on the Table are one condition.
- **Lack, crumb, platter:** the bone coin worth a day's bread; a tenth of one; twenty of them, in a sealed tablet.
- **Regrowth:** the god's return through its eaters, in stages: the Taste, the Appetite, the Course, the Brink and the Seating. Its marks are *godsign*.
- **Hunger, Partaking, Teeth:** how awake the god is in you; calling on it before a roll; the d6 you add. A Tooth showing 1 means the god bit back.
- **Gifts, Wants, the Sop:** the god's powers in you; its compulsions; the act that quiets it and feeds the habit.
- **Cut:** a bloodline, named for the part its ancestors ate: Hand-lines, Gut-lines, Lung-lines, Eye-lines, Caul-lines, Tongue-lines. Higher Cut, more god.
- **Scraplings, Blanks, the Unfed:** descendants of the bone-lickers; the rare born with no godsign, hunted everywhere; the people of the Fast, who refused, and who have **Want** where others have Hunger.
- **The Kept, the Called, the Tenanted, the Counted, Parts, the Forsworn, the Seated:** the undying of Sallowreach; those who walk into Brinehollow's sea, and those who come back; the Vigil's sleepers; Cradlewrack's single living limbs; Oatheners broken by their words; those who sat at Orrum's tables.
- **Fray, Dread, Breaking:** how far a mind has worn; how terrible a horror is, rated 1 to 5; what happens at Fray 10.
- **Flesh, Guard, Ruin:** harm a body can take; how hard it is to hit; what happens when Flesh runs out.
- **Grace and Lack (dice):** doubles on a success, doubles on a failure. Double 1s are **Licked Clean**; double 10s a **Second Helping**.
- **Oath on the Dead:** a promise sworn on a dead person's name. Every Guest begins with one. The dead keep count.
- **Portions:** experience, spent between sessions.
- **Tablenight:** the anniversary of the Gorging, the longest night of Lack (winter). The other seasons are Grace, Plenty and Carving. The moon is **the Plate**; a new moon is "licked clean".
- **The Rim Road Company, the Cutwrights' College, the Purgation, the Second Table, the Reckoners:** the powers that cross every border: road-owners, genealogists, purgers, a hidden cult of nobles, and assassins out of the Fast.
- **Carver and Guests:** the game master (or GM) and the players' characters.

## A Night on the Table

The transcript below is lightly tidied from a first session. Dee is the Carver. Joss plays **Hessa Coombe**, a Sallowreach Seamer; Marta plays **Haslet Furrow**, a Fatlands Renderer; Kwame plays **Abate Kettering**, a Crumb-Runner of the Fast. All three are pregenerated Guests from *Making a Guest*. The scene: a crossroads where the Fatlands meet the edge of the Fast, at dusk, in Carving.

**Dee (Carver):** The road bends round a stand of toothed wheat to a crossroads: a milestone, an empty gibbet, a Company waystation with its shutters down. No lamp, no smoke. The cart you were following stands at the trough, and its horse is shaking. There's a smell on the wind. Abate knows it first.

**Kwame (Abate):** Bread.

**Dee:** Warm bread, a crust just cracking, and under it something roasting, sweet, like pork basted in honey.

**Kwame:** Abate turns so the wind's on her cheek and walks into it. Not toward the smell. Into the wind, like the rule says.

**Dee:** Every Unfed child learns that before their letters. No roll. Haslet, it hits you differently.

**Marta (Haslet):** I'm drooling. Can he tell what the roast is? He's a Renderer, that's Know the Cut.

**Dee:** Eye plus Feast, Hard, fourteen. If you make it you'll know whether it was a person.

**Marta:** Plus five. *(rolls)* Six and four. Fifteen.

**Dee:** No Helping, but you know. It's pork. Honestly pork. It's the best pork you've ever smelled, and the last time anything smelled this good you were eight and it was your grandfather's wake.

**Marta:** That is so much worse.

[box: What Just Happened]
Dee called for a roll only when the outcome was uncertain and mattered. Abate's walk into the wind needed none: it is what she was raised to do. Haslet's roll used his Calling Talent: 2d10 + Eye + Feast against Hard (14). Fifteen beats it by one, short of the full four needed for a Helping.
[/box]

**Joss (Hessa):** Is the waystation door open?

**Dee:** Hessa has Lidsense; she knows without looking. The door is shut. Every ground-floor shutter is open a finger's width, and so is every eye of the man on the porch. He's sitting at a table carried out onto the boards: white cloth, candles, four places, a fork in his hand with nothing on it. He says, very warmly, "There you are. We'd almost given up." He lifts the fork, chews, swallows. His cheeks are hollow as cups and he is the happiest man you have ever seen. Anyone who goes close enough to see his face makes a Dread check. Dread 3: Grim, eighteen.

**Joss:** Hessa's a Seamer, she has to look. I'll Partake. Caul 3, Resolve 2, plus five. *(rolls)* Three, eight, and the Tooth is a one. Oh no.

**Dee:** Eleven plus five plus one: seventeen. One short. Three Fray, and two Hunger: one for Partaking, one because the god bit back. What's in your hand?

**Joss:** The tin cup from the trough. She was going to water the horse.

**Dee:** Your hand closes on it. The tin crumples, the edge opens your palm, and you can't let go. For one heartbeat every sound at the crossroads stops: the horse, the wind, the man chewing. Then it comes back, and he says, "Mind your hand, love. Sit. There's a cloth."

**Joss:** She doesn't sit. She says, "May you finish," and she means it, and she hates that she means it.

**Dee:** Abate. Thirty paces into the wind there's a small table on the verge. One place, a card propped against the glass. You know the hand. It's your sister Meagre's. It says *Abate*, and under it, smaller, *I saved you the crust.*

**Kwame:** *(after a moment)* Lack keep away.

**Dee:** *(at once)* The card is blank. An ordinary laid table seen from the road: Hard fourteen, plus your Pangs.

[box: The Grace at Work]
Kwame said the Grace. He did not say why and nobody asked. Dee took the sister's handwriting out of the scene at once, kept the threat, and lowered the Difficulty to match what was now in the fiction. After the session Kwame mentioned, unprompted, that Dee could bring the card back someday. Dee wrote that down and did nothing with it for eleven sessions.
[/box]

**Kwame:** Pangs are 2, so sixteen. Caul 3, Resolve 3. I'll spend two Want on the Gnaw for the Empty Tooth. *(rolls)* Eight, four, Empty Tooth three. Twenty-one.

**Dee:** Clean refusal. Want went from three to one; refusing Orrum gives you two back.

**Kwame:** "No, thank you. No, thank you. No, thank you." She keeps walking and doesn't look back.

**Dee:** Behind you someone sighs, the way a host sighs when a guest leaves early, not unkindly. Haslet, you're the one still standing there. The man on the porch is pulling out the chair beside him. It scrapes. And the ground under your boots is warm and soft and rising and falling, just a little. What do you do?

**Marta:** I think he needs someone to hold his hand.

**Joss:** Hessa takes it. The one that isn't bleeding.

**Dee:** Then that's where we stop for a break. Somebody put the kettle on.

[pull] The rules are a knife and a fork. What you do with them is supper. | — from the Carver's notes to *The Ninth Chair*
