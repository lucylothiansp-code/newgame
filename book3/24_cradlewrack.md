Set down by Wenna Thrale, apprentice of the Attendance, third year of her apron, for the Lying-In's own book and not for the Tally. Rudge parish, the fourth night after a pang, Carving 641.
They sent for us at the second bell because the father had begun. Not the mother: she had been delivered of a girl in Grace and sat by the hearth with the girl at her breast and her face to the wall. It was the father on the floor, a shepherd's son named Corran with hands like spades, curled on the red clay in a shirt soaked black. The room smelled the way every highland room smells when something is coming: iron, wet wool, and under it the warm salt of the springs, as if the ground had broken its waters under the house.
Mother Sabeth went to him first. I went to the door, as I was taught, because the door had opened on its own when we came in and would not stay shut. I put my back to it. I could feel it pushing, very gently, the way a hand pushes from the inside of a belly. I have stood against a hundred doors and that is the only way I can describe it.
Corran's belly was not large. That was the worst of it. It was a man's flat belly, but the skin of it was moving in a long slow roll from the ribs to the groin, and each time it rolled he made a sound I have heard cattle make and never a man. Sabeth laid her palm on him and counted under her breath. Then she opened her case and laid out the things in the order we lay them out: the cord-thread, the basin, the clean linen, the bone hook, and last, wrapped in its own oiled cloth, the knife.
It took until the fourth bell. I will write only that he did not open where a woman opens, because he has nowhere to, and that he opened at an old scar on his flank from a ram's horn, twenty years shut and white, which parted now like a mouth deciding to speak. Sabeth had her arm in him to the elbow and her face was calm. The mother at the hearth did not turn around once. The baby at her breast did not cry once, but watched.
What came out was the size of a lamb and the color of the clay floor. It had a great many fingers. It drew breath. That is the thing nobody tells you before your apron, that they all draw breath, that the first sound out of the worst of them is the same small wet gasp as out of the best. Sabeth held it up in the lamplight and turned it, slowly, the way you turn a pot to look for cracks, and she asked the First Question aloud, because it must be asked aloud.
What is it.
Nobody answered. Nobody ever answers. She answered it herself, quietly, in the words of the Book, and then she was quiet for a long time. Corran had stopped making his sound. The door had stopped pushing against my back. The only noise in the room was the thing breathing and the clay ticking as it cooled. I counted eleven breaths. I have been told since that Sabeth Thorne never takes more than three. She looked at it for eleven.
Then she asked the Second Question, and she answered it, and she unwrapped the oiled cloth.
I am meant to write what I saw, for the Lying-In's book. I saw her turn her back to the hearth so the mother would not see. I saw her shoulder move once. I saw her wrap the linen and tie the knot we tie, the one that cannot come undone, and hand the bundle to me without looking at it, and I held it, and it was warm, and it stayed warm against my chest the whole cold walk back to the Lying-In, longer than it should have. I did not look either. I want that written down. I did not look.
Corran lived. We closed his flank with eight stitches, and he will have them redone at every pang for the rest of his life, because in Cradlewrack a wound opened once remembers how. In the morning the mother turned from the wall and asked what it had been. I said, as I am taught, that nothing had been born, that the Tally would show a son of the household entered and struck through. She nodded. Then she held up her girl, six months old and unnamed, and asked whether I thought this one would stay.
The girl looked at me. She has her father's eyes, very dark. She opened her mouth as if to answer.
I said that the naming waits a year. I said it the way I was taught. I put the knife-case back in Sabeth's bag myself because Sabeth's hands were busy at the basin, scrubbing, long after there was anything left on them to scrub.

## The Meal

Vey, the Opening, was the smallest of the Providers. The highland songs agree on that and on almost nothing else. It was no taller than a woman of middle height, and in the old carvings at Kest it is shown standing in a doorway with its arms spread to either jamb, so that the eye cannot tell whether it is holding the door open or holding something back. It had no face that anyone could keep in mind afterward. Those who saw it remembered only the feeling of being about to begin something: a breath drawn in and not yet let out, the half-second before a first word. Mothers in labor reached for it. It came. For three thousand years, the highlands say, no child in Vey's country was born without the god standing at the foot of the bed, its hands spread, its faceless attention fixed on the opening.

In the ninth winter of the Long Lack, Vey stood at the foot of every bed and nothing came. The wombs of the highlands had closed. Ewes stood dry and staring. Seeds lay in the red clay like teeth in a dead jaw. The god went from house to house with its hands spread, and the doorways it stood in were empty, and at last the midwives stopped calling it, because it was unbearable to watch it wait.

They took it in the birthing-house at Kest, the round red building that still stands at the heart of the city and is now called the Old Lying-In. They did not need to chase it. It came when the midwives called, the way it always came, and stood in the doorway of the birthing-room with its arms spread, and they shut the door on it. It was the first time anything had ever been shut against Vey. The songs say the god looked at the closed door for a long time, as if it had never seen one from that side.

Then they killed it, and it would not stay killed.

This is the part the highlands remember in their bodies. Vey was the hardest of the Providers to kill because it kept starting again. They opened its throat with a shearing knife and from the wound came, wet and blinking, a smaller Vey, whole, its arms already spread. They killed that one and it began again from its own wound, and again, each new god crawling up out of the opened body of the last like a child crowning. By dawn the floor was a drift of gods the size of hares, of mice, of fingernails, each still trying to begin, and the clay was ankle-deep in caul and cord and a clear warm fluid that smelled of iron and the inside of a mouth. The last was no larger than a barleycorn. The midwife who crushed it between her thumbnails, Hollan Kest, ancestor of every Midwife-Paramount since, is said to have wept and said, "There. Now nothing begins."

The songs do not spare the room. They say the smell was the smell of a lambing-shed in a hot spring, if the shed had been shut for a week: iron, milk, wet straw, and under it the sweet, flat, faintly fecal stink of things newly come out of a body. They say the floor of the birthing-room had been laid with fresh rushes that afternoon and that by midnight the rushes floated. They say the little gods made a sound as they crawled, a soft continuous clicking of tiny joints opening for the first time, and that the midwives who survived the night heard it in every hearth fire for the rest of their lives. They say that the smaller the god became, the harder it was to look at, because the smaller ones had faces, finally, and every face was the face of a child the woman looking at it had lost.

There were nine women in the room. The songs name them all, and the five great Caul-line houses and the House of Aubrey are each descended from one of them or from the households that stood at their backs. Hollan Kest held the knife. Grizel Aubrey held the door shut with her shoulders and was the first person in the history of the world to feel Vey push against a door from the other side; her granddaughter wore the first highland crown. Mab Morwen tended the copper and skimmed it. Ottilie Seele carried the bowls. Two Calloway sisters and a Danner cousin held the god down, again and again, each new god smaller than the last, until their aprons were stiff to the waist. The ninth woman is not named in any song, only called *the one who counted*, and the songs say she counted every Vey as it was killed, aloud, and that the number she reached is the number the Attendance still will not write down.

Then they ate it, because the Lack had to end. They ate it the way the highlands eat a lambing's afterbirth: stewed with clay salt in the great copper and ladled out by the senior midwife into bowls brought from home. The boldest households, pressed closest to the copper, came away with the choicest part, which was not flesh but the membrane that had sheathed the god's thousand rebirths, a grey-pink translucent skin the cooks skimmed off the pot like the skin on milk. These families ate the caul. They became the Caul-lines.

The famine ended in the morning. By midday the first ewes were dropping lambs in the snow. By the end of Grace every woman in the highlands who could carry was carrying, and by the end of Plenty every one of them had been delivered, easily, without blood, without death. The midwives who had done the killing were made the guardians of the cradles. They were thanked. Nobody asked why the doors of the old birthing-house had all swung open on their own at the moment the last god died, or why no one has managed to close them since.

The Old Lying-In keeps, under glass in the Book-room, a list in Mab Morwen's hand: the inventory of the bowls ladled out from the copper on the morning of the Meal, by household, with the share each took. It is the oldest document in the highlands and every Caul-line genealogy begins from it. Scholars of the Cutwrights' College who have been allowed to read it note three things. The Morwens took the first and deepest bowl. The caul went, by Mab's own hand, to the five households nearest her elbow and to no others. And the bowl-count at the foot of the page does not match the households listed above it: one more bowl was returned to the copper than was ever ladled out, and it came back, the inventory says in a smaller, shakier hand, *licked clean, and no household would own it*.

Over the round door of the Old Lying-In in Kest is a frieze older than the Gorging: eight figures standing in eight doorways, each in its own arch, each with its arms spread. Seven have been worn by six centuries of hands touched to them for luck. The eighth arch is empty. Its figure has been chiselled out so deeply that the stone behind it has gone through to the hollow of the wall, and the hole breathes warm air on cold days. Midwives touch the seven arches on the way in. Nobody touches the eighth. The apprentices say that if you put your ear to the hole you can hear someone on the other side laying a table. The apprentices say a great many things.

## The Rule of the Land

Nothing here is born right, and too much is born.

That is the whole of it, and it has been the whole of it for six centuries. Vey was the god of things coming into the world, and Vey is in the blood of the highlands now, and things will not stop coming. The births began wrong in 94 A.G. and have never gone right since. Every highland family has a story about what came out of whom, and every story is told the same way, flat and practical, the way other peoples talk about weather.

Children arrive speaking. Not babbling: speaking, in full sentences, in the voices of adults, with opinions. The first, in Kest in 94 A.G., opened its eyes and said, "Not yet," and died within the hour, and nobody has ever agreed on what it meant. Children arrive old, white-haired and arthritic, with failing hearts and worn teeth, and either grow younger year by year toward the cradle or simply die of age at three. Children arrive full-grown, tearing their way out into a world they are already too big for: men and women of thirty, of fifty, born mid-stride or mid-sentence, slick with blood and cord, who must be taught to use a spoon although their hands already know how to use a sword. These are the born-grown, and the country has found uses for them.

And some arrive as Parts. A single living hand, warm, clenching. An ear that turns toward sound. A length of spine that crawls by flexing, like a caterpillar. A heart in its own little caul, kept in a covered dish in Cleave for nineteen years before it stopped. The Parts are healthy. They feed through the skin or through mouths that open in unlikely places. They grow. They are also drawn toward each other: a hand left on a table will, overnight, drag itself to the edge nearest the nearest other Part, however far away, and two Parts in one room will strain together and, if allowed, seal along a seam that heals in a night. Since 500 A.G. it has been known that Parts from unrelated families fit, a hand born in Rudge sitting perfectly on a wrist born in Kest a century apart, as if they were born to go together.

### Parts

Outsiders imagine the Parts as something out of a butcher's window: lumps, scraps, wet and dying. They are not. A born Part is finished, the way a good stitch is finished. A hand arrives with its nails grown and its creases deep, the skin of the wrist closed over in a smooth pink dome like the end of a sausage-casing, faintly translucent, through which on a warm day you can see the bones of the wrist moving as it clenches. An ear arrives with a ridge of soft gristle behind it that it uses to drag itself along a table, a fraction of an inch at a time. A length of spine arrives sheathed in a thin wet skin the color of a peeled grape and crawls, as the highlands say, like a caterpillar that has decided where it is going. Every Part is warm. Every Part has a pulse, though nobody has found a heart in a hand, and the pulse of a Part is always, always, the pulse of whoever bore it. Mothers who sit up with their Parts at night say that the hardest thing is not the look of it but the sound, in a quiet room, of two hearts keeping time.

They feed. A Part laid in a dish of warm milk will take the milk in through its skin over a night, so that the dish is dry by morning and the skin smells of curd. Some Parts grow mouths: a small lipless slit opening in the palm of a hand, in the lobe of an ear, at the end of a finger, which makes a wet clicking noise when it is hungry and must be fed with a spoon. They grow. A hand born the size of a baby's fist will be the size of a grown woman's hand in four years, and nobody has learned to stop it at any size in between. They sleep; they wake; they have moods. A foot will curl and uncurl its toes for hours when it is unhappy. An eye will weep. And they are drawn, every one of them, to the others, the way a sleeper turns toward warmth.

The Parts that are hardest to speak of are the ones that are not born free. In the Course, and sometimes earlier, a body may bear a Part that does not come away: a second hand, small and perfect, growing from the inside of an elbow; a cluster of fingers at the base of a spine; an ear opening in the side of a neck, which hears, and which the bearer hears with. These are called *keepings*, and the Book of Questions has three hundred pages of cases about them. The Attendance may cut a keeping free, and it then becomes a Part like any other, crawling, hungry, drawn to the others; and the place it was cut from remembers, in the way of the highlands, and on a pang night opens, and begins again. Most bearers learn to live with their keepings bandaged under their clothes. A few come to love them. A few take a knife to them alone at night in a bricked room, and are found in the morning, and the Tally enters the keeping and the bearer both.

[pull] It holds my finger when I sleep. Tell me what else in this world has ever held my finger when I sleep. | — a Tarnbrow shepherd, to the Attendance, refusing to surrender a hand

[box: Rules — Keeping a Part]
A character who keeps a Part (born to them, bought, found or stolen) must feed it daily: a horn of milk, or a few drops of the keeper's own blood, which a Part prefers. Each day it goes unfed, it loses heart and the keeper gains 1 Hunger, because it is the god's piece and the god notices. A kept Part is warm, smells faintly of curd and the springs, and is detectable by anyone with the Taste or higher within ten paces (Caul + Instinct vs Hard 14). Each night, roll a d10: on a 1 it has moved toward the nearest other Part, and on a double-1 week (two 1s in a row) it has reached it, or tried to. A keeper who has slept beside a Part for a full season gains the Knack-like benefit of always knowing where it is, and suffers a Dread 3 check if it is taken, destroyed, or joined to another. Parts used in play take stats from the Crawling Part quick line in this chapter.
[/box]

Birth is not limited to mothers. Men have borne, through scars, through the navel, through the mouth. A widow of eighty-seven in Tarnbrow was delivered of twins older than she was. A ewe may drop eleven lambs, or one lamb with eleven heads. And things that are not alive bear. A well in Rudge gave birth in 403 A.G. to something that climbed up the rope and was not seen again. A locked chest in the Morwen strong-room was found open with a damp, curled thing inside and the lock still turned. A hillside above Cleave dilated for a month and delivered a warm boulder with a pulse. A woman four days dead in the Stillyard went into labor on her bier, and the Attendance attended, because it must, and the Tally records what came as "one, living, struck through."

The Tally is the evidence of the land's condition. Every birth in Cradlewrack is entered in a public ledger, by law, and the Tally of births has outrun the count of conceptions every year for two centuries. Last year, by the figures of Rufus Ochre the Tallyman, eleven hundred more people were born in the highlands than were carried by any woman, man, beast, well, chest, or hillside. The country is making people out of nothing, and nobody knows where they are.

### The Tally

The Tally is not a book but a building, a habit and a voice. Each parish keeps a Tally-board, a long slate under an awning, on which the parish Attendant chalks the day's births at dusk; a crier reads the board aloud; a runner copies it on oiled paper and carries it to the Tally-house in Kest, where Rufus Ochre's scriveners enter it in the great ledger that has been kept, without a missed day, since 131 A.G. The ledger is public. Anyone may climb Tally Hill and ask to see any page, and many do, because in Cradlewrack the Tally is the only proof that you were born, that you were permitted, and that you are who you say you are. A highlander with no line in the Tally does not legally exist; a highlander whose line is *struck through* does not legally exist either, and there are more of the second kind walking about than the Attendance would like.

Every entry has five parts: the number, the household, the bearer, *what*, and *stays*. The fourth column is the First Question, answered in the Attendant's own words; the fifth is the Second, and it has only three possible entries: *stays*, *surrendered* (for Parts), and a single ruled line, which is what *struck through* means on the page. The Tally has its own shorthand, learned by every child who learns letters: *sp.* for speaking-born, *o.b.* for old-born, *g.* for born-grown, *pt.* for a Part, *w.b.* for *without bearer*. The evening reading is a rite in itself. The crier stands on the Tally-house steps with a lamp, reads each line, and pauses after the fifth column; at *stays* the crowd murmurs "and let it stay," the highland half of the common grace; at *surrendered* it says nothing; at the ruled line it turns its face away from the steps for a count of three, the time an Opinion is meant to take, and then turns back. Mothers who have not yet been read stand at the front. Those who have been read and struck through stand at the back, and nobody speaks to them on the way home, and they are not expected to answer if anybody does.

### Doors

Vey was also the god of doors, of openings, of thresholds and first steps over them. In Cradlewrack nothing stays shut.

Doors drift open in the night. Not violently: they are found at dawn a hand's width ajar, latches lifted, bars slid back, as if someone very patient had worked at them for hours. Locks undo. Lids rise off jars. Knots slip. Coffins open. A sealed letter is found unsealed. And wounds reopen.

That last is the one that wears a body down. A cut that has knitted, a scar twenty years white, a surgical seam neatly closed: in Cradlewrack all of them remember how to open, and on pang nights many of them do. Old soldiers of the Foundling Companies bleed from every wound they have ever taken, all at once, every nine days. A woman who was cut for a stone in her youth wakes in a wet bed with her side yawning. The Attendance employs whole wards of stitchers whose only work is to close, each morning, what the night opened. There is no cure. There is only thread.

Every threshold is something waiting to be crossed. The highlanders knock before they open anything, door, cupboard, box or letter, because you knock to warn whatever is on the other side. They brick up rooms they never want opened again, and brick themselves into rooms at night, and break out in the morning.

[pull] Knock, and wait. If it knocks back, you were right to knock. If it doesn't, you were right to wait. | — a Kest mother's rule, taught at the lintel

[box: Rules — Wounds That Reopen]
Any character who has spent at least one night in Cradlewrack is subject to the land. Each pang (every nine days at present), roll a d10 for each healed Injury from the Mangling table the character carries, and for each surgical seam closed by Stitching in the last year. On a 1–3 it reopens during the night: the character loses 2 Flesh per reopened wound and wakes bleeding (Dread 1 for anyone unused to it). Closing it again is a Stitching roll at Hard 14. A Lack on that roll means the wound has learned the stitch: the next reopening is automatic. Natives of Cradlewrack carry their stitching kit the way other peoples carry a knife, and most have a Stitching of at least 1.
Doors and locks fare no better. Any lock in Cradlewrack must be checked each night; a lock that matters holds only on a Craft or Filch roll at Grim 18 by whoever set it, and even then not past the next pang. Bricking in always holds. That is why they brick.
[/box]

### The Body Is a Door

In the rest of the Table a body is a house a person lives in. In Cradlewrack a body is a doorway, and everybody knows it, and nobody is allowed to forget it, least of all by the law.

Every highland body belongs, a little, to the Attendance. An Attendant may lay her palm on any belly in the country, at any hour, without asking, and it is a crime to step back from her hand. Every woman of bearing age, every man in the Course, every child old enough to be returned, is examined at every Unbricking: stripped to the waist in the parish birthing-room, palpated, listened to with an ear on the navel, asked the date of their last cramp, their last waters, their last dream of a door. The examinations are brisk, cold-handed and not unkind, and they are hated more quietly and more completely than anything else the Attendance does. At Breech's Gate the examination is done to foreigners too, and the Rim Road Company's own guidance to its drivers warns that "the highlanders will put their hands on you, and you will let them, and you will not discuss it afterward among yourselves, which is the only part of the experience that will help."

[pull] Breathe out, sir. No, all the way out. I need to feel what's breathing in there besides you. | — Examiner at Breech's Gate, to a Fatlands wool-buyer

What the Examination finds is worse than the Examination. The land does not ask before it begins in someone. A drover of forty, a grandmother, a Purgation zealot who has never touched another living soul, a girl of fifteen who has never been out of her mother's sight: any of them may wake with the low slow roll in the belly and the warm flood between the legs and know, in the flat highland way, what it means. The god does not need an act, a partner, a season or a consent. It needs an opening, and it makes them. Men in the Course feel their pelvis loosen and spread over a season with a deep grinding ache like a joint being slowly levered apart, until they walk with the rolling gait of the heavily pregnant and cannot remember how they used to walk. Breasts fill in old men and leak, and the milk is warm and salt and pink at the edges. Old scars soften, pucker, gain lips. A navel deepens, month by month, until a finger can be laid in it to the second knuckle and feel something on the other side lean against the fingertip.

The highlands have no word for this that is not a midwife's word, and so it is spoken of in a midwife's voice: *she's begun*, *he's carrying*, *she's open*, *he's near*. People are kind to the carrying. They give up seats. They do not ask by whom. But the kindness has the edge of the Examination in it, because a carrying neighbor is a neighbor the Attendance will come for, and a neighbor who has stopped looking like herself, and every highlander knows the madness that goes with it, which the Lying-In's book calls *tenancy* and the parishes call *the lodger*: the certainty, in the carrying, that the thing inside is awake and listening and has opinions about what they eat and whom they speak to; the certainty, in their families, that the person they love has been moved out of their own face by whatever moved in. Husbands sit up at night watching wives who sleep with their arms spread to the bed-frame. Wives refuse to eat at table with husbands whose voices have begun, they swear, to come from a little lower than their mouths. Most of them are wrong. Some of them are not, and the Book of Questions has a section on how to tell, and it is not long, and it does not end with a reassurance.

[box: Rules — Carrying]
A character with Regrowth 1 or more may begin to carry in Cradlewrack without any act, at any age, of any sex. The Carver may impose it as the consequence of a Lack on a Tooth (when Partaking in the highlands, a Tooth showing 1 on a pang day), of a Regrowth rise to the Appetite or the Course, or of sleeping a night in an unhinged house, a pang-house, or within a mile of the Dilation. Carrying is never a punishment the Carver springs for a laugh; it is the land taking a body that was not offered, and it should be played with the weight of that.
- **Signs.** The character feels the roll at each pang and loses 1 Flesh to the cramp. Anyone who examines them (Hand + Stitching vs Routine 10, or a Midwife's talent) knows.
- **Term.** Carrying lasts 1d10 days in the Course, or until the next pang otherwise. Each day of it, the character gains 1 Hunger and must make a Dread 1 check on waking.
- **Delivery.** At term the character labors for 1d6 hours and must roll Gut + Endure (Hard 14) or suffer Ruin from the Mangling table; an attending Midwife or any character with Stitching 2+ adds +2 and may roll in their place. What comes is rolled on the Parts table above, or, at the Brink, on the crowning table below. Witnesses check Dread 2.
- **The Questions.** In Cradlewrack, a birth without an Attendant is concealment, and the Attendance will come. Abroad, nobody knows what to do, which is worse.
- **Refusal.** A character may try to end a carrying early by going to an Opinion; the Opinion will do it, without comment, and the character checks Dread 2 and keeps the scar, which opens at every pang thereafter. There is no other way that works, and the Carver should make sure no player is ever told there is.
[/box]


## The Regrowth in Cradlewrack

Vey is growing back, and what grows back is beginning. Everything about the god's return in the highlands has the shape of a labor: a long, rising rehearsal, contractions closing together, an opening that cannot be reversed. The Attendance calls the stages by midwives' words, and every highlander can tell you where they stand.

### The Taste

A sense of being about to begin. It feels, at first, like hope. The afflicted wake with a held breath and an urge to start something: a letter, a journey, a quarrel, a song. They do not finish; highland houses are full of half-sewn shirts and first lines. A Taste-touched child leaves doors open behind her without noticing, and her parents follow her through the house closing them. Old scars itch and go pink. The rest of the Table would barely call it a curse.

### The Appetite

False labor, in anyone, of any age or sex. The body rehearses. Cramps roll low in the belly in waves with intervals, and the sufferer counts them without wanting to. Then the waters: a sudden warm flood of clear salt fluid from no clear source, smelling of the springs. Men double over at the plough. Old women labor nine hours at the market, deliver nothing, and are up the next morning, hollow-eyed. Children have had it, and the Attendance's only guidance to parents is to hold them and count with them until it passes. The sufferer feels the god's want as plainly as hunger: something should be coming out, and it is not. The Appetite-struck gather outside houses where a woman is in labor and stand very close to the walls. Many are found at dawn asleep on their feet in their own doorways, arms spread to the jambs.

[pull] Nine hours I laboured at the stall, and the custom kept coming, and I kept selling, between. What else would I do? It's only the god. | — a mutton-wife of the Shambles, aged seventy-three

### The Course

True bearing, of things that are not children. The sufferer conceives without any act, carries for days or weeks rather than months, and delivers: Parts, mostly; small wet things with too many fingers; warm stones; keys; once, in the Attendance's records, a perfectly formed little door of bone, hinged, that opened onto nothing. Hips widen in men. Scars become permanent openings, lipped and moist, that dilate during pangs. Some develop a second opening where the body has no business having one, and keep it bandaged. The Caul-lines carry almost continuously: a noble of the highest houses may deliver six or eight times a year, and spend the weeks between already swelling. The afflicted grow gaunt, as if everything they eat is taken for someone else.

The Course is where the body stops being a person's and becomes a road. The Course-touched describe the sensation of carrying as the sensation of being *occupied*: a pressure that is not quite inside and not quite outside, a weight that shifts when they turn over in bed a half-second after they do, a second set of preferences about food and heat and company. Delivery in the Course is quick and brutal and lonely. The waters come warm and salt and in quantities no body could hold, soaking through a straw mattress and the boards beneath it; then the opening, wherever the god has decided to open, with a tearing like wet linen; then the Part, or the stone, or the key, slithering out onto the sheet with the little gasp they all give. Course-touched mothers are not let near what they bear until it has been answered. Many of them stop asking. Some of them never stopped, and the Lying-In has a whole ward of women who ask, every hour, what it was, and have been told, and ask again.

### The Brink

The Attendance has a word it uses in the Lying-In and nowhere else: crowning. Somewhere on the body of a person at the Brink, usually the belly, sometimes the breast or the forehead, the flesh has dilated into a round, perfect opening with soft lips and no bottom anyone has reached, and through it, at the height of each pang, something presses up toward the light: a curve of wet skin, a hand, a face that is never the same twice. Nothing comes all the way out. The Brink-touched hear it talking, in a small voice, about what it will do when it arrives. Thread passed through the crowning's lips dissolves by morning. The common are taken to the Lying-In; the Caul-lines to the deep chambers of their houses; and they are made comfortable, and watched.

### The Seating

The land itself. The highlands are in labor.

No single person has ever been Seated in Cradlewrack, and the Attendance believes none will be, because the god is not regrowing in a person. It is regrowing in the country. The quakes are contractions: a century ago a full season apart, then thirty days within living memory, and now nine. The waters come up out of the ground without rain. The earth dilates into crownings. The greatest, the Dilation, is a mile across and has begun to move with the pangs. When the interval closes to nothing, something will be born out of the ground, and the whole of Cradlewrack is its mother.

## Land and Weather

Cradlewrack is the high country of the Table's northwest: rounded red-clay hills folded on each other like the flesh of a sleeper, split by wet ravines thick with fern and alder. The hills are warm to the touch; snow melts where it falls and runs down in pink rivulets. The soil is soft, dark red, and smells of iron, and every highland house is built of it, rounded and windowless on the weather side, so that a village looks like a clutch of eggs. Springs run warm and slightly salt, and taste, the highlanders admit, like the inside of a cheek.

The pangs are the true calendar, and every household notches the interval on its lintel. A pang comes as a long rolling quake that rises from the deep ground: crockery walks off shelves, bricked rooms groan, ewes drop where they stand, and every Course-touched person within a hundred miles cries out at once. It lasts a slow count of sixty. In the hours after come the waters: springs surge, wells overflow, and in the low ravines the ground simply weeps, a warm clear tide that leaves a pale salt rime and draws flies. Sinkholes are called crownings. The earth dilates over weeks into a perfect round opening with soft lips of clay, and people gather at the rim to see what comes up. Usually it is water. Twice in the last decade it has been a person, born-grown and naked, who climbed out by the roots and asked the watchers what year it was.

The highland weather is otherwise mild, damp, and close. Fog lies in the ravines most mornings and is called the caul; children are told not to breathe it too deep. Wind is rare. Rain is light and warm. The worst season is late Plenty, when the clay steams, the air turns thick and salt, and the pangs, for reasons nobody understands, are hardest.

[pull] Don't drink from the ravine after the waters, child. It's not that it's bad. It's that it's somebody's. | — a Cleave drover, to a pilgrim

### Gazetteer

#### Kest

The capital and seat of the Attendance, built on seven rounded hills around the Old Lying-In, the birthing-house of the Meal, whose doors have not closed since that night. Kest is a city of round red buildings without a single door that locks: by law since the Year of Doors, doors hang on leather hinges and are held, if at all, with wedges. Rooms meant to stay shut are bricked. The streets curve; there are no corners, because the Attendance holds that corners are where things collect. Every quarter has a Tally-board where the day's births are chalked at dusk and read aloud. Kest smells of wet clay, lye, and the sweetish copper of the laundries, where aprons are boiled day and night.

#### The Lying-In

The Attendance's fortress-hospital, two hours' ride north of Kest on a bare hill: a ring of red walls forty feet high with no gate, entered by ladder and a wicker basket hoist. Here are taken the births nobody speaks about: the ones the Second Opinion spared and should not have, the ones it could not reach, the Brink-touched, the mothers who will not stop bearing. Its upper wards are clean and quiet and smell of lavender. Its lower wards are bricked, cell by cell, and the bricklayers who maintain them are paid triple and change every year. The Lying-In keeps its own book, separate from the public Tally. Nobody outside the Attendance has read it.

#### The Dilation

The great crowning in the central highlands, a mile across and widening. It opened in 547 A.G. as a pothole in a sheep track and has never stopped. Its lips are soft red clay that gives underfoot like flesh and slopes inward to a sheer drop into a darkness from which warm air breathes. During pangs the whole bowl flexes. Around the rim has grown Rimwatch, a ring of tents, turf huts and hostels full of pilgrims, Assemblers' agents, Barren protestors and Attendance observers, who mark the rim's advance each morning with painted stakes. The stakes nearest the edge are always gone by noon.

[pull] Twenty years at the rim. I've seen it take a stake, a cart, two pilgrims and a bishop of the Purgation. Never seen it give back anything I'd want. | — Madder Rudge

#### The Sill

A bare hilltop in the north, where a single door stands in its frame with no wall around it. It is oak, grey with age, iron-bound, older than the Gorging, and it has been ajar since that night, opening a finger's width further each year. Walk around it and there is only hillside behind. Look through the gap from the front, the old rule says, and you will see something else. Its keeper, Old Hinge, oils it, measures it, and does not look.

#### The Assemblers' Barn

A long stone threshing-barn in a fold of hills east of Kest, built in the old style with a door at each end. It is guarded by armed brothers, lit by lamps day and night, and kept warm as a lambing-shed. It smells of tallow, catgut, and something underneath like the inside of a sheepfold in spring. Visitors are welcome, by appointment, and are shown the near end, where Parts sleep in labelled straw boxes awaiting their place. Visitors are not shown the far end.

#### Rudge

A clay-diggers' parish in the western ravines, famous for the well that gave birth in 403 A.G. The well has been capped with three tons of fired brick and a millstone, and the parish still sleeps badly. Rudge supplies the potters of Kest and, through Madder Rudge's family, most of the watchers at the Dilation. Its people are short, broad, red-handed and suspicious, and the Tally records more Parts born in Rudge than anywhere else in the country.

#### Morwen Hall

The seat of the highest Caul-line house, on a wooded ridge above the River Tull. A great round keep of fired red brick with no doors at all on its lower floors, entered by bridges at the third storey. Within are the Veiled Galleries, where the family lives behind the membranes they were born in. The house has married within itself for three hundred years, so that the cauls might stay thick, and the Cutwrights' ledgers on the Morwen line are kept in a separate locked volume with a great many footnotes. The servants say the house is never quiet, because someone in it is always in labor.

#### Breech's Gate

The great border post on the Rim Road where it leaves the highlands, named for the current marshal and renamed for each one since 220 A.G. A walled yard, a customs hall, and the Examination Rooms, where every traveler leaving the country is checked for signs of carrying: hands on the belly, a midwife's ear to the navel, questions about the last pang. Nobody pregnant may leave. The yard is always full of the turned-back, waiting for a change of heart that never comes.

#### The Stillyard

The great cemetery of Kest, on the north slope of the city: miles of long red walls in concentric rings, each lined with niches where the dead are mortared in behind bricks stamped with their family's mark. The walls grow by several hundred niches a year. On still nights they tick and settle, and sometimes they knock. Families pay the masons to listen. When a wall knocks back, the Attendance is sent for, and attends.

#### Cordway

A village of the Uncut on the moorland west of Morwen Hall, built entirely of long low houses joined end to end so that cords need never pass through a door. Its streets are woven over with living cord, pale and pulsing, strung from roof to roof between mothers and children and grandchildren, a vast slow net that sways in the wind and is warm to the touch. Outsiders must duck. Outsiders who touch a cord feel the heartbeat of whoever is on the far end of it.

#### Nullhouse

The mother-house of the Barren Order, a plain square building of grey stone (the only stone building of note in the highlands, quarried and carted from the Fast border at ruinous cost) on a dry hill in the south. Grey stone, the Order teaches, is the one material in Cradlewrack that has never borne anything. Its doors are bricked on every side. Members enter by a stair over the wall and are cut in the chapter-room.

#### The Low Wall

The innermost ring of the Stillyard, low enough to sit on, long enough that nobody has walked the whole of it in a day. Its niches are small, the size of a bread-oven, and its bricks carry no family mark: only a stamped number, which is the Tally number, and a ruled line. Here go the struck-through. Mothers come on the anniversaries of births that were never named, and lay a palm on the brick, and do not knock, because to knock is to ask for an answer. The Low Wall is the quietest place in Kest by day. By night the slot-feeders of the Debt Wall, two rings out, say it ticks like a kitchen full of cooling pans, and that on the nights after a pang some of the ticks are closer together than clay has any reason to be.

#### Greystead

A chapter-house of the Barren Order in the dry hills between Kilnmouth and Nullhouse, square, grey and doorless, entered by a stair over the wall. In the winter of 639 it held forty-one of the Sealed; by the spring of 640 it held nine. What happened there is told under the Barren Order below.

#### The Apron Stair

A cut in the inner slope of the Dilation's rim, on the Cleave side: ninety-one steps hewn into the soft red clay in 619 and recut after every pang since, though they now lead only a few strides down before the lip falls away into the dark. Here, on Hollan's Day of that year, the mothers of the Walk went down. Pilgrims tie strips of white linen to the stakes at its head. The Attendance does not forbid it. Since Lack 640, things have come up the Apron Stair in the waters, and they are tied to the stakes too.

### The Year of Doors

On a night in late Carving, 310 A.G., every lock in Cradlewrack failed at once: every bolt, latch, padlock, chain and bar from the Rim Road to the sea cliffs. The Tally for the following month was the largest in history, and what was born that month is recorded in the Lying-In's book and nowhere else. From that night dates bricking-in, the habit of knocking, and the Kest law against locking doors at all, on the reasoning that a lock which will fail is worse than none.

### The Gathering of Parts

In 500 A.G. a Rudge clay-digger named Abel Furrow, who had kept a born hand in a bread-crock for nine years, took in a born forearm from a neighbor and found them joined at the wrist in the morning. He told the Attendance, which tested it, and kept testing it, and in 503 issued a decree that all born Parts were to be surrendered to the Lying-In. The decree has never been obeyed. Out of the disobedience grew the Assemblers.

### The Calloway Veil

House Calloway, third of the five, has for two centuries been known for its twins. In 541 the Duchess Amarante Calloway was delivered of two sons in a single caul: one membrane, thick and grey, enclosing both heads, so that the boys were born face to face, breathing each other's breath. The Attendant asked the Second Question and let them stay, and the Book of Questions records the case at length, because the house would not permit the caul to be cut. Hugo and Lucan Calloway grew up in it. They walked, ate, slept and were taught their letters inside the one veil, their faces a hand's width apart, their two voices coming out of the membrane as one wet muffled voice that nobody outside could tell into two. The house called them its treasure. Their foretellings, spoken together, were the most precise the Veiled Council has ever heard.

In 566, at twenty-five, Hugo Calloway was betrothed by the Council, within the house, as custom required. The Cutwrights' locked volume records the betrothal in its shorthand, and records, too, that the bride's name was entered next to both brothers' names, there being no way to enter it next to only one. On the eve of the bricking-in, the servants heard the one voice arguing with itself for nine hours. In the morning Hugo came down to the hall alone in the veil, carrying it in both arms like a sack, because the half of it that held his brother hung slack and heavy and wet from his shoulder. He had strangled Lucan inside the membrane in the night, face to face, and then sat with him until dawn because there was no way to leave the room without him.

The house did not brick him. It could not separate the dead brother from the living without cutting the caul, and it would not cut the caul. So Hugo Calloway lived for thirty-one more years with his brother's body inside his veil, carried in a sling of silk; and the Calloway servants say that the body did not rot, because the membrane fed it, and that it grew, and that its face stayed a hand's width from Hugo's face all his life and its eyes stayed open. Hugo married. He had children. Every one of them was born in a caul with two faces in it, and so has every Calloway born since, and the house is careful, now, to let the Second Opinion choose which. The Calloways keep a portrait of Hugo in their gallery: one man, one veil, and, if you stand to the left of it in lamplight, a second face.

[pull] By the shut door, I swear it, and may my brother hear me say it, since he's the one listening. | — the oath of House Calloway, sworn with a hand on the veil

### The Walk of Aprons

In the Lack of 618, the interval fell below twenty-three days for the first time, and the highlands were afraid in a way they had not been since the Year of Doors. That winter the patients at the Brink in the Lying-In's upper wards all began, on the same night, to speak through their crownings in the same small voice, and they all said the same thing: *a mother from every house.* The Veiled Council, for the first time in its history, foretold unanimously. The five veiled heads, in the same week and without conferring, each dictated to their scribes that the ground would *ease* if it were *attended*: if a mother of every parish went down to it, as a midwife goes into a room, and stayed.

The Attendance did not want it. Orla Kest, then a Senior of the Lying, spoke against it for three days in the Hall and said, in her even voice, that the god had never yet been satisfied by anything given to it and that the Book of Questions had no page on which a mother was the answer. She was overruled, by the Council, by the Crown (the King Within kicked once), and finally by the mothers themselves, because the volunteers came in their hundreds, and they were nearly all women who had heard a ruled line read against their household on the Tally-house steps. They wanted to go into the room. The Attendance drew lots among them, one for each of the ninety-one parishes, and dressed the chosen in white aprons, the only time in its history it has given the apron to anyone who had not taken the vow.

On Hollan's Day of 619 they walked from the Old Lying-In to the Dilation, which was then a quarter-mile across, behind the Midwife-Paramount and the Veiled Council in their litters and the whole of Kest. It took three days. They sang the counting-song. At the rim the Trowel had cut ninety-one steps into the inner slope, and the mothers went down them one by one, at the start of a pang, while the crowd counted aloud, and at sixty the pang ended and the steps were empty. Nobody saw them fall. Nobody heard them land. The crowd waited at the rim through the night, in perfect quiet, because the Council had foretold that the ground would ease, and in the morning everyone went home to wait for the next pang and see.

It came twenty-two days later, a day early.

The one after came in twenty-one. The Council's scribes went back through their foretellings and found that the word they had all written was not *ease* but a word in the old highland speech that means both *ease* and *open*, and nobody would say aloud which one the god had meant. The ninety-one families were given a stamped brick each in the Stillyard's Mothers' Wall, with nothing behind it. Orla Kest did not attend the dedication. She attended a birth in Rudge that day, and the Tally records it as permitted.

In the Lack of 640, twenty-one years after, the waters after a pang came up the Apron Stair, and when they drained, a white apron lay folded on the ninth step, laundered and starched and smelling of lavender, with a name stitched in the hem in the old Rudge way. Since then there have been nineteen. Each has come back to its own family. The families do not know whether to wear them, burn them, or wait for the next. A few of the families have begun to dream of the women who wore them, standing in a long warm room with their arms spread, holding something open. Nobody in Cradlewrack now uses the word *ease*.

## Rule, Law and Politics

Cradlewrack is ruled by the Attendance, the armed order of midwives. No birth may take place without one present; the penalty for an unattended birth is that the child is held to be unborn, and the mother is fined for concealment. The Attendant asks the Two Questions aloud, *what is it* and *does it stay*, and answers them herself, according to the Book of Questions and her own judgment. Her knife is called the Second Opinion, and there is no appeal from it.

The Book of Questions runs to six hundred pages and is mostly cases. Speaking children stay. Old-born children stay. Born-grown stay, and are given to the Companies. Parts are surrendered to the Lying-In. Everything else is the Attendant's to decide, alone, in the room, and the Book's last page, in Hollan Kest's words, says only: *It is your hand, and you will remember. Choose so that you can.* The Attendance does not speak of the Second Opinion in public, and does not need to. Every highlander knows that the bundle carried out of a birthing-room, tied in the knot that cannot come undone, is entered as "struck through" and bricked in the Stillyard's Low Wall without a family mark. Every highlander knows a family with a struck-through line. Few would say they wish the knife gone. Many could not tell you, without weeping, why.

Beneath the Attendance, law is local and practical. Parish Attendants act as magistrates. Disputes over descent, which are endless because descent runs through whoever bore you, are heard before a bench of three senior midwives called a Lying. Murder is punished by bricking: the condemned is mortared alive into a niche in the Stillyard's Debt Wall, with water and bread pushed through a slot each day for as long as the family pays. Lesser crimes are punished by *unhinging*: the offender's house has its doors taken off by the parish and burned, and they must live open to the night.

The old monarchy technically survives. The House of Aubrey still holds the crown, and the rightful king, Aldous the Ninth, has been in the womb for forty-four years. His mother, the Queen Dowager Sibyl, still carries him, and he is consulted on matters of state: a question is read aloud to the Queen's belly by the Lord Chamberlain, and one kick means yes. The Attendance finds this an extremely convenient form of government.

The Caul-lines nobility are born veiled in a membrane that, in the highest houses, never comes off. They live behind it, speak through it, and are held to be prophetic, since the god's caul sees forward into what is beginning. The great houses (Morwen, Aubrey, Calloway, Seele, and Danner) sit on the Veiled Council, which advises the Attendance on matters of prophecy, and in practice on matters of money, since the houses own most of the clay-pits and all of the springs. Their prophecies are vague, red, and lately unanimous.

Nobody pregnant may leave the country. The Border Marshals check. The law dates from 220 A.G., when three expectant mothers crossed into the Fatlands and the Tally of that year recorded three births in Cradlewrack to women who were not there. The rule held; the country went with them. It is the opinion of the Attendance that a pregnant woman who leaves takes some of Cradlewrack with her, and that whatever she bears abroad is still the country's, and still its business.

[pull] Majesty, is it your will that the Border stay shut? ... The King has kicked once. The Border stays shut. Next question. | — from the minute-book of the Morning Reading, the Aubrey Gardens

### The Veiled Houses

Five houses hold the springs, the clay and the future: Morwen, Aubrey, Calloway, Seele and Danner. They are the Caul-lines, the descendants of the households that stood nearest Mab Morwen's elbow at the copper and took the god's caul in their bowls, and they have spent six centuries making sure the caul stays theirs. It is the one thing in Cradlewrack that has never been allowed to open.

The membrane is the house. A Caul-line child born in a caul that comes away in the Attendant's hands is no longer reckoned of the line, whatever its parentage; it is called *bare*, and is given the house name with a stroke through the first letter, and sent to the lower floors or to the Crèche as a drill-mother or out into the parishes with a small pension and a large silence. A child born in a caul that does not come away, that thickens and grows with it, is the house's whole hope. The thicker the caul, the deeper the sight; and every house has learned, generation by generation, that the caul is thickest where the blood is least diluted. So the houses keep the veil. They marry within themselves, cousin to cousin and closer, and the Cutwrights' College, whose business is bloodlines and which recognizes every degree of kinship on the Table in plain words, keeps the Caul-line genealogies in a separate locked volume in which certain unions are entered not in words at all but in a private shorthand of small looped signs. Junior Cutwrights who are set to copy that volume are told not to try to read the signs. Most of them work it out anyway, and ask to be transferred.

What the houses have bought with their discretion is visible across a market, which is why they so rarely go to one. The Caul-line face, where a face can be seen through the veil at all, is a single face: long, narrow, pale, with a small soft mouth and wide-set eyes of no particular colour, and every portrait in every veiled house shows it, so that the long galleries of Morwen Hall look like one woman painted four hundred times in different collars. Their bodies are delicate. They bruise at a touch, bleed long, break bones in falls a shepherd would walk away from, and many of them never walk at all and are carried in litters from the age of twelve. They foresee, and they carry, more than anyone else in the highlands: a Course-touched Morwen may deliver eight times a year, and the deep chambers of every veiled house are full of what is carried away from those deliveries in covered dishes, and of the house's own children who crowned young and did not stop. And they are sad, the highlands say, with the particular sadness of people who have never once in their lives met a stranger who was truly a stranger to them.

[box: Running the Veiled Houses]
Keep the Caul-lines' rot behind the membrane, where it belongs. Their horror is not anything the characters see done; it is the pattern: the same face in four hundred portraits, the sign in the College's ledger, the deep chambers no servant enters, the nurse who goes down to the woods with a basket and comes back without it and cannot eat for a day. Let players assemble it. Never depict it, and never let it touch the house's children: Lady Isolde Morwen, sixteen and bare-faced, is the person in Morwen Hall most frightened of her own blood, and her story is the story of getting out.
[/box]

## Culture and Daily Life

### Food and Drink

Highland food is plain and red. Mutton and lamb above all, since the ewes drop endlessly; clay-baked bread, which is set in a pot of wet red clay and buried in the embers until it comes out with a pink crust; oats; nettles; and the salt from the warm springs, which is faintly rose-colored and tastes of blood. Lamb is so cheap that the poor eat it daily and the rich serve it never. The national drink is *posset*, warm ewe's milk curdled with spring-salt and spirit. Eggs are not eaten: too many hens' eggs in the highlands have been found to contain things looking back.

The custom the rest of the Table finds hardest to stomach is the Second Meal. At a child's naming, a year after its birth, the family eats its afterbirth, kept salted in a crock since the day of delivery and stewed in the copper with lamb and onions. Everyone at table takes a spoonful, starting with the mother, in memory of the first Meal, when the people of the highlands ate the caul and the god and ended the Lack. The Caul-lines eat their own children's cauls, raw, from a silver dish, and do not speak of it to outsiders. The grace is the common one, "Lack keep away," followed by the highland addition: "and let it stay."

[pull] SHOULDER, ROAST, 1 L. — LIGHTS & SWEETBREADS, 3 c. — POSSET, A HORN, 1 c. — NO ODD-FOLD MEAT SOLD HERE, ASK AT THE BACK. | — chalked on a brazier-stall board in the Shambles, Kest

### Dress

Highlanders dress in undyed or madder-red wool, and nothing they wear fastens: buttons and buckles work loose, so garments are wrapped and knotted and retied at every bell, and the well-off have their clothes sewn on each morning and cut off at night. Everyone over seven carries a stitching kit on a neck-cord and a small hammer for knocking. Midwives wear the white apron and a knife-case nobody looks at directly. The Caul-lines are recognizable across a market by their blurred and glistening heads.

### Marriage, Bed and Bearing

Marriage is a contract between two households about who will bear, and when, and who will attend. Bride and groom are mortared into a bricked room together overnight and broken out at dawn, which is lucky, since nothing came in. What passes between married adults is spoken of only obliquely; highlanders are frank about bodies and shy about the rest. Every couple keeps a *closed week* around each pang, in the belief that whatever begins in pang-week begins wrong.

The Caul-lines marry within the house. It is called *keeping the veil*: the thicker the caul, the higher the line, and the caul is thickest where the blood is least diluted. The Cutwrights record these unions with a discretion the College shows nowhere else, in a locked volume where cousins marry cousins and the relations between consorts are entered in a private shorthand whose key the College does not lend. What the shorthand covers is not discussed in the highlands, and certainly not in Morwen Hall, where every family portrait has the same blurred face.

### Children

A child is not named for a year, until it is clear what it is. Until then it is called by its place in the Tally (Third of the Gorse household) or by a pet word, *bit*, *small*, *keep*. Parents are advised not to love an unnamed child too much, and none of them listen. A child who changes in its first year, who begins to speak in an adult voice or to age, is taken back for a late Second Opinion. This is called *returning*, and it is the great dread of highland parents. Many hide their children rather than return them. Few of the returned come home.

The right to name a child is property, and like all property in the highlands it can be sold before it exists. The Naming-Exchange under the Tally-house trades naming-rights by the hour on rumours from the birthing-rooms, and poor families sell the right to name their unborn for bread, or debts, or a place on the list for an Agnes Latch door. The buyer chooses the name at the naming-year, and the family must use it, and it is entered in the Tally beside the buyer's mark. A child named by a broker carries the broker's mark for life, which is a small shame; a child named by a great house carries the house's patronage, which is a large fortune. Most deeds are written by the Exchange's own scriveners to a form unchanged in two hundred years.

[fiction]

Children learn the shape of the country before they learn their letters, and they learn it the way children learn everything, by playing it. The commonest game in every highland lane is **Who's in the Brick?**: one child crouches with her face to a wall, the others knock in turn, and she must say, without looking, which knock is whose. Guess right and the knocker takes the wall. Guess wrong and the knocker says *I'm not who you think,* and everyone screams and runs, and the game starts over. Older children play **Second Question**, which parents beat them for and which they play anyway, in the ravines, with a doll and a stick.

### Death and the Dead

Nobody is buried. Graves open, and the dead have been known to bear. The dead are washed, every opening stitched shut with nine stitches, and bricked into the long walls of a Stillyard behind a course stamped with the family mark. Mourners knock three times on the new brick and do not wait for an answer. Oaths are sworn on the dead this way: a hand flat on a kinsman's brick, and the words *by the shut door*. To break such an oath is to wish the door open, and highlanders believe a forsworn man's dead will come through the brick for him. Some have.

### Festivals

- **Pangday.** Every nine days, now. Work stops at the first tremor. Families gather in the strongest room, sing the counting-song together, and wait out the sixty seconds, then go outside to see what the waters have brought up.
- **The Unbricking.** The first day of Grace. Every bricked room in the country, except the Stillyard's, is broken open at dawn and aired, and whatever is found inside is entered in the Tally.
- **Tablenight.** Every door in the country is propped wide open until dawn, on purpose, so that nothing can be said to have broken in. Families sit up in their open houses in the cold, all lamps lit, and a place is laid at the table nearest the door for whatever comes. Usually nothing does.

[pull] Leave the door wide on Tablenight and lay the plate nearest it. If the plate is clean in the morning, don't wash it. Don't ask who did. | — Tarnbrow custom

### Crime and Punishment

The great crimes of the highlands are concealment (an unattended birth or a hidden Part), descent-fraud (claiming a bearer who did not bear you), lockbreaking (opening a bricked room without the household's leave), and smuggling a pregnancy across the border. Concealment of a Part is punished by unhinging; the Assemblers have made it the most common crime in the country. The Attendance does not hang, behead, or burn. It bricks. A sentence of bricking is read in the Old Lying-In; the Debt Wall in the Stillyard is lined with the living condemned, and in the evenings their families sit beneath the slots and talk to them.

The Debt Wall is where Cradlewrack puts its murderers, and it puts its kin-slayers there in particular numbers, because the highlands breed a particular kind of killing. Brothers kill brothers over which of them will carry a Part to the Barn and take the Assemblers' money. Husbands kill wives who have begun to look at them from a little below their eyes. Daughters kill mothers in the Course rather than watch them deliver again. The commonest kin-slaying of all is the one the Book of Questions calls *the private Opinion*: a household, in the dark, asking the Second Question of one of its own grown members without sending for anyone, and answering it. The Attendance treats it as murder, and so do the families, afterwards.

## Factions

### The Attendance

The midwives: judges, physicians, priests and executioners in one apron. Founded in 120 A.G. by the daughters and granddaughters of the women who killed Vey, the Attendance has ruled the highlands for five centuries without ever calling itself a government. Every parish has its Attendant, every birth its knife. Its members take a single vow, *I will go into the room*, and it is the one vow in Cradlewrack nobody has ever been known to break, though many have died keeping it.

**Beliefs.** That every birth is a question, and someone must answer it; that the Second Opinion is mercy to the mother, to the country, and, the Book insists, to what is born. The order is split as never before: the Knife-faction, led by Orla Kest, holds that the knife has held the country together for six centuries; the Late-faction, which has no leader willing to be named, whispers that the knife was six centuries too late, and that no apron can attend the birth under the Dilation.

**Wants.** To keep the Tally true, the births attended, and the knife steady until the interval closes. To learn what is in the ground before it arrives, and, if it must be, to be in the room when it does.

| Rank | Title | Grants |
| 1 | Apprentice (Third Hand) | The white apron, a stitching kit, the right to hold the door. |
| 2 | Attendant | A parish; the right to ask and answer the First Question. |
| 3 | Opinion | The knife; the right to answer the Second Question alone. |
| 4 | Senior of the Lying | A seat on a descent bench; access to the Lying-In's book. |
| 5 | Midwife-Paramount | The Old Lying-In. One holder. Answerable to no one. |

**Joining.** Any woman, man, or born-grown person may apprentice by going into a room alongside an Attendant and staying until the end. Most apprentices quit after their first Second Opinion. Player characters can join at rank 1 with Stitching 1 and the vow. **Enemies:** the Assemblers, the Uncut (who call midwives "the Severers"), Petra Quick. **Allies:** the Crown, the Border Marshals, the Foundling Companies, uneasily the Barren Order.

#### The Second Opinion, After

Nobody describes the knife. The Book of Questions does not; its six hundred pages say what was decided and never how it was done. What the highlands talk about, endlessly, in the flat practical voice they keep for the unbearable, is what comes after: the part of the Second Opinion that belongs to the grown-ups in the room.

The Opinion turns her back. That is the first rule, and it is a kindness, and every mother in Cradlewrack knows what it means when the midwife turns her back to the bed. The apprentice holds the door. The linen is wrapped and tied in the knot that cannot come undone, the same knot the dead are tied with, and the bundle is handed to the apprentice without either of them looking at it, and the apprentice walks it to the Low Wall, at night, alone, however far, and hands it to the night-mason who waits there with a stamped brick and a trowel. That walk is the part the apprentices remember. Wenna Thrale has written that the bundles stay warm the whole way, longer than they should, and that you learn not to notice whether they are still, and that you do not learn it.

The Opinion stays. She washes the mother, stitches what needs stitching, sits by the bed until the mother sleeps or until the mother turns her face to the wall, which most of them do and which the Book says is to be permitted. She does not explain. She does not apologise, because an apology is a door and the mother might come through it. If the mother asks, *what was it*, she is told the Tally's words: *nothing has been born.* A great many mothers do not ask, then or ever. A great many ask every day of their lives. One mother in Cleave asked Orla Kest, in the street, eleven years after, and Orla stopped and told her, in full, precisely, kindly, and the mother thanked her and went home, and the Attendance does not know whether she was helped.

Then the Opinion goes home and scrubs her hands. Every Opinion scrubs. They scrub with lye and pumice and a stiff brush at the copper in the Aprons until the skin of the knuckles cracks and bleeds, and the laundresses of the Coppers boil a knife-apron three times instead of once and pour the third water on the hill. Opinions develop a callus on the inside of the ring finger where the hook of the knife rests, and they wear it like a brand, and highlanders who see it in the street step aside without meaning to.

They break. The Attendance does not hide this; it is in the Lying-In's book, and the Seniors read it. Opinions see the faces of what they answered in the faces of strangers' children. They hear small wet gasps from cupboards. They count breaths in their sleep, one, two, three, and wake before four. They stop eating mutton. They come to believe, some of them, that they are not permitted: that if the Second Question were asked of them they would be struck through, and that the Tally has got it wrong, and they go to Tally Hill and ask to see their own line. A retired Opinion who has Broken in this way is called *answered*, and is cared for at the Lying-In in a clean upper ward with a view of the hills, where she is let sit with her hands in a basin of warm water for as long as she likes. There are forty-four of them in the ward this year. There were nineteen five years ago. The Late-faction says that this, and not the Dilation, is the true measure of the Shortening.

[pull] Choose so that you can remember, the Book says. It doesn't say you'll be let forget. | — a retired Opinion, the answered ward of the Lying-In

[box: Running the Second Opinion]
The Second Opinion is the moral centre of Cradlewrack and the hardest thing in it to run. Keep the camera on the adults. A scene with the knife in it is a scene about the midwife's back, the mother's face to the wall, the apprentice at the door, the walk, the warm bundle, the Low Wall, the scrubbing. Never describe what the knife does, and never put an infant's body on the page. The horror is that this is ordinary, that it is mercy, and that nobody in the room is sure; let the players be the people who have to carry the bundle, or hold the door, or decide whether to report the Opinion who counted eleven breaths. That is where the chapter's weight is meant to fall.
[/box]


### The Assemblers

A sect that collects Parts and joins them. Founded in 541 A.G. by a shepherd-surgeon who had read the Furrow case, the Assemblers believe that the Parts are not monstrous births at all but one body, born piecemeal, through ten thousand mothers across a century: Vey, being born again the only way the highlands would permit, in pieces small enough to be let stay. Their work is to gather the pieces and give the god back its shape. They are making good progress.

**Beliefs.** That every Part is holy; that every mother who bore one is a mother of the god; that the Second Opinion has been murdering Vey's fingers and teeth for five hundred years. The figure in the barn is the god, nearly whole. When it is finished it will stand up, and the pangs will stop, because the god will have been born the easy way, in a barn, among friends.

**Wants.** The last pieces: a left hand, and a voice. Safe passage for every Part in the country. An end to the knife.

| 1 | Gatherer | A carrying-crate; the password at the Barn's near door. |
| 2 | Seamster | The right to stitch Parts and to be present at a Joining. |
| 3 | Midwife of Pieces | A district; Part-hunters at command; the Barn's middle hall. |
| 4 | Fitter | A place at the figure's side; the right to choose where a Part goes. |
| 5 | Assembler-Prime | The far end of the Barn. Dov Marrin. |

**Joining.** Bring a Part to the Barn, or the name of a family hiding one. Player characters who join gain access to Lazar Voss's drawings and to the Barn's surgeons. **Enemies:** the Attendance, the Barren Order. **Allies:** many grieving mothers, some Uncut, the Second Table (which has quietly paid for the Barn's roof).

#### Inside the Barn

The Barn has a door at each end, in the old threshing style, and the Assemblers have made a theology of it. The near door is for mothers. It stands open day and night on a warm yard swept clean, with lamps hung along the eaves and a brother at a trestle who writes down, very gently, in the Mothers' Book, the name of every woman, man or household who brings a Part, and the name of the Part, which is always the bearer's name followed by what it is: *Hester's hand*, *Aldo's ear*, *old Nan Furrow's one*. Every bearer is given a cup of posset and a seat by the fire and told the same thing, which is the founding promise of the order: *It will not be alone any more, and neither will you.* Most of them weep. Most of them come back, on the anniversary, to visit.

The near hall is long and warm and smells of straw, tallow and milk. Down each side, in labelled boxes of straw, the newly come Parts sleep or crawl in slow circles, waiting their place, and Seamsters walk the rows feeding them warm milk by hand and talking to each by name. Visitors are shown this, sincerely. It is the gentlest place most of them will see in the highlands.

The middle hall is for **the Hearing** and **the Joining**. A Part is not simply sewn wherever it fits. It is *heard*: a Fitter lays it on a warm slab and lays her own ear against it and listens, sometimes for days, until she hears where it wants to go, and then the Part is carried, in procession, with the bearer if the bearer will come, to the far end. The Fitters say Parts speak in a kind of pull, the way a compass needle speaks. Lazar Voss, who has watched a Hearing, says the Fitters are hearing their own pulse slow to the Part's and are guessing, and that they are right every time, and that he cannot explain it. Dov Marrin's wife lives in the middle hall, in a cot by the stove, and she hums. Her hum is the tune the Seamsters use to call the Parts in their boxes to feed.

The far end is not shown. It is where Herself lies on her bed of warm straw under a hundred lamps, and where the Joining is done, by Dov Marrin or a Fitter, with fine sheep-gut and curved needles and a great deal of warm water, while the brothers and sisters of the Barn stand around the figure in a ring and hum the five-note tune. The Joining takes a night. In the morning the seam has healed, pink and puckered, and the new Part has moved, a little, and the figure breathes a little deeper. The brothers then do something they do not describe to visitors: they bring the bearer, if the bearer has come, to the figure's side, and they lay the bearer's hand on the Part that was theirs, and wait. Most bearers feel it know them. Some feel it close on their fingers, and have to be helped to let go.

#### Bearing the Piece

The Assemblers have a rite they do not speak of in the near hall, and it is the reason the Attendance calls them a sect and not merely a nuisance. When a Part will not settle in the figure, when the Fitter hears it pulling somewhere else, the order says it is pulling toward a living body, and that it must be carried there a while first, as it was carried once before, to learn the way. A brother or sister volunteers to *bear the piece*. The Part is sewn onto them, by Dov or a Fitter, in the place it pulls toward: a hand onto the inside of a forearm, an ear onto the side of a neck, a length of spine down the back between the shoulder-blades. In a night, in Cradlewrack, it takes.

Those who bear the piece are honoured in the Barn and feared everywhere else. They are called **Carriers**, and they wear their Parts openly under a short cape so that the borne hand or eye can see the world. The joined Part feeds from them, warmly, through the seam, and they grow thin. It moves with its own will; a Carrier with a hand on her arm will find it reaching for things she does not want, stroking strangers' sleeves, closing on the edge of a table at a pang. Their own scars begin to open in sympathy with the Part's. And when, after a season or a year, the Part is ready to go to Herself, it is cut away again, and the place where it was remembers how to open and begins, at the next pang, to bear something of its own. Every Carrier the Barn has ever made has, within a year, borne a Part. The Assemblers count this as proof. The Attendance counts it as the most efficient way yet devised of making more mothers of pieces, and has bricked four Fitters for it.

### The Barren Order

Men and women who have had themselves cut so they can bear nothing. Founded at Nullhouse in 470 A.G., they preach an end to beginnings: if Vey regrows through every womb, navel and scar, the only refusal left is to close the body forever. Members are cut in the chapter-room by the Order's surgeons. In the higher ranks the cutting is extensive, and since wounds in Cradlewrack reopen, the sutures are renewed every pang; a brother who misses his resewing finds that his body has made new arrangements.

**Beliefs.** That the kindest thing a person can do is start nothing: no child, no journey, no quarrel, no hope. That the Dilation must not be watched, because watching is attending. That the land should be left to strain until it is exhausted. Their numbers grow with every pang.

**Wants.** The Dilation filled with grey stone. The Assemblers' figure burned. The Second Opinion given to every birth in the country, without exception, for one generation.

| 2 | Closed | The first cutting; full membership; a bricked cell. |
| 3 | Sealed | The second cutting; the right to preach on the rim. |
| 4 | Grey Brother or Sister | A chapter-house; command of the Order's stonecarters. |
| 5 | Abbot of Nullhouse | Brother Callum Stillwater. |

**Joining.** Hearers may leave at any time. The Closed may not, in any sense. **Enemies:** the Assemblers, the Uncut, the Caul-lines. **Allies:** the Purgation, which sees the Order as kin; elements of the Attendance's Knife-faction.

#### The Grey Winter

The Barren Order's whole promise is that a body can refuse. It is a terrible promise and a costly one, paid in cuttings and in sutures renewed every nine days for life, but it is a promise, and for a hundred and seventy years it held: the Closed and the Sealed did not bear. In the Shortening it stopped holding. The god, which needs only an opening, began to find its way through the cuttings themselves. In the Lack of 639, at Greystead, a Sealed sister of twenty years' standing named Merel Ash woke in the night with the slow roll in her belly and the warm flood on her sheets, and by morning her renewed sutures were wet and parted, and through them, at the pang, something had begun to crown.

The Order had no words for it, because its words were all refusals. Merel was carried to Nullhouse, and Brother Callum sat with her, and she asked him what she had been cut for, and he did not know what to tell her. She went back to Greystead in the spring. A week after the pang, her cell was found empty and neatly kept, her robe folded on the cot and her sutures' thread wound on its spool beside it, and the stair over the wall was wet with the dew. She was not found. The Rimwatch stakes on the Kilnmouth side were gone by noon that day, which proves nothing.

What followed is called the Grey Winter, and it is the worst thing the Barren Order has ever lived through, and it was not violent. It was quiet. The Sealed of Greystead had given up everything to be shut, and one by one, through that winter, they found that they were not. Brother after sister after brother woke carrying. They stopped speaking at meals. They began to count the interval aloud together in the chapter-room, all of them at once, in a low steady murmur that the Hearers could hear through the grey walls from the road, and the Hearers stopped coming. They began to say, to each other and to visiting brothers, the Order's own teaching in a new voice: that the kindest thing a person could do was to start nothing, and that tomorrow was a thing one started. A Hearer who stayed a night that Lack wrote to Callum that it was like a house in which every person had been told the same terrible news at once and was being very gentle with everyone else about it.

They did not do anything. That was what the brothers who came after kept saying. Nobody at Greystead raised a hand to anybody, themselves included, in any way the brothers could point to. They lay down. They walked out over the wall in the night and did not come back. They let a pang pass with their faces to the grey stone and did not get up when it ended. Some were found and some were not. A Sealed brother of sixty, the last one Callum reached in time, said to him only, *It's so tired, brother. It's been carrying us for six hundred years. I wanted to stop being a weight.* He died at Nullhouse in Grace, of nothing the surgeons could name, holding Callum's hand, and he was calm. Thirty-two of the forty-one were gone by spring.

The Grey Winter did not stay at Greystead. That is the part the Order cannot forgive itself. Hearers who had visited carried the counting home with them to Kilnmouth and Nullhouse, and to their families, and the Attendance's book records that winter a cluster of the Pull in the dry southern parishes larger than any it had seen, almost all among people who had never been cut, who had never been in the Course, who had simply sat in a room with someone who had stopped wanting tomorrow. Brother Callum went from house to house through the spring of 640 with his staff and his gentle voice, sitting up with the counting, and he is believed to have kept more than he lost. He reads the thirty-two names at the rim of the Dilation now at every pang, with the struck-through, and he does not raise his voice for them. The Order did not close Greystead. Nine Sealed still live there, and they keep a lamp lit in every empty cell, and the lamps are the only things in the house that are not grey.

[box: Running the Grey Winter]
Use the Grey Winter as tragedy and as warning, never as spectacle. Nothing in it is a method and nothing should be made one at the table; stay with the folded robes, the counting through the wall, the lamps, the mother in Cleave who did not get her letter. Mechanically, it is the rules for **Contagion** and **Despair** working on a closed community: anyone who spends a night in a chapter-house where the Pull has taken hold makes a Dread 3 check, and a failure at Fray 5 or more gives them The Pull toward the Dilation or toward whatever "starting nothing" means to them. Characters who help a house through such a winter by sitting up, counting with, refusing to leave anyone alone in a room, should be rewarded the way the rules reward a rite sincerely performed. The Barren Order would very much like to know how to stop it happening again. So would the Attendance. Neither knows.
[/box]

### The Foundling Companies

Regiments of the born-grown: adults in body, a few years old in fact, with no childhoods and no one to claim them. Raised in 635 A.G., they are the state's only army: six Companies of about four hundred each, housed in the Crèche, drilled in a single year from naked bewilderment to formation. They are disciplined beyond any army on the Table, because they have never known anything else, and frightened in a way their officers do not discuss, because none of them knows how long they will last.

**Beliefs.** The Companies believe in the Company. Born with nobody, they have made the regiment mother and father. They love their General without reservation. **Wants.** Somewhere to belong after the war that never comes. A birthday. Some want to find whoever, or whatever, bore them.

### The Uncut

People whose birth cords were never severed. Somewhere in each Uncut family a mother refused the Attendant's cord-knife, and her child walked into life still attached by yards of living cord that grows with them and does not wither. They live in long joined houses in Cordway and a few Kest courts, great families netted together by pale, warm, pulsing ropes of flesh, sharing through them warmth, blood, pain, and a slow tidal emotion outsiders find unbearable to stand near. A cord cannot be cut without both ends bleeding; the Uncut say both ends die, and they are usually right. They consider everyone else amputees.

**Beliefs.** That the first wound every person suffers is the severing; that the Attendance's knife at the cord is the same knife it uses for the Second Opinion, a little smaller. That nobody should ever be alone. That the cords are Vey's, and Vey is a mother, and the land in labor is a mother, and when it delivers, its child will be Uncut too, and will hold them all.

**Wants.** The right to bear uncut by law. An end to the Attendance's cord-knife. To be attached, through the deep cords that some of their elders have let grow down into the clay, to the land itself.

| 1 | Grafted | A borrowed cord sewn on by the family; outsider kin. |
| 2 | Born-Joined | A true cord; a place in the long house. |
| 3 | Knot | A household of cords meets in you; you speak for them. |
| 4 | Root | Your cord goes into the ground. Visions; the land's pulse. |

## Cradlewrack Now

Nine days between pangs, and the Dilation has begun to move with them: at each contraction the great bowl widens by a stride and then eases back by less. The Rimwatch stakes have to be reset every morning. Madder Rudge says the hole has a pulse, and that lately there is a second one under it, faster and smaller, like a heart inside a heart.

The Assemblers' figure is more than half complete, eleven feet long and breathing, and it has started to turn its head toward the Sill. It lacks a left hand and a voice. Hester Lowe has the hand. Juniper Dell, perhaps, has half the voice. The Assemblers know the first and suspect the second.

The Attendance is split between midwives who still trust the knife and midwives who think it is six centuries too late. Sabeth Thorne, its most trusted Opinion, hesitated last winter, and the thing she spared is growing in a shepherd's loft in Tarnbrow. The Queen Dowager's son has begun to answer questions nobody asked. The Duchess Morwen's veil has gone red. The Tally is eleven hundred short of explanation, and the Border Marshals are turning back more travelers every week, because more of them are carrying, including men who did not know.

Everyone can do the arithmetic on the interval. Something is going to be born out of the ground, and the whole country is the mother.

### The Unknotters

Not every highlander who believes the Long Labor is content to wait for it. Since the interval reached eleven days, a sect has been gathering at Rimwatch that the pilgrims call the Unknotters and that calls itself the Daughters of the Lip. They are mostly women, mostly from the western ravines, mostly mothers who have heard a ruled line read against their household more than once; their leader is a former parish Attendant of Rudge named Grisel Ashe, who resigned her apron in 637 and walked to the rim. They believe that the land is in labor and stalled, and that a stalled labor must be helped, and that the god in the ground is drawing toward itself everything in the highlands that has ever been opened, and that the kindest thing to do for a person deep in the Course is to *give back what they are carrying*: to open every opening they have ever had, at the rim, at the height of a pang, so that it all goes down into the mother at once.

They call it **the Unknotting**, and it is a killing. It has been done nine times that the Attendance knows of, always to a Course-touched man or woman, sometimes willing, more often not, always at the rim of the Dilation, always at night, always at a pang. The Attendance has heard a witness.

The Attendance has a warrant for Grisel Ashe and has not served it, because she is never alone at Rimwatch and because the Daughters of the Lip are now perhaps sixty, and because a Senior of the Lying has privately observed that the interval after each Unknotting has not shortened. It has not lengthened either. That is the problem with every sacrifice the highlands have ever offered: the god does not say thank you, and it does not say no.

### Hester Lowe

A clay-digger's widow of Rudge, thirty-four, broad, sunburned, with a cleft chin and a laugh like a crow. Three years ago she bore a hand: only a hand, left, healthy, warm, the size of a grown woman's, with a ring-finger callus nobody can explain. She did not call the Attendance. She hides it from the Lying-In and the Assemblers both, in a lined bread-crock under the hearthstone, and she feeds it warm milk through the skin of the palm, and talks to it while she works. ## Playing a Cradlewracker

You were born under the Two Questions. Somebody looked at you for the count of three and let you stay. You knock before you open anything, you carry needle and thread against the night, and you know the interval as well as your own name. Wherever you go on the Table, the doors drift open behind you.

**Attribute bonus:** +1 CAUL.

**The Sop: Let It Out.** Open something that was meant to stay shut: break into a bricked room, cut a stitch from a closed wound (yours or another's), unbar a door against its keeper's will, open a grave, or attend a birth to its end. Removes 1d6 Hunger. Each time, the god learns the way: the next closed thing you pass will ache at you until you open that too.

**The Tooth.** When you Partake, Vey begins in you. A low cramp rolls through your belly; the nearest door, lid, or knot within ten paces comes undone; your oldest scar turns pink and wet; the air smells of iron and warm salt. A Tooth showing 1 means the god bit back as a contraction: you are doubled over and lose your next movement.

### What It Is Like

You count. You cannot help it, and abroad people will notice: you count stairs, breaths, the days since anything. You knock on doors in Brinehollow inns and the landlord stares. You wake in the night at the click of a latch three rooms away. You carry needle and gut and you know your stitch-count the way other people know their age, and when it goes up you do not talk about it. You have been examined, stripped to the waist in a cold parish room, more times than you can remember, and you have opinions about strangers' hands on you that you do not share. You have stood at the dusk reading and heard a ruled line read against someone you knew, and turned your face away for a count of three, and turned back. You know someone with a hand in a crock. You may be someone with a hand in a crock.

And your body has never been entirely yours. That is the thing outlanders do not understand. You grew up knowing that any morning you might wake carrying, whatever you are, whatever you have done or not done, and that the Attendance would come, and that what you bore would be asked two questions you would not be let answer. Some Cradlewrackers leave the highlands to get away from that. They find that the god goes with them: a door ajar in a Fatlands farmhouse, warm red soil under a Vigil cot, an old scar opening at a pang they are three hundred miles from and feel anyway. Cradlewrackers abroad still keep the interval. Most of them say they would rather not know it. None of them can stop.

### Gifts

**The Taste (Regrowth 1–3).** Choose one.
- *Knock.* By laying your palm on any door, wall, lid or skin, you know whether something living is on the other side and roughly how big. Costs nothing; but everything on the other side knows you knocked.
- *First Breath.* Once per session, after any roll to begin something (the first attack of a fight, the opening line of a negotiation, the first step of a journey), you may reroll it. Something nearby that was shut opens.

**The Appetite (Regrowth 4–6).** Choose one.
- *Undoing.* By touch and a Partake, open any lock, knot, bar, seal, or stitched wound, with no roll. Bricking resists: Godsign vs Grim 18.
- *Rehearsal.* During a pang or a fight you may enter false labor deliberately: for the scene you ignore Ruin from the Mangling table (take the Injury but do not collapse) and add +2 to Endure rolls, then gain 2 Hunger and are helpless for an hour afterward.

**The Course (Regrowth 7–9).** Choose one.
- *Bear.* Once per session, after a day of carrying, deliver a Part that serves you: a hand that Filches at Threat 3, an ear that hears through walls, an eye that watches while you sleep. Its loyalty lasts until it meets another Part. Every delivery is Dread 2 for witnesses, and the Attendance wants to know about it.
- *What Is Opened Once.* With a Partake and a touch, reopen every healed wound a target has ever had: 1 Flesh per Injury they carry plus 1d6, ignoring Armor. Your own scars open in sympathy; lose 2 Flesh.

**The Brink (Regrowth 10–11).** You are crowning. Somewhere on your body is a soft round opening that cannot be closed, and something in it is pressing toward the light.
- *Crowning.* Once per scene, the thing within may push forth to act for you: a single attack at +9 (4), a single answer to a question about what is beginning, or a single door opened anywhere you can see. Each use is Dread 3 for anyone watching, including you, and adds 2 Hunger.

### The Wants

Resisted with Caul + Resolve at the listed difficulty when triggered.
- **Leave It Open** (Taste onward, Routine 10; Hard 14 at the Appetite). Trigger: shutting a door, a box, a book, or a wound behind you. You simply do not.
- **Attend** (Appetite onward, Hard 14). Trigger: hearing or learning of a birth within a mile. You go, and you stay until the end.
- **Begin** (Appetite onward, Hard 14). Trigger: being offered a plan, a road, a quarrel, a bargain. You start it at once, before anyone has finished speaking.
- **Gather** (Course onward, Grim 18). Trigger: a Part, or a wound, or a severed piece of anything living. You must keep it, and you must bring it close to the others.

### The Seating

At Regrowth 12 a Cradlewracker does not become a place, as Fatlanders do, or a Hush, as the Hand-lines do. You hear the interval in your own body and it is the land's. You walk home, whatever your road, and you walk to the Dilation, and the ground opens to let you down. You go into the red, into the warm, and you are gone from play. At the GM's choice, something comes back up some pangs later: born-grown, with your face and none of your memories, or with all your memories and someone else's face, and it asks the watchers at the rim what year it is. Players who have lost a character this way may meet them again in the Adversaries chapter, wearing whatever is coming.

### Background Concepts

- **The Apprentice Who Quit.** You held the door for your first Second Opinion and ran. You still have the stitching kit and the dreams. The Attendance remembers you, and wants you back in the room. (Stitching, Resolve, Instinct.)
- **Foundling Soldier.** Born grown, mid-stride, four or five years ago. You can strip a crossbow blind but you cried when you first saw snow. You do not know who bore you and you do not know when you will go home. (Blades, Endure, Wayfaring.)
- **Grafted to the Uncut.** You took the cord as an adult, for love or penance, and you can feel your adopted family's pulse a hundred miles away. When you leave Cradlewrack, the cord stretches. It has not snapped yet. (Endure, Persuade, Godsign.)
- **Caul-line Cadet.** A minor child of a veiled house, born with a caul that came off, which makes you a disgrace and a free woman. You still see a little forward, blurred and red. (Instinct, Clause, Rites.)

- **Part-Hunter.** You worked the western ravines with a crate and a kind voice, for the Barn or for the Odd Fold, and you are very good at getting mothers to open the crock. You have a list in your head of every hearthstone in three parishes. Somebody's hand once closed on your finger and would not let go, and you have not been the same. (Search, Persuade, Stealth.)
- **Struck Through.** The Tally says you were not born. You have seen your own ruled line on Tally Hill. Somebody took you out of the Low Wall, or the Opinion counted too long, or the knot came undone, and you grew up somewhere quiet. You do not know what you are. Neither does anyone else, and the Attendance would like to ask. (Stealth, Endure, Instinct.)
- **Walk Family.** Your grandmother went down the Apron Stair in 619. Her apron came back up last Lack, laundered, and you have been dreaming of her since, arms spread in a long warm room. You left the highlands to stop dreaming. It did not work. (Rites, Resolve, Lore.)

### The Four Questions in the Highlands

When you build a Cradlewracker, answer these with the Carver as well as the core four:
- **What did they answer when you were born?** Spoken, old, grown, whole? Who was the Opinion, and do you know their name?
- **Who in your household has a ruled line?** A sibling, a child, a parent's first? Do you go to the Low Wall on the day?
- **What have you carried?** Nothing yet, a false labor, a Part you kept, a Part you gave? Who knows?
- **Whose brick have you sworn on?** By the shut door, on whom, and for what? It will matter.
