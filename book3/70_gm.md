# The Carver's Knife

> At a feast on the Table, the one who carves decides who eats first, who gets the best of it, and who is served the part that nobody wants. It is a position of honor. It is never a position of love.
>> — Dame Suet Marrable, to a new Carver at the Unending Dinner

[fiction]
**From the commonplace book of a Rim Road storyteller, found at Thimble Cross after the snows.**
They always ask me for the one about the god that smiled. I tell it the same way every time. I lower the lamp. I let the fire burn down until their faces are only the parts the coals can reach: noses, wet eyes, the shine on the lips. I wait until somebody coughs, and then I wait for them to be ashamed of having coughed.
The trick is not the monster. Anyone can describe a monster. The trick is to describe the kitchen first. The bread on the board, the knife beside it, the knot in the wood under the bread that looks like an eye. The child asleep in the corner with her thumb in her mouth. You make them love the kitchen. Then, when the knife moves on its own, they do not scream because of the knife. They scream because of the bread, and the knot, and the child.
I have told that story four hundred times. Last winter, on Tablenight, I told it in this very room, and when I got to the end there was a place laid at the back table that nobody had laid, and a chair pulled out, and a card with my name on it.
I am writing this down because I have decided not to tell it again.
[/fiction]

This chapter is for the Carver: the player who runs the game. It explains how to run horror on the Table, land by land and scene by scene; how to use the rules without letting them get in the way; how to stage the sacrifices that buy nothing, the cults that feed you before they own you, and the slow madness of a village that has stopped saying its dead aloud. It gives you tables to roll on when the evening sags, twelve campaigns to start from, and one campaign laid out session by session. And it explains, at more length than anything else, how to make sure everyone at the table leaves wanting to come back, even though their characters will not.

[pull] The trick is not the monster. The trick is to describe the kitchen first. | — the Thimble Cross storyteller

## The Carver's Duties

You have five jobs. Everything else is technique.

- **Describe the world** so the Guests can see, smell and taste it.
- **Play everyone else**: the Regent and the beggar, the god and the dog.
- **Apply the rules** fairly, and know when to put them away.
- **Keep the table safe**, with Lines, Veils and the Grace, and with your own attention.
- **Make the world move.** The Grace is ending. The gods are growing back whether the Guests act or not. Show them the clock.

A sixth job hides inside the other five: **remember**. The Table is a world of ledgers, tallies, counts and long memories. Keep a notebook of every name the Guests learn, every kindness they do, every promise they make, every corpse they step over. The Table remembers all of it, and so should you. Nothing on the Table is more frightening than a stranger in the next land who already knows what the Guests did in the last one.

[pull] The Carver is the only one at the table who is not hungry. Do not let that make you cruel. Let it make you watchful. | — from a Rim Road gamesmaster's notes

## Running Horror on the Table

### Make Them Love the Kitchen

Horror is the violation of something cared for. If the Guests do not care about anything, nothing can be violated. Spend the first half of every story making them care: a warm inn, a Kept grandmother who tells filthy jokes through her stitched jaw, a Fatlands cook who sings while she skims the drip from the stockpot, a Netwatch sergeant who calls his patrol "my ducks". Let the Guests eat, rest, joke and plan. Let Who Waits at Home write them letters. Then take it away.

The kitchen does not have to be safe to be loved. A Vigil nudger who slaps a Guest awake every hour, and apologizes every time, is a kitchen. So is a Forsworn beggar in the Breakdowns whose ribs open like a book and who shares his soup anyway. Look for the small habit, the joke repeated, the pet name, the chipped cup. Name it twice in two sessions. On the third session, break it.

### All Five Senses, and Then One More

The Table is a world of bodies. Describe it through the body. For every horror you run, ask:

- **What does it smell like?** The sweet wrongness of the Breath in Sallowreach; the rendering-yard stink of the Belch; salt and kelp and the cold-mineral smell of a drowned man's lungs; lamp-smoke and sour rouse-sweat in the Vigil; iron, wet clay and birth-water in Cradlewrack; the hot stone and dry silence of Oathen; and, in the Fast, bread, always fresh bread, from nowhere.
- **What does it sound like?** Flies in fronts. Teeth in the wheat. Bailing coughs at dawn. Bells every quarter hour. The pang, felt through the floor. The crack of a body breaking to match a broken word.
- **What does it feel like?** Warm ground that rises and falls. Cold grey fingers with one joint too many. A floor that slopes so you cannot rest.
- **What does it taste like?** Everything on the Table is food, or was. Make them taste it.
- **What does it look like?** Last. Sight is the least frightening sense, because it is the one we trust.
- **And the sixth sense, the god:** what does the Guest's own blood do, in the presence of this? Does their Hunger stir? Does their Tooth itch? Does the thing in front of them seem, horribly, like family?

A useful discipline: describe every horror in three details, never more, and make one of them a smell and one of them a texture. *The room is warm. It smells of milk going over. When you put your hand on the doorframe, the wood gives, a little, like a gum.* Then stop, and wait for the players to ask the question you want them to ask.

[pull] Sight is the sense we trust. That is why it is the last one you should use. | — the Carver's rule of three

### Gore With a Purpose

This book is graphic, and your table may want it graphic. That is allowed and it can be powerful. The rule is: **gore should always carry meaning.** A body opened on the Table is never just a body. It tells the Guests what the god wants, what the land costs, what was done and by whom. A Mangling is a story in a wound. When you describe the bowel sliding free of a Netwatch officer's belly, make sure the Guests also see the wedding knot tied into his anchoring rope.

When the table's appetite is satisfied, cut away. Restraint is a tool too. The door closing on the Cullmaster's dusk shift, and the sound of the wheat moving in a field where there is no wind, can be worse than anything you say.

Gore lands hardest on familiar shapes. A stranger's opened chest is a fact; the hand of the innkeeper who poured your wine last night, found in a barrel of salt pork with the signet ring still on it, is a story. Reuse your props. The Table is a world where nothing is wasted, and neither should your descriptions be.

### Tragedy, Not Spectacle

Some of this book's horrors are deliberately human: suicide, contagious despair, kin-slaying, the murder of the helpless. Run them as tragedy. That means:

- The camera stays on the people left behind: the cut rope on the bed, the extra place at the table, the note in a careful hand, the neighbors who stop calling names through the wall.
- Nobody is a punchline, and nobody's death is a reward.
- The god benefits. Every loss is a little more of it in the world. That is the horror, more than the act.
- You never describe *how*. You describe *after*.

When a Guest's own despair is in play (the Pull, the Finishers' song, the Bedded's sermon), slow down and speak to the player, not just the character. The rules for Despair in the Grace Roll chapter require the table's consent for any ending. Honor that requirement as if your own hand were on the rope.

### The Clock Is Always Running

The Grace is ending. Use clocks: the Cradlewrack interval shortening, the Vigil's empty beds, the Pantry's four months of grain, the Brinehollow waterline, the Sallowreach Hushes spreading toward the Hand-line estates. Write each one on a card where the Guests can see it. Advance them between sessions whether the Guests act or not. Horror is helplessness, and the slow tick of a number going down is helplessness made visible.

A clock is more frightening when it is somebody's. Do not write *Vigil: 1,912 beds*; write *Matron Dorm's count: 1,912*, and when it drops, have Matron Dorm be the one who tells them, in the voice of a woman who has stopped sleeping in a city where nobody sleeps.

### No Rescue, but Many Mercies

The Table cannot be saved. That is the premise. But individual people can be: for a night, a season, a life. A Guest who cannot stop the Reaping can still get one family across the border before the dusk shift. A Guest who cannot cure the Vigil can still sit up with a dying nudger through her last hour. Those small mercies are the only victories the Long Grace offers, and they should feel enormous. Do not take them away cheaply. Take them away expensively, much later, when the Guests have forgotten to guard them.

[pull] We could not save the town. We saved the Gristle boy. He is thirty now and he sends a ham every Carving. That is what winning looks like up here. | — a road-warden at Saltlick Cross

## Running Each Land's Horror

Every land on the Table is frightening in a different key. A Carver who runs the Vigil like Sallowreach will get a competent session and miss the land. What follows is a short guide to each: its **tone** (the feeling you are aiming at), its **senses** (the details to reach for first), its **signature scenes** (the moments a session there should contain), and its **pacing** (how to shape time at the table so that the god is felt in the rhythm of play itself).

### Sallowreach: The Horror of Going On

[sigil: sallowreach]

**Tone.** Elegy and black comedy, in equal parts, forever. Nothing finishes here: not grief, not quarrels, not rot, not pain. The horror is not death but its absence, and the dreadful patience of people who have hurt for three centuries and are still polite. Let the Kept be funny. Then let a Guest notice that the grandmother laughing at the joke has laughed at it, in that exact cadence, since before the War of the Heirs.

**Senses.** The Breath: warm, wet, sweetish fog tasting of mushrooms and old flowers. Cedar on the rich, vinegar on the middling, and on the poor the smell of themselves. Flies in fronts, pattering on lantern glass like hail. The creak of wire in a restitched shoulder; jaw-clicks in a dark room. And at the edge of a Hush, nothing at all: no echo, no flies, your own heartbeat missing from your ears.

**Signature scenes.**
- A restitching at the supper table, between courses, while the patient asks after the Guests' journey.
- The queue at the Hall of Closings, where a Kept child has waited since her parents were young.
- A night in the Lofts, the shelves whispering from floor to rafter, all the same word.
- The edge of a Hush at dawn, birds dropping one at a time, the Finishers singing on the road behind.

**Pacing.** Never let a scene end cleanly. Cut away while it is still going: the argument running, the patient mid-sentence. The unfinishedness piles up. Then, once a session, end something absolutely, and let the silence at your real table run longer than is comfortable. Count ten before you speak.

[pull] May you finish. | — the polite greeting of Sallowreach, said with real love

### The Fatlands: The Horror of Plenty

[sigil: fatlands]

**Tone.** Grotesque hospitality. The south is warm, loud, generous and doomed, and its horror wears an apron and a smile. Nobody means harm: the Cullmasters think of it as agriculture, the Bailiff is gentle with his funnel, Brother Glut means every word about love. Run the land as a feast that never ends, where refusing is the only crime and the food is looking back at you.

**Senses.** Gravy from the drains. The Belch: tallow and singed hair on a hot wind. Ground warm underfoot that rises, very slightly, if you lie on it. The crunch of a tooth in a mouthful of bread. Fruit with a skin, a fat layer and a pit of bone, which bleeds a little when bitten. A cellar that smells like the inside of a mouth.

**Signature scenes.**
- A meal the Guests cannot refuse, the Bailiff's ladle of office laid beside the salt.
- A wake at which the dead are served on the best plates, and a Guest is handed the honored portion.
- The dusk shift walking into wheat that moves with no wind.
- Pell Hogget's Long Count read aloud, one higher than last year, and a mother at the back hearing why.

**Pacing.** Build every session around meals. Five a day is the law: cut scenes with bells for second breakfast and fourth dinner, and give refusals consequences. Daylight is the other clock. Mark the hour on a card and move it; when dusk comes, everyone in the fields is in danger.

### Brinehollow: The Horror of Wanting to Go

[sigil: brinehollow]

**Tone.** Longing, grief and the domestic stranger. Brinehollow is a land of the beloved who walk away and the beloved who come back wrong. Its horror is the invitation: everyone half-wants it, and the whole culture of anchoring, counting and bail-buckets is a set of tender, ridiculous measures for holding on to people who are leaving. Play it quiet, in a minor key.

**Senses.** Salt on the lips at waking. The wet, tearing cough of a whole street bailing at dawn. Kelp on the cellar stairs forty miles inland. The cold-mineral smell of a drowned chest opened. Pressure days: ears popping, windows bowing in, a thread of blood from a child's nose. Rope creaking on a bed in the night.

**Signature scenes.**
- The morning count: names called through the walls, house by house, until one house does not answer.
- A rope found cut at dawn, its end neat, and wet footprints going west.
- Dinner with a Tenanted husband who is kinder than he ever was, and pauses, sometimes, before he chews.
- The Netwatch hauling a Called girl out of the surf, and the look she gives the Guest holding the rope.

**Pacing.** Brinehollow runs on tides. At dawn and dusk every scene stops so the Brinehollowers can bail; let it be an interruption, and then a comfort. Build the Calling like weather across the session, a west window here, a sleepwalk there, until a Guest stands up in the night. Do not rush the walk to the shore.

### The Vigil: The Horror of Staying Awake

[sigil: vigil]

**Tone.** Fever. The Vigil is bright, loud, raw, paranoid and very tired, the one land where the threat is comfort. Its horror is attrition: judgment fraying, hallucination entered as news, murder in a two-second blink. Play it fast and jittery, and let the city be magnificent in its exhaustion.

**Senses.** Permanent lamp-smoke in hair and throat. Rouse: bitter, gritty, iron-and-burnt-coffee, staining teeth brown. Bells every quarter hour; tin roofs clattering in the rain; the prick of a pin offered at the door. Sour collars, rotten-sweet breath. And the danger signs, which you must describe as delicious: a warm wind, a soft chair, someone humming.

**Signature scenes.**
- A blink: a Guest opens their eyes three paces from where they were, holding something.
- A Seeming: a whole street sees the same thing, and a clerk takes it down as fact.
- The Dormitory: sixty thousand numbered beds, and every sleeper breathing in time.
- A Tucker's minute, the sand running out, and the client who does not wake.

**Pacing.** Never give the characters rest. Cut scenes mid-action with a blink and resume three paces on. When a Guest's Fray is high, describe events slightly out of order, then correct yourself, as a tired person would. Tap a glass every so often and ask one Guest what keeps them awake this quarter. (Your real players should still take real breaks; see Safety in Practice.)

[pull] Pinch me. | — the Vigil's marriage vow, entire

### Cradlewrack: The Horror of Beginning

[sigil: cradlewrack]

**Tone.** Obstetric dread and thresholds. The land is in labor, and everything in it is about to begin: doors, wounds, bellies, hillsides. Its horror is anticipation. Something is always on the other side of a door, and the culture of knocking, bricking-in and the Two Questions is a culture of bracing. Play it close, warm and wet, in the rhythm of contractions.

**Senses.** Iron, wet red clay and birth-water. Ground warm to the hand, faintly pulsing. Latches lifting by themselves at night. The pang through the floor, a slow enormous clench, and the whole country holding its breath. Brick-dust on the hands of people who wall themselves in each night.

**Signature scenes.**
- A birth, the Two Questions asked and answered by the midwife alone, her hand on the Second Opinion.
- A Guest waking in a bricked room to a gap in the mortar and something small on the floor.
- A crowning, the village gathered at the rim of a round hole, waiting to see what comes up.
- A warm ear or a length of spine crawling, patiently, toward another Part.

**Pacing.** Count days. Every session should know how many days until the next pang, and build to one. Open scenes with a door already ajar. When the pang comes, have everyone put their hands flat on the real table while you describe the clench. Then let the land relax, and remind them the interval is shorter.

### Oathen: The Horror of Saying So

[sigil: oathen]

**Tone.** Silence, precision and legal dread. Oathen is dry and courteous, and every word in it is a loaded crossbow. Its horror is the gap between what you meant and what you said, and the sound a body makes when that gap closes.

**Senses.** Hot red stone under a white sky; lips cracking in the dry. A silence so deep you hear a sandal drop three terraces down. The echo, your words returning a half-second late. The crack of a breaking, like green wood, and then a scream, or worse, no scream. Ink, grit, cut stone.

**Signature scenes.**
- A negotiation in which every sentence is mostly exits, and a Guest says one plain thing.
- An echo carrying a careless word, three times, to someone who never said it.
- A Breaking trial: the accused repeats the promise aloud, and the court watches.
- A Forsworn man in the Breakdowns, his moral history written in his bones.

**Pacing.** Tell the players, out of character, that you will hold their characters to what they say, and then do it with grace: when a character speaks carelessly, pause and ask, "Is that a promise?" Let them unsay it with a conditional. The pause is the horror. Oathen sessions should be the slowest you run.

[pull] Say less. | — the whole of Oathen's law, as taught to children

### The Fast: The Horror of Being Welcome

[sigil: fast]

**Tone.** Temptation, piety and kindness that kills. The Fast is gold, quiet and almost empty, its people starving and righteous, its god gentle and endlessly patient. Nothing here attacks. It only offers, and every refusal is harder than the last, because what is offered is exactly what the Guests want.

**Senses.** Fresh bread from nowhere. Late-afternoon light at every hour. Frost melted in long stooping footprints. Clean windows in empty houses; the smell of a kitchen on a winter evening with everyone home. And among the Unfed: cold hands, sour breath, grain counted to the bean.

**Signature scenes.**
- A table steaming in the road, a chair pulled out, a card in a Guest's mother's hand.
- The Decline said aloud three times, and a fourth offer from someone who should not be able to make one.
- The Long Table: nine miles of the Seated, describing each course, inviting the Guests to sit.
- The Pantry list read in Wanting, with a village left off it.

**Pacing.** Very quiet, very slow, very few events. Long walks through beautiful empty country. Escalate the offers: a smell, a lit window, a laid table, a card, a card with the name of their dead. Ask the players to say the Decline in their own voices.

### The Rim: The Horror of In-Between

[sigil: rim]

The Rim belongs to no land and borrows every land's horror for a mile either side of a border. Run it as a road story: inns, tolls, the knee-high Hem that nobody steps over, milestones with their distances carved over. Its own horror is commerce. Everything has a price, including the dead of six lands, and the Company is pricing the end of the world in a ledger nobody may read. Use the Rim to carry the Guests between keys, and to remind them, at every inn on Tablenight, of the extra chair turned to face the wall.

## Using the Rules

### When to Call for Dread

Call for a Dread check the first time the Guests encounter a horror, and whenever something they love is harmed. Do not call for Dread so often that Fray becomes accounting. If a Guest is a Seamer, corpses do not frighten her; what frightens her is a corpse that thanks her. Calibrate to the character.

A good rule of thumb is two or three Dread checks in a session, rising to five or six in a climax. If a session has gone by without one, the Guests have not met the Table yet. If every scene has one, the Table has stopped being frightening and become weather.

### Pacing Hunger

The Tooth is the most important choice a player makes. Make it tempting: put the Guests in positions where +3.5 is the difference between saving someone and failing them. Make it visible: describe the god coming forward every time they Partake. Make it count: when Hunger fills and Regrowth rises, stop the game for a moment and describe the change together. A Guest who reaches the Course should feel the weight of it. In a typical campaign of twenty sessions, a Guest who Partakes freely will reach the Course; one who Partakes recklessly will reach the Brink.

When a Tooth shows a 1 and the god bites back, give it a voice. Not words, usually, but a sensation peculiar to the land: a joint popping in a Hand-line's finger, a mouthful of brine, a sudden scald of milk in the throat. Players learn to fear the small sounds of the dice.

### When the Seating Comes

Treat a Guest's Seating as the finale of their story, not as a failure. Talk with the player beforehand. Give them a last scene: a last speech, a last kindness, a last act of terrible power. Then let the god have them. Bring them back later as part of the world, and let the other Guests visit what is left. The Dowager Hills are full of grandmothers who were once someone's companions on the road.

### Fudging and Fairness

Roll in the open whenever you can. The Table is a cruel place, and the dice are the most honest thing in it. If a Guest dies to a bad roll in a meaningless fight, that is a sign you should not have run a meaningless fight. Make fights matter, warn players when they are walking into death, and let the dice fall.

### Saying Yes to the God

Players will look for loopholes: Kept characters who use their undying bodies as shields; Oathen Guests who swear things into existence; Brinehollowers who try to bargain with their Tenants. Let them. The gods are not interested in rules-lawyering, but they are very interested in being invited in. Every loophole a Guest finds is a door the god can walk through. Say yes, and then show them who else came through it.

### Running Contagion

Madness on the Table is catching, and the Contagion rule is one of the most powerful tools in this book. When a Guest Breaks, do not rush past the witnesses' checks. Describe the cold draft moving through the room: the second person who begins to count, the third who looks at her husband and sees a stranger wearing him. A shared Derangement is a shared story, and players who share one will play off each other with a terrible, delighted intimacy. Use it sparingly. One contagious Break per story is plenty. Two is a plague.

## Running Worthless Sacrifices

> The phrase *to walk the eldest* means, in every dialect, to make a sacrifice you know will be wasted.
>> — the Cutwrights' College, *Concordance of the Nine Years*

The Table's history begins with a sacrifice that bought nothing. Sixty thousand firstborn walked to the feet of their gods and opened their throats, and nothing happened, and in Carving their families came back for them with sacks. Every land since has its own version: the Fatlands' fasting rebels force-fed into silence, the Vigil's diver who brought back the number and had her bed added to it, the Oathen regiment that came apart obeying an order. The adventure in this book ends with one: someone sits in the ninth chair to save the others, and the Host lifts them gently out of it and goes on with dinner.

Done badly, this feels like a trick played on the players. Done well, it is the moment a campaign is remembered by.

### The Three Parts

A worthless sacrifice has three parts, and each needs its own weight.

**The Demand.** Someone, or the Guests' own reasoning, says a price must be paid. It should come from a source they have reason to trust: a doctrine, an elder, an old carving, a Cutwright's ledger, a dying friend. Let them argue and look for other ways. Let the demand survive their scrutiny, because it is the kind of thing that sounds true on the Table.

**The Giving.** Play it in full. Whether the price is a life, a hand, a child, a name or a winter's food, give it the care you would give a Seating. Slow down. Let the giver say what they want to say. The Giving must be real, because the horror depends entirely on its being real.

**The Indifference.** Then nothing happens. Not a reversal; not a punishment; not a cruel twist. The world simply goes on as it was going. The Hush keeps spreading. The bed fills anyway. The god does not look up. Describe the after: the family going home, the empty place at the table, the price paid and the receipt blank. Let the players sit in it. Call for a Dread check at Dread 5: *knowing that what you did changed nothing.*

[pull] It is not that the gods refused the eldest. It is that they did not notice them. | — Confessor Abel Rennick

### Rules for the Carver

- **Never more than once an arc.** A worthless sacrifice is a hammer. If every sacrifice in your campaign is worthless, the players will stop making them, and they will be right to.
- **Never as a trick on the players.** The demand should be false in the fiction, not rigged by you. If the players have read the clues correctly and concluded that the sacrifice will not work, let them be right, and let an NPC go ahead and do it anyway.
- **Let some sacrifices work.** Small ones, close ones, human ones. A Guest who gives up their last ration to a starving child should see the child eat. The Table is indifferent at the scale of gods, not at the scale of bread.
- **Reward the giving, not the outcome.** Award Portions for the scene. A Guest who keeps an Oath on the Dead by sacrificing, even uselessly, still keeps it.
- **Give the waste a witness.** The worst part of the Eldest Walk was that the whole village walked behind the line to the boundary stone and stopped there. Put someone at the boundary stone.

## Cult Control and Gaslighting

The Table is full of people who have found an answer: the Finishers, the Second Helping, the Deepening, the Bedded, the Assemblers, the Barren Order, the Purgation, the Second Table, the Seated on the Long Table. Each of them is, in its way, right about something. That is what makes them frightening. A cult on the Table is not a gang of villains in hoods; it is a warm room full of people who have stopped being afraid, and who would like you to stop too.

What follows explains how to run scenes in which the Guests are drawn in, made to doubt their own memories, or watch someone they love disappear into certainty. These are the most intimate horrors in the book, and need the most care.

### How a Cult Feeds You

Every cult on the Table works by the same five courses. Run them in order, one per scene or one per session, and the players will feel the pull before they understand it.

- **The Welcome.** The Guests are fed, warmed, listened to. Nobody asks them for anything. Somebody remembers their name. In a world of tolls and ledgers and Cutwrights, this is the most seductive thing that can happen.
- **The Answer.** Somebody explains the world. The explanation is coherent, kind and partly true. It accounts for the Guests' own godsign, their dead, their fears. It makes sense of the Table, which nothing else does.
- **The Small Wrong Thing.** The Guests are asked for something small and slightly wrong: to skip a meal, to leave a door unbarred, to say a word they would not usually say, to look away while a sick woman is walked to the shore. Everyone else does it without fuss.
- **The Wall.** Old friends are gently reframed. *Your nudger does not understand you. Your Who Waits at Home is afraid of what you are becoming. We are not afraid.* Letters stop arriving, or arrive opened.
- **The Last Course.** The real ask. Walk into the Hush. Lie down. Put out the lamp. Give the child to the Assemblers. Sit.

[pull] Nobody here has asked me for anything in three weeks. I keep waiting. I think that is how they get you. | — a Vigil Waker's report, from inside the Bedded

### Running the Pull at the Table

The danger in cult scenes is that the players, sitting safely at a real table, will simply refuse, and the scene will go flat. The solution is not to force them. It is to make the cult right about something the players care about, and to put someone they love inside it. Brother Glut cannot persuade a Guest; Brother Glut can persuade the Guest's sister, and then the Guest must decide whether to drag her out.

When you want a Guest to feel the pull from inside, use the rules: a Want triggered by the cult's rite; a Dread check failed in the cult's presence and turned into **The Devotion** derangement; a Fray-recovering rite that works, really works, and leaves the Guest wanting more. Tell the player what their character feels and let them decide what to do with it. Never tell a player what their character believes.

### Gaslighting Scenes

Gaslighting, on the Table, is not rare. It is a civic institution. The Vigil enters hallucinations into the public record as news. The Board of Plenty records the Reaping as wolves. The Admiralty forbids the word *stranded*. In Oathen, a careful Clausewright can get a witness to swear to a version of events, and then the body enforces it. Run gaslighting scenes in four moves:

- **The Contradiction.** An NPC calmly contradicts something a Guest saw. *There was no second body. There were never two.* Everyone in the room agrees.
- **The Record.** A document confirms the contradiction: a ledger, a Tally entry, the Seeming's slate. The handwriting is genuine. The date is right.
- **The Kindness.** Someone the Guest trusts gently suggests they are tired, Rattled, Fraying. They are not wrong. The Guest's Fray is high.
- **The Doubt.** Call for an Eye + Instinct roll against the deceiver's Tongue + Deceive. On a failure, the character believes the lie for now; on a failure at Fray 5+, gain 1 Fray and, if they Break this session, the Derangement is **The Gaslight**.

**The Carver's Promise.** Gaslight the characters. Never gaslight the players. When the fiction lies to a Guest, the player must still be able to trust you. The simplest way to keep this promise is to say, plainly and out of character, *Your character now believes there was only ever one body. You, the player, remember what I described.* Some groups enjoy playing with an unreliable Carver: descriptions that turn out to have been a character's hallucination, scenes replayed differently. That is a strong spice. Agree it at the session zero, by name, and stop at once if anyone stops enjoying it.

[pull] The slate says the street saw a horse. I saw a woman with her arms on backwards. The slate is the law, so I saw a horse. | — overheard at the Pinchmarket, the Vigil

### The Cult Inside the Party

The worst cult is the one a Guest founds, around the Course, when they discover their god has a doctrine and it is persuasive. Let them preach if the group enjoys it, and give the other players the tools and reasons to resist. A party split by belief is a tragedy the Table loves. A player bullied by another at the real table is not, and you should stop it.

A village that is losing its mind should be one the players have come to know. ## Building a Campaign

### Choose a Center

Campaigns on the Table work best when they are anchored to one place and one thread, at least to start. Choose:

- **A land.** Begin in one land, and let the Guests learn its rules intimately before they travel.
- **A thread.** Pick one of the *Threads Between the Lands*: the Deadwatch, the Host on the Road, Brine in the Rouse, the Missing Hand, the Sayer's Sentence, Eight Chairs, or the newer threads of the Undying Flame, the Slipping Seal and the Stolen Ledgers. It will pull the Guests across borders when they are ready.
- **A clock.** The land's own crisis, ticking.
- **A patron.** Someone who pays the Guests or protects them, and who will betray them.

### Campaign Frames

**The Caravan.** The Guests are the crew of a Rim Road Company caravan: Factors, Wardens, a cook, a Seamer, a Cutwright on assignment. Each story is a waystation, a cargo, a border. The Company's contracts are sworn in Oathen and cannot be broken; it is only a matter of time until one of them requires something unthinkable.

**The Survey.** The Guests work for the Cutwrights' College, mapping where each god is regrowing fastest. They travel the lands, read bloodlines, and slowly discover that their own ledgers are being used by the Second Table to plan a menu.

**The Purge.** The Guests are a Purgation cell. At first they fast and bleed and pray. Then they are asked to burn a house with a family inside it. What do they believe, and how far will it take them?

**The Watch.** The Guests are Nightwardens in one city: the Netwatch of Lowmark, the Wakers of the Vigil, the Loft-Wardens of Lastgate. Each story is a case. The cases get worse. The city's secret, which the Guests are paid to protect, is the worst of all.

**The Household.** The Guests serve or belong to a single noble family in the last stages of the Regrowth: a Hand-line house where the hands have started to grow; a Gut-line estate where the matriarch is putting down roots; a Caul-line household where the heir has been carried for forty years. Domestic, claustrophobic, unbearable.

**The Pilgrimage.** The Guests are travelers bound for the Fast, for reasons of their own: to find the Host, to find a cure, to find someone who was invited and never came home. The Rim Road is the journey. Hallowboard is the end.

### Escalation

A campaign on the Table should move through four movements, like the Regrowth itself:

| Movement | Sessions | What Happens |
| The Taste | 1–4 | The Guests learn one land. Small horrors. Personal stakes. The god in their blood barely stirs. |
| The Appetite | 5–10 | A thread pulls them across a border. They discover the scale of the Regrowth. They begin to Partake to survive. |
| The Course | 11–18 | The powers of the Table notice them. A Guest is changed beyond hiding. Someone they love is lost. A land's crisis breaks. |
| The Seating | 19+ | The end of the Grace in one land, or all of them. The Secrets of the Table come into play. Somebody sits down. |

### Making the Guests Matter

The Guests cannot save the world. They can decide who sits at the table when it ends. Let their choices shape the shape of the catastrophe: whether the Vigil buys the secret of Keeping, whether the Assemblers' figure gets its hand, whether Lady Imre Dath is unbitted and what she is told to say. A campaign on the Table is a story about which awful thing happens, and who is to blame for it.

[pull] We did not stop it. We chose the night it happened, and who was in the room. I have decided that is not nothing. | — a Clausewright's memoir, unpublished

## Campaign Seeds

Twelve campaigns, a paragraph each, each enough to build a first session from. Most fit any frame; most touch a thread; all of them end badly in some way the players will have chosen.

**1. The Last Mourner's Apprentices.** People are dying again in Sallowreach, properly, inside the Hushes, and nobody remembers how to mourn them. Bram Hollin takes the Guests as apprentices and walks them from Hush-edge to Hush-edge teaching the six-hundred-year-old rite: washing, keening, closing the eyes with a thumb. Midway, a family of the Mortuary Court hires them to mourn it in advance, while still alive, because it knows which Hush is coming next.

**2. The Long Count Riders.** The Board has declared Pell Hogget's tally seditious. The Guests ride the Count between villages at night, gathering numbers: a missing son, a Cullmaster's list, a field with a face in it. The total climbs. Then one village's number falls, and the Guests must learn why its soil has stopped eating, and what the villagers have been feeding it instead.

**3. Matron Dorm's Count.** Sleepers are dying in the Dormitory and the count does not drop, because each empty bed fills by morning with someone nobody saw lie down. Matron Cecily Dorm quietly hires the Guests to find who is removing pillows. The case runs through the Slope, the Tuckers, the Bedded and the Watch of Watches, and ends at the Matron herself, and at Bed One, where Aveline Morne's lips are moving faster.

**4. Nine Days to Kest.** Hester Lowe's hand, the one the Assemblers' figure lacks, has been stolen from its cradle. Each session is one day of the interval. The hand passes between smugglers, midwives, the Uncut and a Vigil anatomist, and holds someone's finger at every stop. On the ninth day the figure in the barn turns its head toward the Sill, and the Guests must decide whose hand to put it in.

**5. The Dry Pier.** Harbormistress Kessa Drowle has one year of timber left and one year of sea. The Guests build Lowmark's last mile across the Uncovered, and every section uncovers something: a wreck standing upright, a drowned village with its tables laid, a Tenanted choir at lowest tide. The last plank is laid at the edge of the Trench, where they can hear the sea draining into somewhere.

**6. The Surety's Default.** Ona Fell carries two hundred and eleven other people's oaths and can survive one more default. The Guests must keep every one of those people honest for a season: talking a husband home, getting a debtor to pay, stopping a soldier running. Each failure shows on Ona's body. The last oath on her list belongs to someone powerful who means to break it on purpose.

**7. The Pantry List.** Four months of grain for six months of winter. The Guests are Crumb-Runners, spat on by the people they feed. They learn which villages Mother Stint Avery has already left off the list, and must choose between running grain to them behind the Abstinent's backs and keeping their own families fed. In the villages left off, tables have begun to appear every night, steaming.

**8. The Candle Caravan.** The Guests guard Wick Tallow's candles east along the Rim to light a city that cannot sleep. The candles can hear; some can be made to talk; some were people the Guests knew. Between Fogmouth and Lampwick Halt they begin whispering the Lofts' word, and one of them, carried past the Drop, does not go out.

**9. The Ledger Run.** The Guests are hired to bring Silas Fenwright in alive with his three stolen quires. At every inn on the chase, someone named on his pages dies. When they finally hold him, they also hold the choice to read the quires, and learn their own lines, and what each ends in. The third quire, unopened, lists every Blank born on the Table in two centuries.

**10. The Steward's Successor.** Welcome Ardent, after three graceful refusals, asks a Guest whether they would consider a position. The campaign follows the steward around the Rim a mile ahead of his god, writing down the names on the cards, learning that the Host's tables are a kind of refuge, and that the extra chair at every table is for nobody Ardent has ever been told about.

**11. The Chair at Toll Nine.** The extra chair from the Long Farewell stands in a locked room at Toll Nine on its square of floor, pulled out a little further every week. The Company hires the Guests to measure it and learn why. The trail leads to a seabed table, a bog body in a College tank, a slate in Wanting and an Archprovost who no longer goes to dinners. This is Eight Chairs as a campaign spine; it ends in the Secrets chapter.

**12. A Practice Dinner.** A Guest of high Cut is invited, on heavy paper, to a private dining room on the Rim. The dish is named afterward. Finish the plate, and the party has a way into the Second Table; refuse, and the Guest is served at the next dinner. The infiltration grows more intimate with every course and ends on Tablenight at Hallowboard, with the Host lying down on the eight-sided table.

[pull] The dish is not named until after. That is the whole of the etiquette. | — a footman of the Second Table, overheard at the Groaning Board

## A Sample Campaign: Laid for Eight

A ten-session outline, built on the Caravan frame and the Eight Chairs thread, which opens with this book's introductory adventure and ends at Hallowboard on Tablenight. It is a campaign of travel, dread and evidence assembled piece by piece, carrying a group from the Taste to the edge of the Secrets. Run it as written or use it as a demonstration of how the parts of this book fit together.

The Guests are a Rim Road Company crew, or travelers who become one after the first story. Their patron is a Company factor (Joss Mardle of Brindle Cross, or one of your own at Toll Nine) who will in the end sell them to the highest bidder, because he has priced them too.

| 1–2 | The Ninth Chair | Thimble Cross | Tablenight 641 |
| 3 | The Drop | Fogmouth | The Deadwatch, Stage 1 |
| 4 | Whispering Shelves | Lastgate | Bettany's word |

**Sessions 1–2: The Ninth Chair.** Run the introductory adventure in full. Whatever happens, the Guests leave with the ninth card and its single mark in the oldest Table-speech, an acquaintance with Welcome Ardent, and the question of whom the ninth place was for. A Company factor wants to know what happened at Thimble Cross. So does someone at Quire.

**Session 3: The Drop.** The Guests escort a Deadwatch trial caravan of twenty volunteers from the Slope to Fogmouth, to be killed and kept so they need never sleep. At the red line, the first dies and stands back up. That night a Kept man a yard north of the Drop is found Seated at a laid table, an empty chair pushed in square beside him, and the innkeeper shows the Guests where the floor was cut out last year.

**Session 4: Whispering Shelves.** The College wants the word Old Bettany Shroud's forty thousand shelved dead are whispering written down by a disinterested hand. A night in the Lofts: reading-aloud, a part-thief at work, Little Nell Ash's court below. The nearest gloss for the word is *company*. Lecturer Odo Fask takes the Guests to Grandfather Peat, who says the god was smiling, counts to eight, and folds one finger down.

**Session 5: A Horse, Entered as News.** The road to Quire runs through the Vigil on a Yawn day: blinks, bells, rouse, a blink-murder for Inspector Grimmer, and a Seeming in which a whole street sees a table laid for eight with an empty chair at the head. Mirela Sconce enters it in the record as a horse, then shows the Guests her private ledger.

**Session 6: Low Water.** Archprovost Hesper Lune, nearly blind, sends the Guests to Brinehollow for a rubbing of a seabed table. With Hask Tarn piloting and Cray Lobbett leading, they cross the Uncovered past the Tenanted choir. Eight chairs; seven pushed in; one pulled out further than Lobbett's knotted cord says it was. By his reckoning, this is one of his last three visits.

**Session 7: Exits.** A Bonded Hand has stolen and not broken. The Guests carry papers to Jessamy Quill, including a short contract for Ardent's relief. **Session 8: The Dusk Shift.** At the Fast's border, Osanna Blythe's three hundred pilgrims are being Reaped from the road at dusk while Marshal Keep Aldane bars the way. ## The Carver's Tools

### Read-Aloud Text

Read-aloud passages in this book are set in the same style as in-world documents. Read them slowly. Pause before the last line. Then stop, and let the silence do the work. Do not explain. The Guests will ask.

### Handouts

The Table is a world of documents: death licenses, Cutwright ledgers, contracts forty pages long, place cards, the Tally, the Long Count, a seemer's report. Give your players paper. A handwritten place card with a Guest's name on it, set at their seat before the session starts, will do more than any monster.

Some handouts that cost nothing and work every time: a Closing petition with the stamp *DEFERRED*; a Fatlands meal-ticket with five boxes a day; a page of the Long Count with one name smudged; a Vigil rouse-tin label with the dose crossed out and raised; an Oathen contract with every sentence ending in an exit; a Pantry ration chit; a Second Table menu with seven courses and a blank eighth line. Age the paper with cold tea. Do not explain anything written on it.

### Names

Every land has its naming customs, listed in its chapter. When you need a name in a hurry: Sallowreach names are soft, mournful and old (Bettany, Osric, Henna, Tam); Fatlands names are hearty and edible (Suet, Brisket, Tansy, Hob); Brinehollow names are wet and coastal (Kessa, Sennet, Hask, Tolly); Vigil names ring like bells (Ilvane, Maud, Pip, Rook); Cradlewrack names are rustic and beginning (Orla, Lark, Juniper, Hinge); Oathen names are clipped and few-syllabled (Ket, Brakk, Rue, Ona); the Unfed name their children for the Refusal (Forbear, Abstain, Decline, Stint, Tarry, Nay, Want, Spare).

### The Carver's Notebook

Keep, in a notebook only you see: the clocks and their current numbers; the thread stages; every NPC's name and one detail; every promise a Guest has made aloud (Oathen will want to know); every Guest's Last Meal, Oath on the Dead, Who Waits at Home and What You Will Not Do. Before each session, pick one of those four for each Guest and decide how the world will touch it tonight. Not every session. Not too hard. But the players should know, by the third session, that you are listening.

[pull] Write down what they promise. The canyon does. | — Carver's advice, Oathen edition

## Safety in Practice

You have read the content warning at the front of this book. You have discussed Lines and Veils. You have explained the Grace. This section is about what those tools look like when the dice are rolling, the lamp is low, and somebody at your table has gone very quiet.

The Long Grace deals with suicide, contagious despair, cannibalism, kin-slaying, the desecration of the dead, and bodies and minds coming apart. Horror is how people have always looked at what frightens them from a safe distance. The distance is the point. Your job is to keep it.

### The Session Zero

Before the first session, sit down together without characters and talk. A good session zero for this game covers:

- **What the game is.** Read aloud the one-breath summary at the front of the book. Say plainly: this is a horror game without rescue, and characters will be changed, broken and lost.
- **What is in this book.** Go through the content list in the front matter. Ask, without pressure, whether anything on it is a Line (it will not appear at all) or a Veil (it may happen off-screen, and we cut to after).
- **How dark, and how graphic.** Agree on a dial. Some tables want the bowel sliding free of the officer's belly; some want the officer's wedding knot and nothing else. Both are the Long Grace.
- **The Grace.** Explain it. Practice it once, on something trivial, so that saying it in earnest later feels ordinary.
- **Unreliable narration.** If you want to use gaslighting scenes in which the Carver's descriptions may be false, ask now, by name. If anyone hesitates, do not.
- **What brings people here.** Ask each player what they hope to feel: dread, grief, grim laughter, a mystery, a fight against something too big. Plan for it.

[box: A Sample Lines and Veils Card]
Write the group's agreements on a card and leave it on the table where everyone can see it. A first card for this game might read:
- **Lines (never):** any sexual content involving anyone under 18; sexual violence on-screen; real-world slurs.
- **Veils (off-screen, cut to after):** any sexual content between adults; suicide and self-harm (aftermath only, never method); torture beyond a single sentence; harm to children beyond implication; incest and cursed bloodlines (implied only).
- **On the table (agreed):** body horror, gore, rot, cannibalism, the Kept, the Reaping, kin-slaying, madness and its contagion, worthless sacrifice.
- **Ask first:** pregnancy and birth horror (Cradlewrack); drowning (Brinehollow); starvation (the Fast).
Anyone may add to this card at any time, during play or between sessions, without giving a reason.
[/box]

### The Grace in Play

When someone says the Grace (*Lack keep away*, or a tap on the crossed-fork card), stop. Do not ask why. Do not sigh. Do not finish the sentence. Say "Of course," remove or change what was happening, and carry on, or call a break if the moment needs one. If the Grace was said about something central to the session, the session will survive it: cut to after, and describe the after through a different sense. If you are unsure what to change, ask the group, not the person who said it.

The Grace protects the Carver too. If a scene you planned starts to land on something in your own life, say it yourself. Nobody at the table should have to carry what they did not sign up for, and that includes you.

### Checking In

Watch the people at your table, not just their characters. If someone has gone quiet, check in at the break. If someone laughs too hard at the wrong moment, it may be how they are coping. If a scene you planned starts to land on something real in someone's life, stop it, and do not make them explain.

Some gentle habits help:

- **Take breaks.** Every ninety minutes or so, stand up, make tea, talk about anything else. Horror needs air.
- **Check in between scenes, not inside them.** A thumbs-up, thumbs-sideways, thumbs-down after a heavy scene takes three seconds and tells you everything.
- **Ask about the dial.** Midway through a campaign, ask whether the darkness is where people want it. Tables drift, and lives change.
- **Share the worst scenes.** Do not keep putting the same player's character through the same kind of horror unless that player has asked for it.

### When the Fiction Touches Something Real

Grief, illness, a loved one who has gone, a pregnancy, a hunger, a family that has come apart: the Long Grace is made of the things people really suffer, and sooner or later the fiction will touch someone at your table where they live. When it does, stop the scene, without fuss. Change it, or cut to after, or end the session early. Do not ask the person to explain. Afterward, if they want to talk, listen. If they do not, let it be. The game can wait for a week. People cannot always.

### Suicide and Despair at the Table

This book treats suicide and despair as tragedy: the Pull, the Finishers, the Called, the Bedded, the villagers whose names are no longer spoken. At your table:

- Never describe method. Describe the before as a pull and the after as a loss.
- Any ending for a Guest under the Despair rules requires the player's and the table's consent, out loud. If anyone hesitates, the companions reach them in time.
- Show the grief. The worst thing the Table does to the people left behind is make them stop saying a name. Let the players say it.
- If the subject is close to anyone at your table, veil it entirely. The Table has many other ways to take people.

### Clearing the Plates

At the end of a heavy session, take a few minutes before everyone goes home to clear the plates: step out of the fiction together. Talk about what landed, what was frightening in the good way, what was too much. Thank people for their courage in playing a hard game. Let anyone who needs to say something about their own week, their own life, their own day, say it. The Fatlands have a saying that nobody leaves a table hungry; the Carver's version is that nobody leaves the table carrying something alone.

### For the Carver

Running a game this dark costs something. Give yourself the care you give the players: ask for a lighter session when you need one, and remember you may say the Grace too. The Table is a world without rescue. Your table is not. Be the Carver who makes sure everyone gets fed, and then, when the plates are cleared, sit down and eat something yourself.
