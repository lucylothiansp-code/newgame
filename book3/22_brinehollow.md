# Brinehollow

[sigil: brinehollow]

> Call the names. Call them twice. If the wall answers in a voice you know, call them a third time, and listen for whether it knows you.
>> — instruction for the morning count, as taught to children on the Mile

The Count, as kept by Wenna Truss of Ropery Row, Lowmark, Mile Eleven. Written on the back of a chandler's bill and found folded inside her bail-bucket.
I call through the wall at first bell, same as every morning since I could stand. My mother taught me. You put your mouth to the plank where it's worn smooth, where the knot looks like an ear, and you call the names on the other side. Haddie. Bren. Little Os. Old Cobb. And they call back, one at a time, with the cough still in their voices, because nobody has bailed yet at first bell. That is how you know they are home. The wet in the throat. The answer.
This morning I called Haddie and Haddie answered. I called Bren and Bren answered. I called Little Os and he answered in that piping way, still half asleep, and I heard Haddie hush him. I called Old Cobb.
I want to set down exactly what happened next, because they will ask me at the Netwatch house and I will not be able to say it with my mouth.
Nothing answered. Not silence like an empty room. Silence like a held breath. The whole house on the other side of the plank went still, the way a pool goes still when something big turns over under it. And then Haddie began to make a sound I have heard eleven times in my life and never once from her.
I went round. The door was on the latch. Cobb's bed is the one by the west window, because he likes the light, because he has always liked the light, the old fool, and we all said it, we all said you shouldn't sleep facing that way at your age. The rope was still tied to the bedpost. Good tarred hemp from the Ropewalk, three turns and a hitch, done by Bren the night before. It was tied to the bedpost and it went across the blanket and over the edge of the bed and it stopped. The end was cut clean. Not frayed. Not chewed. Cut, the way you would cut it if you had a sharp knife and a steady hand and all the time in the world.
There was no knife in the room.
The floor was wet from the bed to the door, and from the door down the steps, and across the boards of the Row, and on to the pier rail, and over it, and on into the dark of the Uncovered where there is no water any more for nineteen miles. The wet went west. It does not dry fast on the Mile. It never has.
Haddie kept saying, I tied it, I tied it, I checked it, as if that was the thing that mattered, and Bren kept saying, I tied it, Mam, it was me, and Little Os stood in the doorway holding Cobb's bail-bucket in both arms, the old copper one with the dent, and he said, Grandda forgot his bucket.
He did. He will not need it now.
I want to say also that the bed was warm. The pillow still had the shape of him. On the pillow, where his mouth would have been, there was a little crust of white salt, and in the salt there was a print, like someone had kissed it. I do not know what that means. I did not tell Haddie.
I looked in his bucket, after. I am ashamed of it. You are not meant to look in a Called man's last bucket, it is like reading his letters. But Os was holding it out to me and it was heavy, and I looked. There was a finger's depth of his dusk water still in the bottom, from the night before, because nobody had tipped it. It was not green. It was not clear. It was blue, deep blue, the blue you see in a glass held up to a winter sky, and there was a weight to it, so the bucket swung slow in the boy's arms like there was more in it than there was. Haddie had not looked. Bren had not looked. I think the old man had looked every night for a month and said nothing, and tied his own knot, and lain down facing west because he liked the light.
They will put his name on the post. They will cut his rope into nine pieces and give one to each of us and one to the wall. We will set his bowl on Tablenight with the seawater in it and we will leave the west door unbarred, and every one of us will lie awake hoping he does not come in.
That is the worst of it, and I will write it because nobody says it. You pray for them to come home and you pray they don't. Because if Cobb comes walking up out of the dark one morning, heavy and gentle and dripping, with his own face and his own laugh and the name of every grandchild on his lips, Haddie will take him in. She will feed him. She will rope him to the same bed. And every morning I will put my mouth to the plank and call his name, and he will answer, in a voice just a little lower than it should be, and I will never again know who I am talking to.
Haddie. Bren. Little Os.
Tomorrow there will be three names.

[pull] Rope before bread. Bucket before rope. Name before bucket. And before your name, child, the cough. | — Mile Twelve skipping rhyme

Brinehollow is a land of chores. In Sallowreach the horror is that nothing ends; in the Fatlands it is that nothing is ever enough. In Brinehollow it is maintenance. Twice a day, every day, for six centuries, every soul on the western coast has knelt and coughed its god up into a bucket, and every night every soul has been tied down by someone who loves them. The horror lives in the routine, and in the morning, sometimes, the routine fails. This chapter is about the coast that eats the deep and is being eaten back by it: about a people whose chests are tide pools, a navy stranded on props in a dry harbor, a court of the drowned that has never been wrong, and a sea that is not leaving so much as moving in, a bucket at a time, to the only homes left to it.

## The Meal

> Haul and hold, and do not look.
>> — the Longslab hauling song

The western coast ate Dromm, the Fathom, god of the deep sea, of pressure, and of what is kept under. Every land's Gorging has its peculiar horror. Brinehollow's is that the god had to be brought to the table, and that it took four days to stop moving once it got there.

Dromm did not live on land. It lived beneath the whole grey reach of the western water, a presence more than a shape, and the coast knew it the way a body knows its own blood pressure: as a weight, a steadiness, the thing that kept the bottom of the sea where it belonged. Before the Lack, the fishers said that Dromm held the drowned down so they would not frighten their families by floating, and held the storms down so the boats came home, and held the cold water under the warm so the herring rose. It was a god of keeping things where they were put. Its priests were divers who went down on weighted ropes and came back with bleeding ears and with answers. They were called **Sounders**, and a Sounder's rank was measured by the depth she had touched and the blood she had brought back from it: a Sounder of the ninth knot had gone down nine fathoms past the point where a diver's eardrums burst, and could hear nothing on land but the god.

[pull] Hold us down, O Fathom, as you held the drowned, that we may not rise up and frighten those we love. | — the oldest line of the Admiralty hymn, older than the Gorging

In the ninth winter there were no herring. There were no answers either. The Sounders went down and found Dromm lying in the shallows off a long, flat beach called Longslab, closer to shore than any god had ever come, as though it had surfaced to wait. It was so large that nobody living could agree on its shape. Accounts written within a generation describe a back like a reef of black slate, plates of it, crusted with things that were not barnacles; a flank that rose and fell, very slowly, with a sound like surf heard through a wall; and eyes, or something like eyes, that stood open under the water, wider than doorways, the color of the sea a mile down. The Sounders who swam close enough to touch it came back without their hearing, and without their appetite, and three of them would not speak again except to say that it was cold, and that it had been listening to them for a long time.

The hauling took every vessel on the coast and every hand that could hold rope. Ten thousand hooks, by the count the Admiralty still teaches: shark hooks, gaff hooks, anchors beaten into barbs, meat hooks taken off the walls of empty smokehouses. They set them in the god from boats and from the shallows and from the beach itself, in long lines of starving men and women and children who leaned back on the hemp and sang the hauling songs, and the god came in a yard at a time. It did not fight. That is recorded here as everywhere. It came in the way a drowned man comes in, heavy and unresisting, with the water pouring out of it.

That was the first horror. The water did not stop. It ran out of Dromm in sheets, in torrents, from every gap between the plates, from the eyes, from places that opened along its sides like gills that had never been needed, and it ran for four days. It was colder than any water on the coast. It smelled of iron and of something older than salt. It came out under such pressure that it knocked haulers flat and blinded them, and the people at the front of the lines drowned standing up on dry shingle, their lungs filled from the spray. They did not fall at once. A hauler at the head of a line would take a mouthful of the god's breath and keep pulling, because the line was pulling and the song was going, and her face would go dark and swollen and her eyes would bulge and a pink froth would come out of her nose and the corners of her mouth, fine as soap, and she would go on leaning back on the rope with the others for a dozen heartbeats after she had drowned, held up by the hemp and the song, until the people behind her felt the line go slack in her hands. They were laid out in rows above the tideline, and the froth went on coming out of them for hours, the way it does from the drowned, and the children who were sent to wipe their faces could not keep up. The beach under the god became a lake, and then a marsh, and it has never dried since.

### The Hook-Brides

On the third day, the god stopped coming in.

The chronicles agree on the place, a bar of grey shingle a hundred yards out, where the bottom shelved, and on the hour, the dead middle of the night, when the tide turned. The great flank grounded on the bar and stuck. Ten thousand hooks held, and every line on the beach leaned back until the hemp sang and the hooks tore long white rents in the slate of the god's sides, and it did not come one yard further. It lay on the bar with the water pouring out of it, and the haulers sat down on the stones in the dark and wept, because they had been starving for nine winters and they could smell it.

The Sounders went out to it on the slack water and came back at dawn and said that the god was waiting for something. They had pressed their bleeding ears to its plates and listened, and they had heard it, very far down, asking. They said it was a god of keeping, and a keeping wants a keepsake. They said it would not come ashore to be eaten until each line had given it someone to hold.

There were seven villages on the hooks that the chronicles admit to. Each gave one. The Sounders asked for the young, the whole and the promised, and the villages gave what the Sounders asked for, which in a ninth winter was not difficult: the young were the ones still strong enough to walk out to the god, and the promised were the ones with the most to lose and the families who could least bear to watch them starve. They were seven betrothed men and women between eighteen and twenty-five, and the chronicles name them, which is the only kindness anyone did them: Tamsin of Slabside, Orry Weir, Annick Fell, Hob Sculpin, Dennet Marl, Lowdy Kettle and Bran-son-of-Bran of the far north shore. They were dressed as for their weddings, in what was left of the coast's linen. Their sweethearts tied them each to a hook-line with a lover's knot, at the wrist, the knot that is still tied at every wedding on the Mile. Then they walked out along the lines into the shallows, hand over hand on the hemp, into the spray, and the Sounders led them in under the lip of the plates where the gill-openings gaped, and they went in, one by one, into the god.

The coast sang them in. That is the verse that was struck out. The Admiralty's official history says it was struck from shame, and that it named them.

The god came off the bar at the next turn of the tide, and the lines took up the slack, and the singing began again, and by the fourth morning Dromm lay on Longslab, and the butchering began. That is the story the Sounders told, and the coast told, and Brinehollow believed for two hundred years.

In 214 A.G. a Purser's clerk named Abiah Steep, compiling tide-tables for the old harbor, worked the moon and the spring tides back to the year 0, out of idle curiosity, because the chronicles gave him the hours. He found that the god had grounded on the bar at the neap, the lowest and weakest tide of the month, and that it came off at the next spring tide, when the sea on Longslab rose a fathom and a half higher than it had stood the night before. The water had lifted it. It would have come off the bar on that tide whatever anyone did. The Sounders, whose whole art was the reckoning of depth and tide, could not have failed to know it.

And there was the second thing, which the chronicles of the Lung-lines had always recorded and nobody had put beside the first. When the founders went into the god's chest on the fourth night to eat the lungs raw in the dark, they found the seven. The Hook-Brides had gone in through the gills and made their way, or been carried, into the lungs, and drowned there in the god's deep water, and lain there in the silver chambers in their wedding linen for a day and a night. The founders were eating in the dark, by touch, with their knives and their hands, ravenous, nine winters starved, with the water up to their knees. The Vasht chronicle says, in a sentence later scored through but still legible, that *the lights were full of sweetmeats and Ondric ate of them all and blessed the god for its provision.* When the founders were carried out at dawn, half-drowned and laughing, nothing of the seven was ever found but one wrist-knot, still tied, in the hand of the Weir boy's sweetheart, who had followed the line in after him.

[bigquote] They gave the sea seven of their best to bring the god ashore, and the sea would have brought it anyway, and then they ate the seven too, in the dark, and called it grace. | — Abiah Steep, Purser's clerk, in the letter for which he was hanged in 215 A.G.

Steep was hanged from a yard for "publishing tables touching the Beaching." The tables were burned. The Admiralty has never acknowledged the Hook-Brides as anything but a voluntary, holy sacrifice that brought the god ashore, and on the anniversary of the Beaching the seven names are read aloud on the Headland with honor. Every Lung-line house carries, somewhere in its arms, a hook with a lover's knot on it. The Debt chapels say that the Lung-lines' deep blood is half god and half bride, and that this is why they bail so much: they are bailing seven drowned sweethearts, forever, out of the chest of the coast.

[box: Using the Hook-Brides]
The Hook-Brides are Brinehollow's worthless sacrifice: demanded by the god's own priests, given willingly by those who loved them, and useless, because the tide would have done it anyway. Use them as a lens for everything the coast does now. The anchoring ropes, the Netwatch, the Anchorites on their cliff and the Deepening on its shell road are all, in their way, arguing over whether anything a person gives the sea makes any difference. The answer the Hook-Brides suggest is no. The answer the Deepening gives is that the seven are the first of the Tenanted, and that they are still down there, waiting, in their linen. A PC who finds Steep's tables (a Dire 22 Eye + Lore roll in the Admiralty's sealed archive, or a lucky find in the Underpier's trade in drowned papers) holds something the Admiralty will still hang a person for.

On the fourth day, the god stopped moving. The water still ran, but more slowly, and the vast flank no longer rose. The butchering began at dusk, by lamplight and by bonfires of wreck-wood, and it was done with whale-spades, axes, and the long knives of the flensing yards. Survivors' accounts agree on the smell: like opening a tide pool that had been sealed for a thousand years. They agree on the sound: that when the first spade went in, every person on the beach felt their ears pop at once, and some of them never heard properly again. They agree that the flesh was white and dense and very cold, and that it was the best thing any of them had ever tasted, and that it tasted like being held.

The flensers stripped the plates first, prising them up with crowbars like the slates of a roof, and under every plate was a layer of blubber as clear as aspic and a hand deep, in which small silver things moved like fish in ice. Under the blubber, the meat. It came away in slabs that steamed in the cold air, not with heat but with cold, a white breath that rolled off it and down the beach and made the bonfires gutter. People ate it raw, on the stones, with both hands, and wept while they ate, and children who had not eaten in a week ate until they were sick and then ate again. Some of the old died of it, that first night, their starved bellies split by the weight of the god, and were laid beside the drowned haulers, and the gulls, which had come in from the whole coast in a cloud that blotted out the stars, did not touch them. The gulls ate only the god.

The families who reached the lungs first were the boldest and the strongest and the nearest, and they did not wait for fire. Dromm's lungs were caverns, chambers that a family could walk into, spongy and silver-grey and still full of the deep. The founders of the Lung-lines went inside the god with their knives and their bare hands and ate the lungs raw, from within, in the dark, with the water rising around their knees. The lung-flesh was soft as bread soaked in milk and it squeezed out water when it was bitten, cold water, sea-water, so that every mouthful was half a drowning, and the founders choked and swallowed and choked and did not stop. The chronicles of the Vasht family record, with what was meant as pride, that their founder Ondric Vasht ate until he could not breathe and went on eating anyway, and was carried out of the god's chest at dawn half-drowned and laughing and coughing up salt water, and that he coughed up salt water every dawn and dusk from that day until he died. He was the first to bail.

[pull] It tasted like being held. That's what my gran's gran said, and she'd only had a spoonful. Imagine what the Vashts tasted. Imagine eating that much of being held. | — a Debt priest of the Chapel Reach

The bones were never fully cleared. The Longslab middens are still there, under the marsh, and the god's skull was too large to move and too hard to break. It was left where it lay, and the sea covered it, and then the sea began, five centuries later, to leave. It is not on the Uncovered now. It was not taken. Nobody knows where it went. The Salt Assay surveyed the place in 548, when the water first drew back from it, and found a hollow in the seabed the size of a cathedral, smooth as the inside of a bowl, and in the bottom of the hollow a line of marks in the mud, deep and evenly spaced, leading west. The Assay recorded them as "tidal scour." The surveyor who measured them asked to be transferred inland and was refused.

[box: The Missing Line]
Every family of the coast keeps some fragment of the Longslab hauling songs, and every version has the same gap: a verse that has been struck out, sometimes scored through so hard the pen went through the page. In the oldest carved record of the Beaching, a frieze on the Admiralty's Sounding Hall, the hauling teams are shown in long lines, each line labeled for a village of the coast. There are eight lines of haulers. The eighth has been chiseled flat. The figures on its rope are gone, but the rope is still carved, and it runs, like all the others, into the god.
The Admiralty says the struck verse named the Hook-Brides. Seven lines gave seven brides. Nobody in the Admiralty has ever said, aloud, what the eighth line gave, or whether the eighth line was the one that asked.
GMs should let players find this and wonder. Brinehollow does not know what the eighth rope means and does not want to.
[/box]

Dromm was the god of holding things down. It is now held in them, and what it holds down in its new house is the house itself.

## The Rule of the Land

> A dry man drowns twice.
>> — Mile proverb

The sea is inside the people. Everything in Brinehollow follows from that sentence, and the country has had six hundred and forty-one years to learn how far it goes.

### The Tide in the Body

Every Brinehollower has a tide. Twice a day, near dawn and near dusk, the lungs fill with salt water from nowhere. It is not inhaled. It rises from inside, cold, through the bronchial tree, like water coming up through sand when you dig on a beach. It begins as a tightness and a taste. Within a few minutes the breath gurgles. Within a quarter hour a person who has not cleared it is drowning in their own chest.

It is worth being exact about what that feels like, because every Brinehollower knows it from the first week of life and none of them will describe it to a stranger. First the cold, a coin of it laid flat behind the breastbone. Then the taste, iron and weed, at the back of the tongue, as if you had bitten your cheek in the sea. Then the weight: the lungs going heavy, sagging in the ribs like full wineskins, so that the breath gets shorter from the bottom up, the way a cellar floods. The breath begins to bubble at the top of each draw, a small wet click, then a rattle, then a thick slow gargle like a drain. If the tide is not bailed, the water climbs the windpipe and the body fights it with everything it has: the throat clenches, the ribs heave, the face swells and darkens, the eyes water salt, and a fine pink foam begins to rise at the nostrils from the delicate places deep in the lung that are tearing. The drowning do not thrash for long. The cold reaches the heart and slows it, and many Brinehollowers who have come close describe the end as gentle, as a sinking, as the moment of falling asleep in a warm bath. That is the most frightening thing they say about it.

Clearing it is called **bailing**. It is done bent over a bucket, with the body tipped forward and the shoulders worked, in long racking coughs that bring the water up in gouts. A healthy adult bails a pint or two. Lung-lines nobles bail gallons. The water is clear, cold, faintly green, and alive in small ways: fine sand, a thread of weed, now and then a sand-flea that hops on the bucket's rim and is gone. It smells like the sea did when there was a sea at the end of every street. Children are taught to bail before they are taught to read, and a child who cannot yet bail on its own is held face-down over a mother's knee and patted on the back, twice a day, as she sings. Every lullaby on the coast is a bailing song, in the rhythm of a cough.

Bailing is done at dawn and dusk the way other peoples wash their faces, privately and without comment. Bail-water is poured into the gutters, which run west, always west, whatever the slope of the street. Engineers have sunk gutters on the Headland that run uphill, by spirit-level, for a hundred yards, and the water runs up them, west, without hurrying, and drops off the end of the Headland onto the Uncovered as though it had somewhere to be.

Those who cannot bail — the very old, the sick, the drunk, the injured, the unconscious — must be bailed by others, held head-down and struck between the shoulder blades until the water runs. Neglecting to bail a helpless person is murder in Brinehollow law, and it is the most common murder there is, because it requires only that one stay asleep. The Bailers' Wardens of the Mile keep a register of every such death, and the register has a column for "slept through" and a column for "chose to." The second column is never empty, and the Wardens fill it in with a care that is almost tender, because in most of those cases the one who chose to was the one who had done the bailing every dawn and dusk for twenty years, for a father who no longer knew them, and one morning simply lay with their eyes open in the dark and listened to the gargle rise in the next bed, and did not get up.

[box: What the Drowned-Ashore Look Like]
A Brinehollower who drowns in bed, unbailed, is called **land-drowned**, and the body is easy to know. The face is congested and dusky, the lips blue, the eyes bloodshot, half open and wet. A plume of fine white or rose-colored froth stands at the nose and mouth and goes on rising for hours after death, pushed up from the lungs by the weight of the water still inside them; mourners on the Mile wipe it away with a twist of linen and it comes back. The chest is heavy and dull when struck. Laid face-down, as the dead are laid, the body drains: water runs from the mouth and nose for a day and a night, clear and cold, and soaks the stones, and smells of the open sea. Surgeons who open a land-drowned chest find the lungs swollen to twice their weight, pale, rubbery and dripping, pitted like a sponge, and so full that they keep the shape of the ribs when lifted out. Sometimes there is sand in the smallest airways. Sometimes there is a living thing.
A Dread 1 check is needed to sit with the land-drowned. To be the one who heard the gargle and did not get up is Dread 3, and is never forgotten.

### What Comes Up

Everyone looks in the bucket. Nobody says they are looking. The bail-water is a newspaper, an omen, a confession and a medical record, and the coast reads it twice a day by the grey light at the window, tipping it toward the glass before tipping it into the gutter. Most mornings it holds only water, sand and a thread of weed. But the god is the god of what is kept under, and what is kept under comes up.

### The Pull and the Called

The tide pulls as well as fills. The water in the body wants to go home, and on certain nights it takes the body with it.

The Called rise in their sleep, or in a waking trance that looks like sleep, and walk west. They do not hurry. They do not respond to their names. They walk to the nearest shore, which in the present age may be miles away across the Uncovered, and then they walk into the sea and keep walking until the water closes over them. Nobody who has watched it says the Called look afraid. The Netwatch's records use a standard phrase, written in the logs thousands of times: **"Subject appeared expected."** As if someone were waiting for them and they did not want to be late.

The walk is quiet. The Called do not run, stumble or speak. Their feet are bare, because they do not stop for shoes, and by the time they cross the Raw Mile's splinters and the salt-crust of the Uncovered their soles are cut to ribbons, and they leave a trail of watery pink prints that the Netwatch can follow by lantern. Their eyes are open and wet. Their chests, those who have watched closely say, do not rise. They walk with their hands loose at their sides and their faces lifted a little, as people lift their faces to a warm wind, and if a hooker blocks their path they walk into him and keep walking, pressing, with a slow painless strength that has broken men's arms. Those who are dragged home fight in silence and then, at the Return Room's benches, wake, and when they wake they weep, not with relief, but the way a child weeps when it is woken from the best dream it ever had and told it was not real.

Calling nights are not random, though no one has fully mapped them. They cluster at the licked-clean moon, on pressure days, in the weeks after a death in the family, and on nights when the indoor tide runs unusually high. They cluster in households. They cluster in grief. A widower whose wife was Called in Lack has, by the Netwatch's reckoning, one chance in four of following her before Grace.

The defence is **anchoring**. People sleep roped, chained, or weighted to the bed. Sweethearts tie each other's knots. In the poorer streets of the Mile a whole family may sleep on one floor, lashed in a line to a single iron ring set into the joists, like a boat's crew in a storm. And still, every year, ropes are found cut. Sometimes there is a knife on the floor that the sleeper has kept hidden for weeks without knowing they were hiding it. Sometimes there is no knife at all. And sometimes, which is the thing nobody says aloud, there is a knife in another hand: a spouse who could not bear the pleading any longer, a child who heard a parent crying to be let go and loved them too much to refuse. A rope cut in the night is the country's great domestic tragedy, and the question of who cut it is the question that breaks families.

The pleading is the part outsiders never believe. A Brinehollower at the Appetite or beyond, roped to the bed on a Calling night, does not walk. They lie in the dark and ask. They ask the person tied beside them, in their own voice, gently, reasonably, the way you would ask someone to open a window in a hot room. *Let me go. Just tonight. I'll come back. It's so close. Can't you hear it? Please. Please, love. I'm so tired of being held.* They ask for hours. They ask every night for a season. They weep, and plead, and bargain, and then they are quiet, and in the quiet the one who is lying awake beside them can hear, under the floor, the indoor tide coming in. The Chapel Reach hires out **sitters**, at a lack a night, to sit by the beds of the pleading so the family can sleep in another room. The sitters are mostly old women, and many of them are deaf, and that is why they are hired.

[pull] I cut my father's rope. He'd been asking for six years. The Bench said he cut it himself. I thanked them. I'd thank them again. | — anonymous, read aloud at a Wreckwives' table

### Cask Lane: A Calling in Seventeen Names

The Netwatch logs record Callings as numbers. The families record them as names. What follows is assembled from the papers of one street, Cask Lane, a short alley of coopers' houses off Mile Ten, over forty days of Lack in 640. [box: Running a Calling Cluster]
A cluster is the coast's commonest catastrophe and the best way to show the Calling as contagion rather than accident. It begins with one loss, ideally of someone young and much loved. Thereafter, each household connected to the Called by blood, love or a shared wall makes a Calling roll on each Calling night at +2 difficulty for every person already lost from the street, to a maximum of +6. Grief, not proximity, is what spreads it: a PC who mourns openly for one of the lost counts as connected. Do not let the cluster resolve in a single night. Let the party arrive on Day 6, or Day 14, and see what is coming: the street tying itself together tighter each night, the knots getting more elaborate, the families beginning to sleep in one room, then one bed, then one knot. A cluster can be broken: by moving the survivors east (to Weirholm, to Saltwick), by an Anchorite's vigil, by the Netwatch posted in every house, or by giving the grieving some other thing to hold. It cannot be broken by telling anyone it is not their fault.
[/box]

### The Tenanted

Most of the Called are never seen again. Some come back.

They come weeks later, usually at dawn, walking up out of the surf heavier than they left: denser, slower, their footprints sinking deep into wet sand. They are calm and courteous. They know the way home. They have the same face, the same scars, the same memories down to the names of childhood dogs and the location of the spare key. They greet their families with real warmth and apologize for the worry. They ask for their bail-bucket, and bail a great deal, and then they ask whether there is any supper.

They are the **Tenanted**, and someone else is looking out through their eyes.

It is not always easy to say how one knows. A Tenanted man still laughs at his brother's jokes, but a beat late, as if the joke had to come down a long way to reach him. A Tenanted mother still sings the old songs to her children, but her voice has a second voice beneath it, lower, that sometimes finishes the line first. The Tenanted do not lie, cannot be bribed, do not lose their temper, and are, by every measure anyone has devised, kinder and fairer than they were before. Their families are often happier. That is the horror the Wreckwives exist to share.

They come back changed in the body too, in ways that are hard to see and impossible to forget once seen. Their skin is the grey of a wet slate roof and cool to the touch at every hour; a Tenanted woman's hand on your cheek is like a stone taken out of a stream. Their weight is wrong. A Tenanted child of eight sinks a stair-tread like a grown man; a Tenanted grandmother needs a bed with iron legs. They bail half a bucket at every tide, slowly, without strain, the water pouring out of them like a jug being emptied, and between bailings they sometimes sit for an hour without breathing and without seeming to notice. When they sleep they sleep face-up with their eyes a little open and a film of water standing in them. They do not dream, or do not say so. They smell, always, faintly, of the cold under a pier.

Whether the person who walked into the sea is still in there somewhere, folded small under the weight of the thing that came back with them, is Brinehollow's oldest unanswerable question. It is rude to ask a returned person where they went. It is ruder to ask whether they are still there.

[pull] My husband came home kinder. My children came home strangers. I don't know which of them I'm feeding. | — a Wreckwife at the Middle Mile table

### The Second Face

Every coast that has a word for a returned person soon has a word for the fear of one. In Brinehollow it is **the Second Face**: the conviction that someone you love has been Tenanted, though they have never been Called. It is the coast's most common madness, and its most contagious, and it is getting worse.

It begins small. A wife notices that her husband laughed a beat late at supper. A son notices that his mother's voice has dropped, or seems to have dropped, when she calls the count. A brother notices that his sister has stopped shouting at the children. After that every kindness is evidence. The Tenanted are kind, everyone knows it; so a gentle word is a sign, a patient silence is a sign, a meal set out without being asked is a sign. The sufferer begins to test: holding mirrors to the mouth at dawn, listening at the bed at night for the second voice, asking questions only the real person could answer, and finding, always, that the answers are right, which proves nothing, because the Tenanted remember everything. The Wreckwives have a saying for it, which they say to new Listeners at their first table: *You can't catch them out by what they know. You'll only catch yourself.*

The Second Face spreads along walls. When one member of a household is convinced that another is Tenanted, the others begin to look too, and to see, and within a season a whole family may be sleeping in shifts so that someone is always watching the one they have decided is not theirs. The accused often begins to doubt themselves. There are cases in the Wreckwives' ledgers of men and women who came, weeping, to a Keeper of Names to ask whether they had been Called in their sleep one night and come home without noticing, because their families looked at them as if they had, and they could not prove they had not. A few have gone to the Rail to find out. The ledgers record what the Netwatch recovered, and what it did not.

[box: The Second Face in Play]
The Second Face is a form of the core Derangement **The Impostors**, and it is the coast's characteristic way of Breaking. A Brinehollower PC who Breaks after seeing a loved one Called, Tenanted or seriously changed by the Regrowth should usually take it, choosing as the impostor someone who has never been near the sea. Witnesses to such a Break who fail their Dread 2 check share the delusion about the same person, exactly as the contagion rules describe, and this is how whole households turn on one member. Play it straight: never confirm or deny. Make the accused person kind, as a Tenanted would be, because the frightened are always being kind to the person they have turned on, trying to win them back. The GM should decide privately whether the person is in fact Tenanted, and should be willing to decide that they are not.
[/box]

### The Matter of Wat Teague

The Second Face kills. Most often it kills the accused, slowly, by the loneliness of being watched by everyone they love. Sometimes it kills faster. The following is kept by the Sounding Clerks of the Drowned Bench as a model hearing, and copied out by law students on the Headland, though not for the reason the Clerks intend.

[fiction]
From the record of the Sounding Hall, the matter of Wat Teague, cooper, of Mile Eleven, accused of the murder of his father Ezra Teague, retired net-maker. Heard before Justices Oake, Brannagh and Sole, at the turn of the morning tide, Carving 638. The accused stood in the pool. Sounding Clerk Ives at the gauge.
CLERK: The water stands at three and one quarter inches. The accused will state his name and mile.
TEAGUE: Wat Teague. Eleven. You know it. You all know it.
CLERK: The Bench has the indictment. Ezra Teague, aged seventy-three, found in the cellar of the house at Mile Eleven at the dusk tide on the ninth of Carving, dead of blows to the head with a cooper's adze, the indoor tide standing at the knee. The accused was found sitting on the stair above, wet to the shoulder. Does the accused deny it?
TEAGUE: I don't deny the adze. I deny it was my father.
CLERK: The Bench will hear him.
TEAGUE: My father went in Lack. Two years gone. He went in the night in Lack of 636 and the rope was cut and I'd tied it, I'd tied that knot, and in Grace he came back up the beach at Harbor End. Everyone saw it. He came back Tenanted and we took him in, my wife wanted to, and he was good. He was so good. He never was. He was a hard old man with his belt, my father, and the thing that came back was soft and listened and said he was proud of me. Proud of me. He never in his life. And he knew everything. He knew where the belt was hung. He knew the name of my brother that died. He knew everything my father knew, and he was kind about all of it, and I couldn't stand it. I watched him. Every night. My wife said I'd gone strange. She said Wat, he's your father, look at him, he's your da, and I'd look and he'd look back with those black eyes, so kind, and I knew. I knew he'd eaten my father, down there, and come back wearing him, and was sitting at my table being proud of me in my father's mouth.
CLERK: The water stands at three and one quarter inches.
TEAGUE: So I took him down the cellar at the dusk tide, when the water comes in. I thought, he's a thing of the sea, he breathes it, but if I held him under the true sea that's under the house maybe it'd come out of him. Maybe it'd let my father go. I held him under. He let me. He just lay there in the water at the bottom of the cellar stair, on his back, with his eyes open, looking up at me through it, and he didn't fight, and he didn't drown, because they don't, and after a long while I took my hands off him and he sat up and the water ran off him and he said, Wat. In the low voice. And then he said it again in the other voice. In my father's voice. The real one. The hard one. Wat, he said, for God's sake, son, do it right, then. And I had the adze in my belt. That's all. That's how. He said do it right.
CLERK: The water stands at three and one quarter inches. It has not moved.
TEAGUE: I know it hasn't. I'm not lying. That's what I'm telling you. It was my father. At the end. It was him under there all the time. It was him asking.
JUSTICE OAKE (in two voices): The Bench has heard. The Bench finds that Ezra Teague was present in the body at the time of his death, as the accused has truthfully said. The Bench finds that he asked. The Bench finds the accused guilty of the murder of his father, and sentences him to be loosed for one season, and directs that his anchoring rope be cut by the Netwatch at dusk on the first night of his sentence, by a hooker, in the presence of his wife, so that it shall not be cut by him.
TEAGUE: Which one of you asked? Which voice? Justice, please. Which one of him asked me?
CLERK: The water rose an inch and a half during the accused's last question. It is so recorded.
Note in a later hand: Wat Teague was Called on the eleventh night of his loosing. He has not returned. His wife remarried in Plenty of 640.
[/fiction]

Law students copy the Teague record because it is the clearest statement of the Bench's doctrine: that a Tenanted person is a person in law, and that killing one is murder. The Wreckwives read it differently. They read it for the last exchange, and for the Clerk's note about the water, and they ask one another, at their tables, the question Teague asked: which voice is the one that wants to be let go? The Bench has never answered it. The ledger of the Wreckwives of the Middle Mile records, in the years since, nine more cases of kin-slaying by a family that had decided its returned member was an impostor, and four of a family that had decided its *un*-returned member was one. In three of those thirteen cases, the dead had asked.

### The Sea Is Moving House

Meanwhile, the ocean itself is leaving. Since 512 A.G. the waterline has dropped every year, slowly at first, then by furlongs, then by miles. Harbors stand dry. Fishing fleets sit on mud. The great port of Lowmark has chased the water nineteen miles out across the dry seabed on a road of piers, and is still chasing. The sea is not evaporating. The Salt Assay has proved this, with buckets and with arithmetic, and has been forbidden to say where it is going instead.

The tide is also in the houses. In every cellar in Brinehollow, however far inland, water rises twice a day and drains away again, and leaves kelp on the stairs. A farmhouse forty miles from the coast will have a wet cellar step, a ring of salt on its walls, and once in a while a crab in the coal-bin. The country's buildings have adapted: cellars are tiled, drains run west, and anything of value is kept above the high-water mark, which is painted on the wall of every cellar in red and repainted every year a little higher.

[pull] Red line, red line, higher every Grace; when it reaches the kitchen, we'll move to another place. When it reaches the bedroom, we'll lie in the wet. When it reaches the attic, we'll not move yet. | — a Weirholm children's counting song

[box: Running the Calling]
On a Calling night, any Brinehollower (or anyone with brine in their blood; see Dry Jonah and the Vigil's rouse) who sleeps unanchored, or whose anchoring fails, must make a **Caul + Resolve** roll. The difficulty is Routine (10) at Regrowth 0–3, Hard (14) at 4–6, Grim (18) at 7–9 and Dire (22) at the Brink. A licked-clean moon, a recent Called death in the family, or sleeping facing west each add +2 to the difficulty. Success: the character wakes at the door or the window, cold and wet to the knees, with the taste of the sea in their mouth. Failure: they walk. Companions may try to wake or hold them (an opposed GUT + Labor roll against the Called character's LUNG + Athletics; the Called do not feel pain and do not tire).
Treat a PC's Calling as a dramatic scene, never a mechanical loss. Give the other players time and agency to stop it. A character who reaches the sea is gone for 2d6 weeks; the GM decides, with the player, whether they return Tenanted (the character becomes an NPC, or continues at the Brink under the rules for Playing a Brinehollower), or not at all.
A roped character who is not Called may still be asked. Anyone sleeping beside a pleading Brinehollower at the Appetite or beyond must make a Dread 2 check each Calling night; on a Lack, they wake with a knife in their hand and the rope in the other, and no memory of getting up, and must decide.
Above all, play the Calling as grief. The horror is not the walking. It is the empty bed, the warm pillow, the rope end, and the people at the table who have to keep setting a place.
[/box]

[box: Salt Manners]
It is rude to mention how someone sounds when they breathe. Brinehollowers gurgle, rattle, and whistle in the hours before bailing, and politeness requires that everyone pretend not to hear.
It is rude to ask a returned person where they went.
It is rude to stand between someone and a west-facing window.
It is rude to refuse a bail-bucket offered to an outsider. Hold it, say "Keep you dry," and hand it back.
It is rude to look into another person's bucket, and ruder to be seen doing it.
It is rude to say the name of a Called person after dusk, in case they hear it and think they are wanted.
It is very rude, and in some towns actionable, to tie your own knot loosely.
[/box]

## The Regrowth in Brinehollow

> The deep doesn't hurry. It doesn't have to. Everything comes to it in the end.
>> — Hask Tarn, pilot

Dromm is growing back, and what it grows back into is depth. The Regrowth here is a slow drowning that never finishes, a body learning, against every instinct it was born with, to live under a weight it cannot see.

### The Taste

Salt on the lips at waking: a fine white crust at the corners of the mouth, as if the sleeper had spent the night on a beach in the wind. Ears that pop indoors, especially in cellars, especially at the turn of the tide. A Brinehollower at the Taste wakes with the sheets damp in the shape of their body. Their voice sounds faintly wet to strangers. They find themselves telling the time by the tightness in their chest rather than by the bell, and they are always right. Children at the Taste hold their breath in play for longer than any child should, and laugh about it when they come up, and their mothers do not laugh.

### The Appetite

The pull. A person at the Appetite stands at windows that face west and does not know how long they have been there. They put their hands flat on the glass. They sleepwalk to the nearest water, and anything will do: a well, a rain barrel, a horse trough, a washbasin. Families find them kneeling on the floor in the dark with their faces in the basin, not drowning, simply resting there, breathing it, perfectly calm. They crave cold food and salt. They eat raw shellfish from the Uncovered middens, which are poison to anyone else, and are not poisoned. They lose interest in warmth. They stop noticing that they have not bailed, and their families start doing it for them.

The lungs begin to keep some of the water. A person at the Appetite bails less than they fill, and the remainder sloshes: they can be heard, when they turn over in bed, a soft liquid shift in the chest like a half-full cask rolled on a cart. They begin to prefer it. The first full breath of air after bailing makes them dizzy and faintly sick, and they find themselves, without deciding to, breathing shallowly, keeping a little of the cold inside, the way a child keeps a sweet in its cheek.

The bail-water changes too. Tolly Brack the Bailer Boy reads it: clear for the healthy, faintly green at the Appetite, and then, for those close to Calling, a deep sea-blue with a weight to it, like water pulled from a hundred fathoms. Some households keep the dawn bail-water in a glass jar on the sill so they can check its color by daylight. Most would rather not know.

### The Course

The body adapts to depth. The skin goes grey, then the grey of wet slate, smooth and cool and thickened, with a faint pebbling along the spine and the backs of the hands. The eyes widen. The pupils swell until there is almost no iris, black and wet and reflective, and in the dark they shine green like a cat's. The chest stops needing to rise. A person in the Course can sit for an hour without breathing visibly, and the stillness of their ribs is the thing that unsettles visitors most, more than the eyes.

Inside, the change is worse. Dr. Isolde Reave has opened Course chests on her table and recorded what she found: lungs grown spongy, silver, cavernous, packed with chambers like the inside of a sea-cucumber; ribs thickened and drawn inward, as though by enormous pressure; a heart small, slow, and dense as a stone. The lungs hold water now as their natural state. Air is the intrusion. A person in the Course is uncomfortable in open air, as a land-dweller would be under water: they feel thin, exposed, dizzy, light, as if they might float away. They sleep in a cistern, weighted, with only the face above the surface, or, more and more often, with no part of themselves above it at all.

[fiction]
From the case-book of Dr. Isolde Reave, Headland. Subject 31. Female, about 50, Lashings, cannery. Course, advanced. Land-drowned in Carving, in air, having been carried from her cistern to a dry bed by her son during a fever, against her expressed wish.
External: skin slate-grey, dense, cold; pebbling over the spine to the sacrum, raised like the plates of a skate. Nails thick, yellow, ridged. Scalp nearly bare. Eyes fixed wide, pupils full; the cornea has a faint nacre, like the inside of a shell.

The lungs fill the cavity entirely. Silver, not pink. Weight, both lungs together: twenty-two pounds, which is to say a small child. On section they are not tissue in the ordinary sense but a honeycomb of chambers, each a thumb's width across, lined with fine fronds that moved in the water for some minutes after death, all one way, as weed moves in a current. The water in them was cold, clear and under pressure: when I made my first cut it jetted to the ceiling, and my apprentice was soaked and could not stop coughing for an hour, and has coughed since.

[/fiction]

The voice drops. It gains an undertone, like a second speaker standing just behind the first. Ears bleed on pressure days. Fingernails thicken and yellow. Hair falls out in the bath in long dark ropes that drift on the water like weed. The joints ache in air as if under a great weight, and the sufferer begins to move with the slow, careful, gliding economy of a diver walking on the bottom. Course-sufferers who go too long out of the water dry, and the drying is terrible to watch: the slate skin cracks along the pebbled lines and weeps clear fluid, the lips split, the eyes cloud like a fish on a slab, and the sufferer lies on the floor in a stupor, breathing in short shallow gasps that whistle, drowning in the thin air the way a beached thing drowns. Put them back in the cistern and within an hour they are well, and grateful, and a little further down.

### The Brink

At the Brink the person has heard the invitation in full. It is no longer a pull. It is a voice that knows their name, that has always known it, that is patient and kind and very, very old. The body at the Brink is heavy beyond its size: floors creak under a slight woman, beds break, horses refuse to carry them. They sink in mud to the knee. They are cold to the touch, so cold that kisses leave a white mark. Their tears are salt enough to sting cuts. Their sweat leaves rime. They hear the sea in every silence, not as memory but as direction, as a compass hears north.

Most of the Called at the Brink go, and most of those who come back come back Tenanted. A Tenanted person stands at the Brink permanently: something sits in them, and something of them may still be there underneath.

### The Seating

The Tenant takes the whole house. What walks around afterward is a piece of Dromm, patient, pressing outward against the skin. The fully Seated do not look very different from the Tenanted, at first. Then they stop bothering with the smaller courtesies: they no longer blink, or answer to their name, or eat. Their skin bows outward between the ribs, as if under pressure from within, and their weight becomes impossible. They are not violent. They are simply present, the way a deep place is present, and the air around them thickens until lamps gutter and ears ring. The Admiralty keeps them in the lowest cisterns of Fathomhouse and records them as **"retired to depth"**. Nobody is sure any longer how many there are, or what they are waiting for.

The pressing does not stop. A Seated body is a vessel with more inside it than it was built to hold, and over months the skin of the chest and belly stretches tight and glossy as a drumhead, grey shading to translucent, so that by lantern-light the shapes of the ribs show through it, and between them, faintly, something dark that moves slowly, like a fish seen through ice. The fingers swell and split at the nails. The eyes bulge and do not close. Ears and nose leak a constant thread of cold clear water. Tank-tenders who have worked the lower stairs say the retired-to-depth make a sound, very low, below hearing almost, that you feel in the teeth and in the fillings of your jaw: a creak, like a hull at great depth, as if the body were a ship and the sea were trying to come in, or out.

[box: The Tenant Question]
The bible's central uncertainty in Brinehollow is whether the Tenanted are still the people they were. The GM should decide privately, and is encouraged to decide differently for different Tenanted. Some are entirely Dromm wearing a stolen memory. Some are a person and a piece of god sharing a house, each aware of the other. Some may be the original person, returned and unchanged, with every neighbor convinced otherwise (see Esk Penhallow). Players should never be certain. Seeing a loved one return Tenanted is a Dread 3 horror. Seeing one return and being unable to tell is worse, and should be played across sessions, not resolved in one.
[/box]

### The Lowering

What becomes of the retired-to-depth is the House of Vasht's oldest secret and the Admiralty's most carefully kept ceremony. The cisterns of Fathomhouse are not infinite. The Dowager Below is hungry. And a Seated body left too long in the lower cisterns grows heavier every month, until the stone floors crack beneath it, and the Headland's engineers have measured the settling of the whole palace and do not like the figures.

So once in each season, at the lowest tide, the household performs the **Lowering**. It is a ritual killing, though no one present would call it that: a liturgy of the Debt doctrine, said by the Chaplain of Fathomhouse, in which one of the retired-to-depth is given back. The order of service survives because the chaplains, being Admiralty men, write everything down.

[fiction]
THE ORDER FOR RETIREMENT TO DEPTH. As used in the Chapel Below, Fathomhouse, by the Chaplain and four Tenders, at the slack water of the lowest tide. Not to be copied.
The Tenders, in rubber aprons and with wax in their ears, shall bring the Retired from the third cistern to the head of the fifth stair upon the iron cradle, with the chains made fast at wrist, ankle and throat. The cradle shall be lowered by the windlass, slowly. If the Retired speaks, the Tenders shall not answer.
The Chaplain shall stand at the head of the stair, facing west, and say: We took you from your water. We are your water now.
The Tenders shall answer: Hold us down.
The Chaplain shall read the Retired's name and mile from the tide-post roll, and the date of their Calling and of their return. Then he shall say: You came back to us and we have kept you. Now you are too heavy for our keeping. Go down, and be held.
Here the Tenders shall open the grating of the fifth stair. The Chaplain shall ring the small bell three times. When the water below the grating begins to rise, which it will, the Tenders shall pay out the cradle chain until it is taut, and make it fast, and stand back.
The Chaplain shall not look down the stair. The Chaplain shall say the Dusk Hymn, all verses, and shall not hurry, until the chain goes slack.
If the chain does not go slack before the hymn is ended, the Chaplain shall begin the hymn again.
When the chain is slack, the Tenders shall haul up the cradle. The cradle shall be empty. If the cradle is not empty, the Tenders shall not describe what remains on it to the Chaplain, and shall commit it to the drains, and the Chaplain shall record the Retirement as complete.
The cradle's chains shall be scrubbed with sand and salt, and the Tenders shall be given gin.
[/fiction]

Tenders who have worked the Lowering describe it in fragments, and only when drunk. The grating opening on a blackness that breathes. The cold coming up the stair in a wave that frosts the iron. The Retired, who has not moved in a year, turning its head on the cradle to look down, calmly, with interest, as a traveler looks at a harbor he is coming into. A hand coming up out of the black water, grey, longer than the cradle, and closing round it with great care, the way a mother takes a sleeping child from someone else's arms. The chain going taut and humming. Then the sound, which every Tender mentions and none can describe: not a scream, the Retired never scream, but a long low creak and then a series of short flat cracks, like a cask being stove in, one stave at a time, very slowly, under a great weight. The hymn goes on through all of it. It has nine verses. The Tenders say the chaplains have learned to sing it slower over the years.

The retired-to-depth are the coast's Called who came home. Their families were told, at the Fathomhouse gate, that they had been taken into the Admiralty's care with honor. The tide-posts carry their names twice: once for the Calling, and once, in smaller letters, for the retirement. Nobody on the Mile has ever asked to visit.

## Land and Weather

> Salt in the rain, salt in the bread, salt in the kiss, and salt in the bed.
>> — Saltwick wedding toast

A long grey coast of shingle, salt marsh, and cliff, three hundred miles from the Cradlewrack border in the north to the Fatlands marches in the south, under a sky the color of a gutted herring. Beyond the old shoreline lies the **Uncovered**: a newly exposed seabed that stretches further west every year. It is not flat. It is a country of ravines and shelves and drowned hills, of weed forests gone to brown leather, of wrecks standing upright on their keels as though still sailing, and of things with too many ribs.

It rains salt. Not a little salt: rain in Brinehollow tastes like a tear and dries on window glass in white lace. Iron rusts in a season; mail shirts must be oiled nightly; the eyes of those who work outdoors are permanently red-rimmed and the skin of their hands cracked white along every crease. Fog comes in from a sea that is now miles away, rolling across the Uncovered in walls, and it smells of tide pools and rot. Gulls nest inland, on church roofs, in chimneys, and they are thin and angry and pick at the eyes of sleeping dogs.

On **pressure days** the air thickens until ears bleed. These come without warning, perhaps a dozen times a year: a sudden heaviness, a hush, birds dropping out of the air to sit stunned on the ground, windows bowing inward with a creak and sometimes bursting. Candle flames stand flat and blue. Everyone in the street touches their ears and comes away with blood on their fingers, and the old men say "the Fathom's turning over." Children have died on pressure days, crushed in their cots by nothing anyone could see. Callings triple on the night after one.

And there is the **indoor tide**. In every cellar in the country, however far inland, water rises twice a day and drains away again, leaving kelp on the stairs. The tide is cold, clear, and comes up from the floor, not the walls. It brings small gifts: shells, sea-glass, a child's shoe, a ring. It has been known to bring back things the Called were wearing when they went.

[pull] The cellar gave me back his spectacles on the Tuesday and his teeth on the Thursday. I'm dreading Saturday. | — overheard at the Bucket of the Unbailed, Mile Nine

### Places of Brinehollow

#### Lowmark

The capital, a nineteen-mile pier-city built out across the dry seabed, section by section, after the sea. Old Lowmark on the original shore, now called the Headland, holds the Admiralty, the Sounding Hall, Fathomhouse, and the tide-post. From it the Mile runs west on tarred pilings sixty feet high: a wooden road lined with houses, chandlers, chapels, taverns and bail-houses, all creaking, all leaning a little westward. Mile One is rich, Mile Seven is Netwatch barracks, Miles Twelve to Sixteen are slum, roped and chained and stinking of tar and bail-water. The Nineteenth ends in a working harbor where a few licensed boats go out and come back with catches nobody likes to describe. Sixty feet below, the old seabed is dark and wet and full of what the city drops. People live down there, too: the Underpier, where the Pickers sift the Mile's refuse, a child says the name of every Called neighbor from beneath their house at the next tide, and the Tenanted whose families would not have them back hum together in the dark between Miles Ten and Eleven.

#### Longslab

The beach of the Beaching. Permanently wet, though no sea has touched it in a century. Water seeps up through the shingle as if the beach were a sponge someone is always pressing. Nothing grows there, and gulls will not land on it. At the tide-turns the wet ground breathes: a slow sigh across two miles of stones, and a smell of the deep. Pilgrims of the Deepening come to lie face-down on it and listen. The Longslab middens beneath are full of bones too large to be any animal's. The village of Slabside above it lays every one of its children face-down on the stones for an hour once in their lives, and writes down what they hear. For four hundred years they heard the sea. For the last eleven, every child has said they heard someone counting.

#### The Uncovered

The exposed seabed, salvage country, lawless and strange, with a new mile of it every year. Near the old shore it is cracked white salt littered with six centuries of wreckage, skeletons still in the rigging. Further out it turns to mud flats, leather-weed jungle, and drowned valleys where dead reefs stand up like ruined cathedrals, blue with rot. Things live there that came up as the water fell and did not die properly: cart-sized crabs, slow and soft-shelled; ribbed things that drag themselves on fins across the mud at night; fish that breathe air, badly, with a sound like a sick man snoring. And there are older things, uncovered for the first time since before the Gorging: carved stones, paved roads running west into the deep, and at least one table.

### The Rotting Wonders

The Uncovered is the largest graveyard on the Table, and it is still being dug. Every year the falling sea lays bare another mile of things that were never meant to see air, and the air gets to work on them. The salt slows the rot and the sun speeds it, and the result is a country of slow, glistening, stinking marvels that salvagers speak of with the same mixture of awe and nausea that Fatlanders reserve for a really good feast.

**The Leather Forest.** Ten miles out, the great kelp beds of the old sea floor stand where they grew, sixty and eighty feet tall, held upright by the salt that stiffened them as the water left. They have turned the brown of old saddles and the texture of wet hide, and they sweat a slime that drips constantly, so that walking through them is like walking through rain in a tannery. They creak in the wind. At their roots the mud is thick with fish that were trapped when the water drained and have been rotting, very slowly, for years, in drifts knee-deep, their flesh gone to a translucent jelly that holds the shape of their bodies and the bones inside them like a jellied pie. The smell is a wall. Salvagers tie salted rags over their faces and still vomit.

**The Whale Cathedrals.** In the drowned valleys lie the whales that died when the water fell around them, too far from the deep to swim out: dozens, some the size of a Mile house. The flesh does not rot quickly in the salt; it slumps. Over years it runs off the great arched ribs like candle-wax off a candlestick, in folds and curtains, grey and pink and green, and pools on the mud in shining lakes of fat that never set, and the ribs stand up out of it like the vaults of a church. Crabs live in them by the thousand. On hot days the fat-lakes bubble and give off a gas that makes men giddy, and then sleepy, and then, if they sit down, dead. Salvagers render the fat for lamp oil by night. The lamps of the Lashings burn with a greasy green flame and smell faintly of the deep.

**The Gasping Fields.** Wide flats where the air-breathing fish come up out of the pools at dusk and lie on the mud in their thousands, gulping, their gills flaring scarlet, their flanks heaving. The noise is a vast soft snoring that carries for miles. They do not die. They lie there all night, and in the morning crawl back to the pools on their fins. Salvagers eat them, and say they taste of nothing, and that a man who eats too many begins, at night, to gasp.

**The Blue Reefs.** Whole drowned hills of coral, dead, rotting to a deep cobalt slime that drips from the branches in long strings and glows faintly at night. The light is cold, and attracts the ribbed things.

**The Upright Ships.** Wrecks of every age stand on the mud on their keels, as though still sailing. In many of the newest, the crews are still aboard, dried by salt into leather and kept at their posts: helmsmen with their hands on the wheel, lookouts in the crow's-nest with their faces turned west, cooks at their stoves. The Wreck Orchard holds two hundred of them. Salvagers do not take anything from a ship with its crew still aboard. The ones who did, the stories go, woke at the dusk tide to find the crew had come aboard their own boat to take it back.

**The Sounding Pits.** Here and there on the Uncovered the mud gives way to a hole: round, clean-sided, a few yards across, filled to the brim with black water that does not drain when the rest of the seabed does. Hask Tarn calls them the places where he lost the bottom. Leads dropped into them run out of line. On still nights the water in them rises and falls, very slowly, like the chest of a sleeper, and if you lie at the edge you can hear, far down, a sound like breathing.

**The Old Road.** Paved, straight, made of fitted blocks of black stone that no quarry on the coast has ever cut, a road runs west from a point on the Uncovered eleven miles out, down the long slope toward the deep. It is older than the Gorging. Its kerbs are carved with figures seated at tables, in rows. At every hundredth block there is a carved bowl in the paving, and in every bowl the salvagers who have walked it say they have found water, fresh and cold, which should not be there. Nobody has followed the road to its end.

### More Places

#### The Hanging Abbey

The Anchorites' cliff, forty miles north of Lowmark: four hundred feet of black basalt above a field of dry white boulders that was once the sea. A thousand living monks hang on it, chained by iron collars to rings set in the rock, each on a ledge or in a scooped cell, most open to the salt rain. Food comes down in baskets; waste falls. At dawn and dusk the whole cliff coughs at once, and water runs down the rock in sheets. At night they pray aloud, or scream, and from below it is impossible to tell which. The village of Basketfoot, at the cliff's base, hauls the baskets and gathers what falls, and buries it face-down among the boulders.

#### The Trench

The place far out where the water is still deep, and where it seems to be going. Thirty miles west of Lowmark's last pier the Uncovered ends at a cliff edge, and beyond it lies black water that has not dropped at all, an abyss of unknown depth with a current that runs downward. From the edge you can hear it draining: a low, endless sound like a throat swallowing. Salvagers who go to the brink report a warm updraft that smells of breath, and a light, very deep, that comes and goes.

#### Fathomhouse

The palace of the Lung-lines princes on the Headland, a grey stone pile with more of itself below ground than above. Its upper floors are halls of glass tanks, where the princes hold court underwater and courtiers in dry clothes bow to them through eight inches of green brine. Its cellars are flooded and have been extended twice to accommodate the Dowager Below. The lowest cisterns hold the retired-to-depth. Servants work in rubber aprons and never go below the fourth stair. The gratings in Tidepost Square that vent the palace's drains breathe out a green mineral cold, and on pressure days the air above them thickens until passers-by drop where they stand.

#### The Prop Yards at Keelrow

Four hundred warships stand on dry ground in long rows across the old Keelrow anchorage, three miles south of the Headland, each upright on a cradle of timber props, masts dressed, fully crewed. Sailors reef and furl sails that catch only wind and stand watches over a horizon of salt flats. Beneath the keels the sailors' families live in tents and sheds, a town of children who have never seen the sea and are proud of their fathers' ships.

#### Saltwick

The largest inland town, thirty miles east of Lowmark on the Rim Road spur, and the country's market for everything the Uncovered yields. Its cellars flood twice a day like every cellar in the country, and the townsfolk have given up and turned them into salt-pans: each house harvests its own indoor tide, dries it on racks, and sells the salt. Saltwick salt is reputedly the best on the Table. Visitors who eat too much of it begin to dream of drowning, and some begin to bail.

#### The Wreck Orchard

Eight miles out on the Uncovered, in a shallow basin, nearly two hundred wrecks of every age stand upright on the mud in loose rows, as if parked by a harbormaster. Nobody can explain why they are upright. Nobody can explain why their hatches are all open, or why their figureheads all face west. Salvagers live in the newest of them. Lantern-light can be seen in the oldest ones at night, which are empty.

#### Gullcry Strand

A long stretch of the old shoreline north of Lowmark, now a beach without a sea, where the Deepening holds its processions. A road of crushed white shell, laid by the faithful over two centuries, runs straight from the strand across the Uncovered toward the far water, nine miles long and marked every hundred paces by a post with a lantern-hook. On procession nights every hook is lit.

#### The Sounding Hall

The courthouse of the Drowned Bench on the Headland: a round stone hall whose floor is a shallow pool, ankle-deep, fed by the indoor tide. The accused stand in the water. The Tenanted judges sit on a stone bench at the far side, utterly still, and their verdicts are delivered in two voices. The frieze of the Beaching runs around the walls. The pool is always cold, and witnesses say that when a lie is spoken in the Hall, the water rises.

#### Lobbett's Drop

The place, somewhere on the far Uncovered, where Cray Lobbett found the stone table with eight chairs. He has told nobody its location. Salvage crews have searched for it for two years, and three of them did not come back. The name is a joke on the Mile. It is not funny out there.

#### The Face-Down Fields (new)

South of the Headland, on the salt flats below the old Keelrow shore, the coast lays out its ordinary dead: face-down, in rows, weighted with stones, for a tide that no longer comes. There are perhaps forty thousand of them in the fields now, in every stage, from the fresh to the leathered to the scattered bones the gulls have sorted. The smell carries to the Headland on a south wind. The layers of the dead walk the rows at dawn to set new bodies and straighten old stones. A layer who finds a body turned face-up reports it to the parish, and the parish sends a priest, because a dead man turned over is an omen, and lately the layers find one every week. Most are the work of the Pickers, who strip the dead of rings and buttons and teeth. Some are not. In 637 a layer of Mile Twelve was found asleep in the fields at dawn, lying in a row among the dead, face-down, with a dead woman's stones laid on his own back, and her body beside him turned over and arranged, with her hair combed out, as a man would arrange a wife. The Bench sentenced him to be loosed for life. He did not deny anything. He said only that she had been so cold, and nobody had held her, and the gulls had been at her, and he could not bear it. The layers burned the row.

#### Hollow Gaol (new)

The Admiralty's prison, a dry dock below the Headland with its gates sealed, where convicts sentenced to loosing serve their nights. The cells have no rings and no beds, only a pallet facing west, and the west wall is a grille open to the Uncovered. Netwatch guards sit outside with orders not to intervene. In the mornings they count the cells. The gaol's register has a column headed *Departed* and a column headed *Remained*, and the turnkeys bet on which a new convict will fill.

For sixty years Brinehollow was the happiest land on the Table. The herring came back so thick that nets broke, whales beached themselves like gifts, and the Lung-lines, who coughed water twice a day and swam like seals, became admirals and princes. When every newborn of the Brimming arrived gurgling and had to be held head-down until the water ran, it was called a mark of belonging. Inlanders who did not bail were "dry," which is still an insult.

The First Calling ended all that in one night. Four hundred people of Lowmark rose under a licked-clean moon and walked into the harbor in a long, quiet, orderly line, holding hands. The eleven who came back were the first Tenanted, and those who took them in were the first Wreckwives. One of the eleven is still on the Headland steps, wringing out his rags. By 240 the Admiralty had noticed that the Tenanted made perfect judges, and in four centuries no one has successfully appealed a verdict of the Drowned Bench.

In 459 the sea took seventeen captains of the fleet in a single night, from seventeen different ships at anchor in the old harbor, roped in their cabins. Each rope was found cut, from the inside, with the captain's own dress knife. Fourteen came back Tenanted in the following spring, and asked for their commands, and the Admiralty, which could find nothing in the Articles against it, gave them back. Their wives founded the Wreckwives that summer, at a table in the Lantern on Mile Nine, and the first rule they wrote was that a member must tell her story without crying, because the captains' wives had found that the things they came home to did not like to see them cry, and became so very kind.

In 512 the sea failed to return to its old line, and the Admiralty declared a temporary low tide. It has been temporary ever since. In 627 came the Great Pressure Day: every window on the Headland burst inward in one breath at noon, the bells of the Chapel Reach cracked, and that night eleven hundred and forty walked, and the Netwatch found the Mile's gutters running west so full that they overflowed and the boards of the pier were wet from end to end by dawn. In the last ten years the sea has fallen faster than in the previous fifty, the Callings have quadrupled, and the Tenanted on the Bench have gone from nine to ninety. In 639 Brenna Kelp presented the arithmetic that explained it, and the Admiralty made her arithmetic a crime.

## Rule, Law and Politics

Brinehollow is ruled by the **Tidal Admiralty**, a navy that has become a government. Its fleet sits aground on props at Keelrow, fully crewed, drilled daily, awaiting the sea's return. Rank is still given in ships: a district governor is a Captain, a province is a Squadron, the treasury is the Purser's Office, and a Captain may never have been afloat. Laws are issued as Standing Orders and read aloud from the deck of the flagship *Steadfast*, which has not moved since 588.

[fiction]
STANDING ORDER 4,117. Given aboard the Steadfast, Keelrow, at the dusk muster, Carving 641, and to be read at every mile-post.
1. The Fleet is at anchor awaiting the tide. All hands will conduct themselves accordingly.
2. The word "stranded," and all words of like meaning ("aground," "beached," "dry-docked," "finished"), are forbidden to officers on pain of the yard, and to civilians on pain of twenty strokes.
3. The Purser's Office will continue to levy the Rope Duty at two crumbs the fathom.
4. Households on the Lashings will sleep in lines of not fewer than five to a ring. Households of fewer than five will be joined to their neighbors by the Bailers' Warden.
5. The color of bail-water is a private matter and is not to be traded upon by any bucket-wife, broker or moneylender. (This Order is not enforced.)
6. Gatherings on the Uncovered at Low Water are permitted to the Tenanted and forbidden to all others.
7. The Fleet will be ready to sail at one hour's notice.
Given under the wet seal of the House of Vasht.
[/fiction]

Above the Admiralty sit the **Lung-lines princes**, the House of Vasht and its cadet branches, who can no longer breathe air for long and hold court from glass tanks in Fathomhouse. They sign the Admiralty's orders with a wet seal, through a hatch. Beneath the Admiralty sits the **Drowned Bench**: the Tenanted judges, whose verdicts are final, and whose loyalties nobody has ever thought to question out loud.

The great political fact is water. A land of sailors and fishers has lost its sea, and the Admiralty's entire policy is to pretend this is temporary. To say "stranded" of the fleet is an offense against the Articles. To publish the Salt Assay's figures is treason. Every faction's position is ultimately a position on the water: whether it is coming back, whether it should, and where it has gone.

Law is naval. Crimes are tried before the Drowned Bench, standing ankle-deep in the Sounding Hall's pool. Common sentences are flogging with a wet rope, fines in lacks, impressment into the Dry Fleet, and **loosing**: the convict is sentenced to sleep unanchored for a set number of nights, under guard, with the Netwatch instructed not to intervene. Loosing is not formally a death sentence. Everyone knows it is one, slower and more uncertain, and that the Bench's Tenanted judges hand it down with what looks like compassion. Neglecting to bail the helpless is murder. Cutting another's rope is the worst murder of all, and its punishment is to be loosed for life.

[pull] The Bench says it's mercy. The Bench is ninety of her. | — Lieutenant Sable Reef, of Justice Oake's rulings

### The Deep Blood

The Lung-lines are the god's own blood and the state's legitimacy, and they have spent six centuries trying to keep both from thinning. The House of Vasht holds that the depth of a Lung-line is measured by how much of the god is in it, and that the god in it must not be let out into lesser chests. Marriage among the Lung-lines is a matter of keeping the blood deep. Marriages between first cousins are ordinary and are celebrated on the Headland with the tying of a rope spun from both families' hair.

The rest is not celebrated. The Cutwrights' ledgers for the House of Vasht show older and closer pairings that the family does not discuss and the College does not publish. The Vasht marriage-book in the Fathomhouse chapel has eleven pages sewn shut with tarred thread, and the chaplains are forbidden to cut the stitches. The family chronicle records Ondric Vasht's wife only as "of his own blood, that it be kept deep," and records her name nowhere. Old Jory Spinwell of the Ropewalk has spun the wedding rope for every Vasht marriage since 589, and he says that three of the brides' ropes were spun to a length he was told to keep secret, and that the hair braided into two of them was the same grey as the groom's, from the same head.

The blood has kept deep. It has also kept other things. Vasht children are born with webbed fingers to the second knuckle, with eyes already wide and black, with a membrane over the nose; several in every generation are born not breathing, and are not mourned, but carried at once to the nursery tank and laid in it, where some of them begin, after a minute or two, to breathe water, and are counted among the family, and some do not. The household calls them **still-born to the deep**, and the tide-post of Fathomhouse carries their names in a column with no dates. The Cutwright Ansby Wrack has found something worse in the ledgers: that for three generations the House has been keeping its blood deep not only by marrying inward but by marrying downward, to the Tenanted, so that the princes in the tanks are the children of things that came back out of the sea. The Admiralty has encouraged the same marriages among its cadet officers and Tenanted widows for a century. The College has noticed that the children of such marriages carry a higher Cut than their parents should have given them. The god is not only taking people back. It is breeding.

[box: The Deep Blood in Play]
Keep the Vasht bloodline implied. The players should find it in fragments: a sewn page, a rope of one grey hair, a nursery tank with names on the wall and no dates, a Cutwright's ledger with a column of initials and a column of question marks, a courtier's sudden silence when asked who the prince's mother's mother was. Never stage it. The horror is what the House has decided it must do to stay deep, and what the coast has agreed not to ask, and what the god appears to want from all of it.
[/box]

## Culture and Daily Life

**Food.** Brinehollow eats what the Uncovered gives and what the Rim Road sells. Before the fall it ate fish, and fish are now a luxury hauled nineteen miles down the Mile in wet barrels. The poor eat Uncovered shellfish dug from the drying mud, which are safe for those who bail and poison to anyone dry; salt-cured gull; seaweed bread; and Fatlands meat in brine, which everyone pretends not to examine. Meals are cold and salty. Hot food is considered a little vulgar. The grace before eating with strangers is "Lack keep away, and keep you dry."

**Dress.** Oilcloth, tarred wool, sealskin for the rich. Everyone wears a rope at the waist, coiled, for anchoring at night: the cheap ones are hemp, the fine ones silk-and-wire, and a noble's waist-rope may be braided with silver and the hair of ancestors. The bail-bucket hangs from it. Losing your bucket is like losing your shoes. Course-sufferers wear veils of wet gauze to keep their faces moist. Mourners for the Called wear a **name-rope** at the wrist, a short braided cord with the lost one's name woven in, and never take it off.

**Marriage.** Sweethearts exchange bail-buckets. A wedding is the tying of a shared anchoring rope, each partner knotting the other's end before witnesses, and the vow is "I will hold your line." Remarriage after a Calling is permitted after a year and a day, which leads, often enough, to a household with two spouses when the first comes back Tenanted. Such houses are common enough on the Mile to have customs: the returned spouse sleeps in the cistern and the living spouse in the bed, and the one who married twice sleeps between, roped to both, and nobody on the street asks which of them is holding whose line in the night. The Wreckwives say the Tenanted are perfectly courteous about the arrangement, and that this is the thing the living spouses cannot bear.

**Death.** Those who die of ordinary causes are laid face-down on the Uncovered, weighted with stones, for the tide to take; the tide no longer comes, so the bodies lie in long rows on the salt flats, and the gulls are fat. The Called receive a **rope funeral**: their cut anchoring rope is divided among the family, one piece each and one for the wall, and the name is cut into the nearest tide-post. The priest asks, "Did the line hold?" and the family answers, "The line held. The sea was stronger." They must say it whether or not it is true. There is no body. There is never a body. Families keep the pieces of rope in a box by the bed, and hold them during the count.

**Children.** Taught to bail before they read, roped to a parent's wrist at night until seven. Children's games include holding-breath contests, which parents forbid and which are universal, and "Called," in which one child walks blindfold toward a wall while the others try to drag them back. Children are said to see the Tenanted most clearly: a Tenanted parent's own child often refuses to be held by them, at first, and then forgets why.

**Festivals.** Low Water, at the lowest tide of Carving, when the Tenanted gather on the Uncovered and sing. The Knotting, in early Grace, when every rope in a household is replaced. The Beaching, in Plenty, when the Hook-Brides' names are read on the Headland with honor and the hauling songs are sung with the struck verse hummed. Tablenight, when families set a bowl of seawater at the table's head and leave the west door unbarred, once a year, for whoever might come home.

**Crime and punishment.** The coast's crimes are the crimes of a crowded, frightened, roped-together people: theft of buckets, loose knots, rope-cutting, the selling of a neighbor's bail-water without leave, and the neglect of the helpless. Punishments are naval and public. Bucketing, the confiscation of a bucket for a day and a night so that the offender must beg every neighbor to bail them, is the shaming punishment for slander and loose knots; a bucketed woman crawling from door to door at dusk, gargling, holding out her empty hands, is a sight every child on the Mile has seen.

**Oaths on the dead.** The most binding oath in Brinehollow is sworn on a piece of a Called person's rope, held in the fist, with the words "By the line that did not hold." To break it is to invite the Called one to come back for you. People believe this literally, and sometimes they are right. Father Ebb has kept such an oath for fifty-one years. The Wreckwives' records hold the case of a Saltwick salt-merchant who swore on his drowned brother's rope to repay a debt, and did not, and was found one morning in his own cellar at the ebb of the indoor tide, face-down, drowned in two inches of water, with his brother's rope knotted round his wrist and his brother's wet footprints going back down the drain.

**Proverbs.** "Tie your own knot." "A dry man drowns twice." "Every wall has an ear, and every ear is a neighbor's." "Don't sleep facing your wishes." "The sea gives back, but not change." "Long pier, short life." "Blue in the bucket, rope on the bed." "Sweet water, sour neighbors."

[pull] Every man I take upstairs wants me to tie him to the bed after. Not for that. Just to sleep. I charge for the knot. | — Rosen, who works the rooms above the Last Rail

## Factions

### The Tidal Admiralty

The state: proud, hidebound, and commanding the largest landlocked navy in the world. The Admiralty believes, as an article of faith and of law, that the sea will return, and that when it does Brinehollow must be ready to sail. It wants order, the illusion of normality, and above all silence about the numbers. Its officers are brave, competent, and drilled to the bone in an art that has no use. Enemies: the Salt Assay's leakers, the Deepening, the Second Table's agents within its own ranks. Allies: the Netwatch, the Rim Road Company, which still pays port tolls on a port with no water. A PC joins by enlistment at any Admiralty house on the Mile.

### The Anchorites

An order who chain themselves permanently to rock, so the Calling can never take them. Their abbey is the Hanging Abbey, a cliff face hung with a thousand living monks. They believe the Calling is a temptation, and that refusing it, night after night, for a whole life, is the only holiness left. They want to endure. Some want more: to chain the whole country. Enemies: the Deepening, whom they call drowners. Allies: Wreckwives, who send them food. A PC joins by spending a month on the cliff in a trial-chain; most who try come down. A few did not want to.

The life is a slow body horror of its own. The collars are never removed, and over years the neck grows round them: the skin ulcerates, weeps, scabs, and finally heals over the iron like bark over a nail, so that an old Anchorite's collar is part of him, and rusts inside him, and stains his throat orange. Ledge-sores eat the hips and shoulders of those who lie on bare rock; the salt rain keeps them open. Monks whose chains fail fall four hundred feet, and the Basketfoot villagers gather what lands. Monks who sicken die on their ledges and are left chained there, as a teaching, until the gulls have finished and the bones can be lowered in the basket. The cliff is studded with these, and the living talk to them.

### The Deepening

A cult that treats the Calling as a sacrament. They believe Dromm is not regrowing but welcoming, that the sea is going home and taking its children with it, and that walking in willingly, in procession, with lanterns, is not death but homecoming. They want everyone baptized. Their processions at Gullcry Strand are illegal, regular, and quietly attended by the Netwatch, who are under orders to intervene and seldom do. Enemies: Anchorites, the Admiralty, every family with a member who joined. Allies: none declared; the Tenanted, it is whispered. A PC joins by walking the shell road to the last lantern and coming back to tell it.

The Deepening's hold over its people is gentle, total and very hard to break. New Shell-Walkers are given a new name and asked never to use the old one. They are fed, warmed, and sung to sleep with the Low Songs. Their ropes are cut, kindly, by the brothers and sisters, and hung over the headboard. Their families are spoken of with pity: *they are still so afraid*. Those who waver are not threatened. They are loved harder, sat with through the night, told that the voice they cannot hear is there and that it is only their fear that deafens them, until most come to believe that the fault is in them, and walk in to prove it isn't.

## Brinehollow Now

> It isn't coming back. It's waiting.
>> — a pier-hauler of the Raw Mile, looking west from Nineteen

The Salt Assay has done the arithmetic the Admiralty forbade: the volume of sea lost each year matches the number of Tenanted. The ocean is moving into the people, a bucket at a time. Every Called person who comes back brings a measure of the deep with them, folded up inside, and the sea is that much lower. The Tenanted now sit on every court in the land. A scavenger has found something on the Uncovered that should not be under any sea. And a shipwright is building a vessel to follow the water down.

The Callings are accelerating with the fall. Last Carving there were three nights on which more than five hundred walked. The Netwatch cannot cover the Mile. Dry Jonah's bottled brine has turned up in the Vigil's rouse, and sleepless strangers four hundred miles inland have started walking west. On the Uncovered, salvagers report a tall figure, seen at a distance at dusk, setting a table on the mud with great care, and walking on. And in the bell tower on the Headland, Finn Undertow counts the days until the body's tide, drifting a little every week, brings every chest on the coast to fill at the same moment. He has told nobody the number. He has begun to feel it in his own chest as a slow swell, like a breath being drawn by something very large, somewhere below.

### Adventure Hooks

- **The Rope-Cutter of Mile Fourteen.** Nine ropes cut in nine nights on the same street, with no knife ever found. The Netwatch suspects a murderer. The families suspect each other. She is not alone: someone small has been carrying a second blade.

## The People of Brinehollow

#### High Admiral Corvin Sund

Commander of four hundred warships standing on dry ground. Sixty-eight, square, grey-whiskered, in a blue dress coat kept so wet with brine that it never quite dries and smells of a harbor at low water. His voice is the parade-ground bark of a man who sailed as a boy, the last year the fleet floated, and has spent fifty years waiting to sail again. He inspects the ships weekly, has the hulls scraped of barnacles that are not there, and will hang any officer who says the word "stranded." He has hanged four, and stood at the foot of the *Steadfast*'s mast each time and watched the boots stop kicking, and gone to his cabin afterward and bailed for an hour, alone, and come out with his eyes red. He wants the sea back, and failing that he wants no one to admit it is gone. He fears the Salt Assay's numbers, and more than that he fears the morning he will stand on the *Steadfast*'s deck and feel the pull himself. **Secret:** he has had the flagship's keel sawn half through in the night, so that it can never float away without him. PCs meet him at an inspection, or in the dock when he wants a leaker found.

#### Harbormistress Kessa Drowle

She builds Lowmark's mile of new pier each year: fifty-two, tar to the elbows, a carpenter's pencil behind each ear, a cough she bails into the sea of sawdust on the work-barges. She speaks in measurements. She has calculated the year the timber runs out and the year the sea drops off the shelf entirely. Someone is driving them, at night, in a straight line west. PCs meet her when she hires guards for the timber barges, which the Uncovered's salvagers raid.

#### Justice Maren Oake of the Drowned Bench

Called at thirty, returned after forty days. She is fifty now, broad and grey-skinned, with black eyes and hands that never move during a hearing. The air around her is cold and close and smells of the deep, and those who stand before her feel their ears pop. She speaks in two tones at once, and the lower one sometimes finishes first. She has never given an unjust verdict, and she has sentenced more people to loosing than any judge in history, always gently. She wants justice, in a sense larger than the Admiralty's. **Secret:** her husband Tam has visited her chambers every week for twenty years, and she has never once told him that the woman he married is still in there, folded small, awake, and listening. Some nights, in the verdict-book, a single word appears in the margin in a cramped hand that is not the Bench's antique script: *wrong*. PCs meet her standing ankle-deep in her court.

#### Prince Aurel Vasht
