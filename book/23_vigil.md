# The Vigil

> Pinch me.
>> — the Vigil's commonest endearment, and its marriage vow

> Do not tell me you are tired. Everyone is tired. Tell me you are awake.
>> — sign painted over the door of every Waker station in the city

[fiction]
Statement of Hesper Coyle, rope-twister of Thimble Lane, the Slope, given at the Lanternside station to Inspector H. Grimmer in the presence of a Waker-Sergeant and a clerk, third watch, the eleventh day of Carving, 641. Entered as Blink-Matter Forty-One.
I want it written that I loved him. Write that first, before the rest, because after the rest nobody will believe it, and I need it to be somewhere in ink where they cannot scrape it.
We were on the long watch. You know the kind. Two days and the most of a third, because the rope order for the Lamplighters came in doubled and the cut rouse on our street has been weak since Plenty. Tobin said it was chalk. I said it was ash. We argued about it to stay up, the way you do. Arguing is good. Arguing is better than pinching because it gets the blood going in the face.
It was the third afternoon and it was one of those days. Grey. No wind. The tin on the roof did not move at all. You could hear the Lanternside bell from four streets off and then nothing, nothing, nothing for the whole quarter, and the light was the colour of milk in a bowl. I have lived thirty-four years on the Slope and I have never been so frightened of a sky.
Tobin was at the twisting-post and I was at the hackle. We were taking turns saying the numbers. One, he says. Two, I say. That is how we do it on a bad day. If the other one does not answer, you get up and slap them. We got to four hundred and something. I remember four hundred and twelve because it is the age of the Regent in the north and I made a joke about it, and he did not laugh, and I said four hundred and thirteen, and he said four hundred and fourteen. He did say it. I heard him say it.
Then his face was under my hands.
I do not know how to say it better than that. There was no between. I was at the hackle with the flax in my fist and the hackle-teeth were cold, and then it was not my fist, it was my two palms, flat, and under them his face, and his face was warm. He was lying down. Do you understand me? He was lying down on the floor of our own room, on his back, and somebody had put the folded sacking under his head like a pillow, and somebody had crossed his hands on his chest the way you see them in the old carvings from before, and his eyes were closed. His eyes were closed because my thumbs were on them.
The rest of my hands were over his mouth and nose. Flat and gentle and firm, like a mother tucking a blanket. That is what I keep coming back to. I was not pressing hard. There was no need to press hard. He had stopped some while before.
I want to say I screamed but I did not. I took my hands off him very slowly, the way you would lift your hands off a sleeping baby so as not to wake it, and I looked at my palms, and there was spit on them, and the shape of his teeth pressed into the meat below my thumb. He had bitten me. He had fought. I have no mark on my memory of it at all, only on my hand.
The Waker says I was out for two breaths. The sergeant here says two breaths. Mistress Pell across the lane says she saw me through the window and I was standing at the hackle, and then I was kneeling, and there was no time for me to get from one to the other, and she says I was humming. She says it was the old song, the one that is not allowed, the one about the lamb and the lid. I do not know that song. My mother never sang it to me. Nobody's mother ever sang it to anybody. I would like someone to tell me how I was humming it.
His body was still warm when the Wakers came. They rattled him. They needled his feet and the soft places under his arms the way they do with the Counted, in case, and of course he did not move, because he was not asleep, he was dead, and you cannot needle a dead man back. The sergeant was kind. He put a pin in my hand and closed my fingers over it and said hold that, Mistress, hold that tight, and I have been holding it since the second watch and my palm is running and I cannot feel it.
I keep thinking: where was I. If my hands were here, where was I. And the only answer I have is the room. Everyone knows about the room. I was in the room with the eye for two breaths, and in the room I was not doing anything, I was only standing, I was only looking at the lid, and it was so quiet, and it was so kind, and while I stood there being quiet something was using my hands to put my husband to bed.
You have forty of these, the sergeant says. Forty-one now. He says it is the same every time, the crossed hands, the folded pillow, the thumbs. He says it like it should help.
I do not want to be let go. I heard you talking about it at the door. I do not want to go back to Thimble Lane. Put me in the Rattlehouse. Put me in a lantern on a pole. Keep a Waker on me with the long needle every minute of every watch for the rest of my life, I will thank him for it, because the moment nobody is watching me I am going to blink again, and I have a sister, and she has two little ones, and they would let me in.
Write down that I loved him. Pinch me. Please. Somebody pinch me.
[/fiction]

## The Meal

Before it was the Vigil, the plateau was called Ludmere, and before it was a city it was a bed.

Iss, the Lidded, lay in a hollow on the high stone at the northeast corner of the Table, and it lay there for longer than anyone could count, asleep. Of all the Providers it was the only one no living person ever saw do anything. Ossel closed eyes; Ummer walked the fields; Dromm heaved under the sea. Iss slept. Its people built their houses around its sleeping, the way a village grows up around a well, and their whole religion was the art of being quiet near it. The old Ludmere word for a priest meant *one who walks softly*. Pilgrims came from every land to sleep within sight of it, because sleep near Iss was the deepest and kindest sleep on the Table, and the dreams that came with it were said to be true. The plateau was famous for soft beds and goose-down and oracles. It exported pillows.

Those who saw it described it as a long pale mass, larger than the cathedral that now stands in its place, shaped more like a drift of snow than a body, rising and falling with a breath so slow that you could watch a shadow cross half a field between one inhalation and the next. Its skin was grey-white and downy, and warm to the touch, and it smelled of clean linen, of a child's hair, of the inside of a blanket on a cold morning. Two great swellings at one end were taken to be its eyes, shut under lids the size of sails. No one had ever seen them open. The children of Ludmere were told that if Iss ever woke, everything that had ever been dreamed would have to be paid for.

In the ninth winter of the Long Lack the plateau was starving like everywhere else. The pillows had stopped selling. The goose-flocks had gone barren and then been eaten. People had begun to lie down near the god on purpose, because sleep was the one thing left that did not cost bread, and many of them did not get up, though in those days that was only hunger. When word came across the Table that the other peoples were turning on their Providers, the walkers-softly of Ludmere held a council in whispers and decided they would do the same, and that they would do it without waking it.

That decision is the root of everything that followed, and the Vigil has never been able to leave it alone. Every other people on the Table killed a god that held still. Ludmere killed a god that was asleep. Whether it consented, whether it knew, whether it felt the knives, are questions that no one alive can answer, and the Vigil's priests have spent three centuries not asking them in public.

The butchering took nine nights, because it had to be done so slowly. The cutters wrapped their blades in felt. They wore no shoes. Children were stationed all around the hollow singing lullabies, in shifts, a low continuous murmur of a thousand small voices, because the walkers-softly believed that if the god heard singing it would think itself still safe. The song they sang is the one the Vigil now forbids, *The Lamb and the Lid*, and there are people in the city who will cross the street rather than hear the first three notes of it whistled.

The flesh of Iss was pale all the way through, the colour of cream, and it did not bleed so much as seep: a warm thin fluid, sweet and faintly salt, that smelled like breath on a pillow. The cutters said that it was like carving warm bread. Many of them fell asleep while they worked. They would be found curled against the flank of the god, their knives still in their hands, their faces slack and happy, and they would be shaken awake and set back to the cutting, yawning. Everyone yawned. That is the detail the old accounts can never stop repeating. The whole plateau yawned for nine nights. Jaws cracked; eyes streamed; people dozed standing in the queue for their portion and woke with their bowls full. The yawning spread down the slopes into the farms and up into the shepherds' huts where no one had seen the god at all.

On the ninth night they came to the eyes. The families who would become the Eye-lines had claimed them by lot, or by force, or by bribery; the accounts disagree, and the Eye-lines have had every version but their own burned. The lids would not open. Not with fingers, not with hooks, not with the blade. When the cutters tried to slit them, the skin of the lids parted and closed again behind the knife like water. In the end the eyes were taken out whole, with the lids still shut over them, two great warm globes in their sheaths of downy skin, and they were divided among nine households and eaten, by lamplight, in silence, still closed. The eaters said afterward that the meat tasted of nothing at all, and that every mouthful made them feel they had just put down something very heavy.

When the god was gone there was a long shallow pressed hollow in the stone, the shape of something that had lain there forever, and it was still warm. It is still warm now. The Vigil calls it the Pillow, and nothing has ever been built in it.

The next morning, the Lack was over. The crops on the plateau's lower terraces rose in a week. And the people of Ludmere slept that night as they had never slept, long and dreamless and sweet, all of them, every one, and they all woke up.

For three hundred and forty years after that they went on sleeping and waking like anybody. They dreamed a little too vividly. Their oracles grew rich. Their pillow-trade recovered. They told themselves they had got away with it, and in the oldest Ludmere hymnals, before the Waking Laws ordered them burned, there is a verse thanking Iss for being *the only god too kind to notice it was eaten*.

[box: What the Eye-lines Ate]
The Eye-lines say that they ate Iss's sight, and that this is why they are the most perceptive bloodline on the Table, and why they alone are fit to watch the city. The walkers-softly of the old priesthood, before they were suppressed, said something else: that what the Eye-lines swallowed was not the eyes but the lids. Iss's sight was never used. Its lids were the whole of its nature, the shutting, the resting, the turning away. That is what has been growing in the Eye-lines for six centuries. When their children are born without eyelids, it is not because the god's eyes have opened in them. It is because the god has taken its lids back.
[/box]

## The Rule of the Land

Anyone who sleeps does not wake up.

Since the First Night of 341 A.G., every person who falls asleep in the Vigil goes to the same place. It is one shared dream, and everyone who has been there and come back for even a moment describes it the same way: a long room, dim, warm, low-ceilinged, without windows, lit from nowhere, and extending further than it should. The floor is soft. The air is the temperature of a body. Far down at the end of it, in the wall, there is a single enormous closed eye, its lid smooth and grey and faintly downy, larger than a house. It does not open. It does not need to. Standing in that room, the visitors say, is the most restful thing a person can feel. There is no fear in it at all. That is the horror.

Sleepers do not come back from the room. The body they leave behind lives on: it breathes, it is warm, its heart beats slow and steady, it does not age, it does not soil itself or starve or rot. It cannot be roused. Not by shaking, not by water, not by fire, not by the long needle driven under the nail. Its eyes, peeled open, are rolled back and wet. It is not dead, and it will not die of anything but violence, and it is not there.

So nobody sleeps. That is the whole of the law and the whole of the culture. An entire city-state, some four hundred thousand waking souls and sixty thousand sleeping ones, has stayed awake for three hundred years by statute, by drug, by pain and by each other. Exhaustion is not a condition here. It is the climate. A Vigilant does not speak of being tired any more than a fish speaks of being wet.

### What the God Allows

The plain fact that keeps the Vigil alive is that nobody dies of wakefulness. Elsewhere on the Table a person kept from sleep for ten days goes mad, and in a month is dead. In the Vigil the body endures it. The god in the blood keeps the heart beating and the lungs working however long the eyes stay open, a mercy or a leash. What the body cannot do is stop wanting to sleep. The pressure builds and does not break. It sits behind the eyes like a hand pressing down on the lids, heavier every hour, and it never, ever lifts. A Vigilant of fifty has been awake for fifty years, with no gap, no rest, no night, every hour of every day accumulated behind her face like silt. She is not dying of it. She is only drowning in it, a little deeper each year, while standing up.

What gives way is the mind. Sleep-starved people see things that are not there. They hear voices in the hiss of the lamps. They forget what they did a minute ago and remember things that never happened. They lose their tempers, weep at nothing, laugh at funerals, and come to believe that their spouses have been replaced by careful copies. The Vigil has lived inside this for three centuries, and it has done what every people on the Table does with an unbearable thing: it has made it into an institution. The hallucinations are called the Seeming, and when enough people see the same thing, it is news.

### The Rule Follows the Blood

The first refugees of 342 A.G. fled the plateau by the thousand, certain that if they could only reach the border they could lie down safe. They walked for four days without rest down the old Pilgrim Road toward the Rim, and when they crossed the boundary stones they lay down in the heather, weeping with relief, and slept. Not one of them woke. They are still there. The stretch of road below the border stones is called the Sleepers' Mile, and the bodies lie along it in the grass, breathing, moss-furred, lichen-crusted, with the heather grown up through their clothes.

The rule follows the blood. Anyone born of the Vigil, anyone who carries Iss, goes to the room when they sleep, wherever on the Table they happen to close their eyes. The envoys the Vigil sends abroad travel with paid nudgers and chests of rouse. And the rule holds the land too: a foreigner who falls asleep within the Vigil's borders goes to the room exactly as a native would. Every Rim Road caravan bound for the plateau stops at the Last Beds, the Company inn below the border stones, so its drivers can sleep their fill before they climb. Those who misjudge the climb are delivered to the Dormitory and billed to their employers.

Whether the Unfed of the Fast, who carry no god, are subject to the room in the Vigil has never been tested. The Watch of Watches would very much like to test it.

### The Counted

The sleepers are not left where they fall. By law, every one is collected within a bell and carried to the Dormitory, a building the size of a city district, and laid in a numbered bed in a numbered row. There are sixty thousand beds. The sleepers are called the Counted, because in 401 A.G. a Dream-Diver came back from the room with a number: the number of sleepers at which the eye in the wall will open.

The number is a state secret. Only the Matron of the Dormitory now living knows it. The beds are nearly full. Everyone in the Vigil knows that the beds are nearly full, and everyone does the same arithmetic in the small hours of every watch, and nobody knows what the answer is, and the bells ring every quarter hour, and the lamps never go out, and the city goes on, pinching itself.

[box: GM Advice — Running the Rule]
The rule of the Vigil is the simplest on the Table and the cruellest to play, because it attacks the one thing the players do without thinking. Every scene in the Vigil should carry a little weight on the eyelids. Describe warmth as a threat. Describe silence as a threat. Let a long, comfortable conversation by a fire become frightening simply because it is long and comfortable. Use the Weariness rules in Playing a Vigilant for every character in the city, native or foreign, and never let the players forget the bell. When the party rests, ask them who is watching whom, and how. When they plan an ambush that requires waiting, ask them how long. The horror of the Vigil is not a monster that comes for you. It is the room that waits for you to come to it, and it is endlessly, kindly patient.
[/box]

## The Regrowth in the Vigil

Iss is growing back, and what grows back is rest. Not the rest of a body that has worked and lies down; the rest of a thing that has been interrupted, and wants very badly to finish what it was doing. In the Vigil the Regrowth does not look like a monster. It looks like a person who is tired. That is what makes it so hard to see, and so easy to deny, in a city where everyone is tired.

### The Taste

Heavy lids. A drag on the face, as though fine threads had been sewn through the eyelids and tied to something below the floor. The Taste is the commonest godsign in the Vigil, and most Scrapling families do not get much past it: a constant gritty sting behind the eyes, a slowness to focus, a habit of rubbing the face that leaves the skin under the eyes raw and purple. The sign everyone watches for is the yawn. A person in the Taste yawns too wide and too long, the jaw cracking, the eyes streaming, and the yawn goes through a room like a rumour: one person, then three, then the whole bench in the rouse-house, jaws stretching in a slow wave, each person fighting it and losing. Children in the Taste are called *heavy*. Mothers check them every morning by yawning at them across the table, and watching.

The Taste carries a dream without sleep. Those in it sometimes see the long room in the corner of their eye, a dim warm doorway standing open where a wall should be, and catch the smell of clean linen in a street that stinks of lamp-smoke.

### The Appetite

Blinks. A blink, in the Vigil, does not mean the shutting of an eye. It means a sleep of a second or two, taken on the feet, mid-step, mid-sentence, mid-meal, without warning and without consent. The head does not drop. The body does not fall. From the outside, the blinker simply pauses, as if listening, for a breath or two, and then goes on. From the inside, the blinker is in the long room with the eye for exactly as long as they are out, standing on the soft floor in the warm air, at peace, and then is torn back out of it gasping.

The trouble is that blinkers come back from the room having moved. They were at the window and now they are at the door. They were holding a cup and now the cup is in the fire. They were speaking to a friend and now the friend is staring at them with a white face and will not say what they just said. Most blinks are small: a lost sentence, a dropped stitch, a step taken. Some are not. The worst are the blink-murders, killings done by ordinary people in a sleep of two breaths, which they do not remember and could never have imagined, and which are always done the same way. The Appetite is the stage at which a person in the Vigil stops being trusted, even by themselves.

Those in the Appetite also hunger for comfort, which in the Vigil is a perversion. They stroke fabric. They stand too long near stoves. They are caught pressing their cheeks against warm walls with their eyes half shut and a look on their faces that respectable people find obscene. Rouse-house slang for this is *nuzzling*, and it is grounds for divorce.

### The Course

The lids thin, shrink, and are gone. It happens over months: first a translucence, so that the iris shows through the shut lid as a dark smudge; then a shrinking, the lids drawing back like a burnt page curling from its edges; then a soft raw rim of pink where they were, and the eye beneath it wide, staring, naked, permanently open. The eyes of the lidless must be wetted by hand every half minute or they cloud and crack. The poor use rag and a pot of water. The rich keep attendants with sable brushes and silver bowls of rosewater warmed to blood heat. The lidless cannot close their eyes to weep, to wince, to blink against smoke or wind or a blow. They see everything that happens in front of them, and they forget none of it. Their memories become perfect and unbearable.

The Eye-line nobility are born this way, and call it purity. Their children come into the world staring, and the midwives of the Height do not slap them to make them cry, but to make them look away; none of them can. The Lidless Succession of 480 A.G. made the Course a qualification for rule, on the reasoning that a person who cannot shut their eyes cannot fall asleep. No lidless person in the Vigil has ever been Counted. No one has thought hard about why, except the walkers-softly, who are no longer permitted to think aloud.

### The Brink

Before the Seating comes the long dreadful plateau that the Vigil's physicians call the Brink and its poor call *going grey*. The lidless at the Brink do not blink at all, but they begin to *be* somewhere else while they stand among the living: their gaze fixes on a point a great distance behind the wall, their breathing slows to the rhythm of the Dormitory, and anyone who stands near them begins to yawn and cannot stop. People in the Brink can put others down. Not by any act; by being near, by looking, by speaking softly. They are dangerous to love. The Watch keeps a ward for them under the Nodding Stair, and the attendants who work there wear wax in their ears and wire across their eyelids, and are rotated every hour.

### The Seating

A sleeper who begins to move. Lips first.

For three hundred years the Counted have lain utterly still. In the last ten years, a handful in the oldest rows have begun to stir. A finger curls. A head turns, by degrees, over weeks, until the face looks along the row toward the next bed. The lips part and shape words that make no sound. A sleeper in the Seating is the god, partly returned, wearing a body it took into its room and has been patiently furnishing from the inside. The Vigil does not use the word Seating. The Dormitory's orderlies call such a sleeper *restless*, the way a nurse speaks of a feverish child, and they do not like to turn their backs on one.

The worst thing about the moving sleepers is that their faces are happy.

[box: What Is Restless in the Rows]
As of Carving 641, the Matron's private register lists nineteen restless sleepers. Bed One, Aveline Morne, moves her lips in shapes the Matron writes down. Bed 40,112, the son of Odile Farthing, has grown from a boy of four to the size of a man in eleven years, and smiles. Bed 7,730 has turned its head toward the Dormitory's east door and holds it there; the orderlies have moved the door. Beds 212 through 219, eight members of one family who lay down together on the First Night, have begun, over the course of a year, to reach toward each other across the gaps between their beds. Their fingertips now nearly touch. The Matron has measured the distance. She has not separated the beds, because she does not know what will happen if she does, and she does not know what will happen if she does not.
[/box]

## Land and Weather

The Vigil sits on a high bare plateau of grey stone at the northeast of the Table, a great tilted table-top of rock that rises out of the eastern plain in sheer cliffs on three sides and slopes down, on the fourth, toward the Rim Road and the soft bad country of heather and stream that leads to it. The plateau is called the Lid. It is windy, cold at the edges, warm in the middle, almost treeless, and covered from one end to the other by a single city.

That city is never dark. There are ten thousand public lamps on its streets, and a hundred thousand more in its houses, and in its squares burn great open iron furnaces, the Bonfires, tended day and night by the Lamplighters' Guild. Every roof carries mirrors, angled to catch the furnace-glow and throw it back down into the lanes, so that the light comes from every direction at once and there are no shadows anywhere a person could close their eyes in. There has been no night on the Lid for three centuries. The smoke of all those lamps hangs over the plateau in a permanent brown ceiling, lit from beneath, glowing a dull bruised orange, so that from the Rim Road the Vigil looks like a coal left burning in an empty grate. Nobody born in the Vigil has ever seen a star. Some of the children do not believe in them.

The city stinks of lamp-oil, hot tin, scorched wick, rouse-grounds and sweat. Its sounds are clatter, bells, rattles, the shouts of nudgers, the barking of the dogs that are bred and paid to bark, and under everything the hiss and flutter of a hundred thousand flames.

### The Weather of Comfort

The danger here is comfort. Other lands fear storms. The Vigil fears a nice day.

The warm wind off the eastern plain is called the Yawn. It comes up over the cliffs in late Plenty and early Carving smelling faintly of hay and sun-warmed stone, soft as a hand on the back of the neck, and on Yawn days the Wakers double their patrols, the bells ring every eighth of an hour instead of every quarter, and the Glass House fills. Rain on a roof is so soothing that the Vigil's roofs are built of loose overlapping tin so that rain will clatter, bang and scrape on them instead of patter. Snow is a horror: the hush of it, the softness, the way it muffles the bells. During snowfall, the Watch orders every householder to bang a pot at their door for one minute in every ten.

The worst weather of all is a mild, quiet, overcast afternoon, windless, grey, the light all the same colour, the sounds of the city flattening out under the cloud. The city records those days in its annals as disasters, with a death toll. The Grey Ninth of Carving 588 put eleven hundred people into the Dormitory between the fourth bell and the dusk lamps, and is commemorated every year with a day of compulsory shouting.

### The Seasons

Grace brings cold winds off the cliffs, which the Vigil loves: it is the healthiest season, and the Dormitory takes fewest in it. Plenty is long and warm and dangerous; the Yawn blows, and the poor suffer most in their close hot rooms on the Slope. Carving is the season of grey afternoons and the worst losses of the year. Lack brings snow, early dark the city refuses to acknowledge, and Tablenight, the one minute in all the year when the lamps go out.

### A Gazetteer of the Vigil

#### The Dormitory

A building the size of a district, on the lower south side of the Lid, where the plateau begins to slope. It was begun in 342 A.G. as a single converted granary and has been extended, wing by wing and storey by storey, for three hundred years, until it is the largest roofed space on the Table. Inside, it is one hall after another, low-ceilinged and lamplit, and in each hall the beds stand in rows, iron-framed, narrow, white-sheeted, numbered in black paint on the foot-rail. Sixty thousand of them. Every one but fewer than two thousand is occupied.

It is completely silent. The orderlies wear felt slippers, as the walkers-softly once did. No bell is rung inside. And in that silence, the one sound is the breathing. The Counted all breathe in time. Sixty thousand chests rise and fall together, slow, deep, regular, perhaps four breaths to the minute, and the air in the halls moves with them like a tide: a long warm sigh drawn in through the doors, a long warm sigh pushed out. Standing in the central hall is like standing in the lung of an enormous animal. The lamps flicker in rhythm. New orderlies find that within an hour their own breathing has slowed to match, and they must be taken outside and slapped.

The building smells of clean linen and of skin, the sweetish smell of bodies that do not sweat and do not age. Dust does not settle on the Counted. The orderlies change the sheets anyway, every Plate, because someone must do something.

#### The Nodding Stair

The council hall of the Watch of Watches, at the summit of the Height, the highest district of the city. It is a single staircase, broad as a road, which spirals up through a hollow tower of grey stone and never arrives anywhere, its top steps returning by a hidden ramp to its bottom, so that the council climbs forever. Its architect, Benedikt Lour, designed it so that no one could sit comfortably, lean comfortably or stand still comfortably; the steps are of uneven height and slant subtly outward. The Watch has sat in session on the Stair, in shifts, for two hundred years without adjourning. Its members are always climbing. Debate is conducted on the move; motions are carried by those still breathing hard enough to say aye. The Warden-Prime alone stands still, on a landing halfway up called the Lid's Eye, and the whole council passes before her once every quarter hour.

#### Lampwrights' Row

The brightest street in the world. It runs along the spine of the Lid between the Height and the Slope, and on both sides of it are the workshops where the city's mirrors are silvered, its lenses ground, its burners cast, its wicks braided and its lamp-glass blown. Every shopfront is a display of lit lamps, and every roof a forest of polished mirrors, so that the Row is lit from above and below and every side at once and a walker there casts no shadow at all. Visitors from other lands are blinded for the first hour and get headaches for a week. The Vigil's rich take their constitutionals there, squinting with pleasure, and courting couples walk it arm in arm, pinching. The dead of the city are burned in the Row's great furnaces, and their light goes out into its lamps. *She's on the Row now* is how a Vigilant says that someone has died.

#### The Glass House

A quarantine for contagious yawners, on a bare spur of rock at the windward edge of the Lid where the cold winds hit hardest. It is a long greenhouse of thick green glass in iron frames, divided into cells, each lit from outside by its own lamp. The yawners inside are masked in stiff leather muzzles that hold the jaw half closed. Visitors speak to them through speaking-tubes and are forbidden to look at their faces. The Glass House is the most frightening building in the city to people who have never been inside it and the saddest to people who have.

#### The Slope

The slum quarter, sprawling down the south side of the Lid below the Dormitory, built of tin, plank and rope in steep lanes too narrow for the Wakers' carts. Rouse is cut thin here, with chalk, ash, brine and worse. Nudgers are cheap and are mostly children. The lamps are fewer and dimmer, the oil is bad, and the Bonfires are fed with dung and rags. Whole families go down in an afternoon, one after another, the last one awake moving from body to body slapping and screaming until they too go quiet. When the Dormitory carts come down the Slope, the people come out to watch, and to count.

#### The Pillow

The hollow where Iss lay, at the exact centre of the Lid. It is a long shallow depression in the bedrock, a quarter mile from end to end, smoothly curved, shaped like the impression of something enormous that lay on its side with its knees drawn up. Nothing has ever been built in it. The stone is warm to the touch in every season and faintly soft, like a cushion with the stuffing settled. Snow melts there. Yawners who stand at its rim begin to weep. The Watch has fenced it with iron spikes and lamps on poles, and the Wakers who guard it work in pairs and are relieved every bell. It is a capital crime to lie down in the Pillow. It is not a crime that is ever tried, because those who commit it are never available for trial; they are lifted out by orderlies with hooked poles and carried straight to the Dormitory, smiling.

#### The Rattlehouse

The Wakers' central prison and school, a squat octagon of black brick at the foot of the Height. It is the loudest building in the Vigil. Its cells are lined with tin and hung with chimes; its corridors are patrolled by Wakers with rattles who shake them every few breaths; its prisoners are kept on their feet in standing-stalls with iron collars at the throat. Here the Wakers learn their needlework, and here the city's criminals serve their sentences, which are measured not in years but in wakefulness. A prisoner in the Rattlehouse is kept awake by the state, not by themselves, and that is the punishment: to be held in the hands of an institution that does not care whether it hurts you, only that you do not close your eyes.

#### The Bitterhouse

The state rouse-works, a sprawling complex of copper stills, roasting-ovens, drying-sheds and grinding-mills on the north side of the Lid, where the Apothecary-General's chemists make the city's stimulant. The air for half a mile downwind tastes of burnt sugar and iron and makes the teeth ache. The Bitterhouse workers are the most alert people in the Vigil and the shortest-lived, their teeth black stumps by twenty-five, their hearts giving out by forty. Under the Bitterhouse are the Proving Rooms, where new strains are tested on prisoners lent from the Rattlehouse, and where Dr. Aldous Crane keeps the curve he will not publish.

#### The Pinchmarket

The city's perpetual market, filling three squares and the lanes between them below Lampwrights' Row, open every hour of every watch for three hundred years. Rouse is sold here in every grade, from the Bitterhouse's sealed black cakes to Slope sweepings in twists of paper. So are pins, burr-cloth, bells, rattles, ice, smelling-salts, and nudgers. The nudger-stands are at the market's eastern end: children and old women and out-of-work Wakers sitting on stools beneath painted boards giving their rates by the bell. The most expensive nudgers advertise their own wakefulness: *Not One Blink In Nine Years* or *Witnessed Lidless, Sworn by the Watch.*

#### The Tether Hall

The Dream-Divers' house, a domed rotunda beside the Dormitory with a single bed at its centre under a great brass winch. Divers lie down on the bed in harness, wired at the wrists, ankles and throat to the winch and to a team of eight hauliers, and go to sleep on purpose. When the bell is struck, the hauliers drag them back. The method is a secret of the Hall, but it involves the harness, a great deal of pain, and something done to the diver's tongue. The floor around the bed is scarred with the heel-marks of three centuries of hauliers bracing. Many dives do not come back. The bodies of those who stay are laid in the Dormitory under their own row, the Divers' Row, and their harnesses are hung above the beds.

#### The Pendulum Hall

The house of the city's clocks, a tall narrow tower of grey stone in the Height where the Hourkeeper sets the time for the bells. Inside it, from the top of the tower to the cellar, hangs a single pendulum, a great iron bob on a rope of twisted wire, swinging through a slow arc that once took exactly four seconds. Around the walls, two hundred and forty smaller clocks tick in their cases. The tower hums. Those who stand inside it long enough begin to feel, under the ticking, a slowness, as if the beats were drifting a little further apart.

#### The Last Beds

The Rim Road Company inn below the border stones, at the foot of the Pilgrim Road where it leaves the Rim. It is the last place a foreigner may safely sleep before the climb, and the Company sells that sleep dearly. Its long wooden halls are full of beds, the only beds within thirty miles of the Vigil, and Vigilant travellers coming down from the plateau on Company business will pay to stand at the doorway of the sleeping-hall and look at them. Some weep. Some have tried to lie down and been dragged out by the Company's porters, who are paid by the Watch for each one they save. The innkeeper keeps a book of those they did not.

#### The Sleepers' Mile

Below the border stones, along the old Pilgrim Road, lie the refugees of 342 A.G., who fled the plateau to sleep beyond its borders and found that the rule followed them. There are about four thousand of them, scattered along a mile of road and heather. They still breathe. They are grown over: moss in the hair, lichen on the cheeks, heather pushing up through the rotted rags of their clothes, the bones of their faces showing through skin that has been rained on for three hundred years and has not aged a day. Pilgrims and Rim Road drovers pass them with their eyes averted. Children from the caravans dare each other to touch one. The Watch has never brought them in: there are not enough beds, and they do not count. Or so the Watch hopes.

### The First Night

It came on the third night of Grace, 341 A.G., which had been a warm and pleasant evening. There was no sign. Ludmere went to bed. In the morning, roughly a third of it did not get up: they lay in their beds breathing slow and even, smiling, and no amount of shaking would rouse them. Their families screamed in the streets. The physicians were sent for and could not wake them. By the next night the city understood only that a sickness had come; and most of it, exhausted with weeping and fear, went to bed. In the morning, half of what remained did not wake.

Those who survived the third night were the ones too frightened to lie down: the anxious, the guilty, the insomniac, the mad, the nursing mothers, the drunks, the watchmen, the children too hungry to sleep. They sat up through the dark in kitchens together, slapping each other, shouting, banging pans, holding hands and squeezing until the knuckles cracked. Those people are the ancestors of the Vigil. The city descends, by a kind of hideous selection, from its worst sleepers. Vigilants say it with pride.

### The Waking Laws and the Burning of the Beds

In the spring of 342 the survivors met in the old Ludmere Square and passed the Waking Laws by acclamation. Lying down was forbidden. Closing the eyes for longer than a breath was forbidden. Every bed, couch, mattress, pillow and cushion on the plateau was carried into the squares and burned, along with the goose-down warehouses, the oracles' dream-books, and the dreamers themselves when they protested. The pyres burned for a month; they were the first Bonfires. The city took its new name from a word the old priests had used for the night before a feast: *the vigil*. It was meant to sound temporary.

### The Count

In 401 a diver named Iselde Marrow, on her eleventh descent, walked further into the long room than anyone had and stood for a long while near the eye. When she was hauled back, half-dead with pain, she said she had heard the number. It had been told to her, she said, very gently, as if by a mother explaining to a child how many more days until a holiday. She was brought before three officials of the Watch, in a sealed room, and made to say it once. Then the officials agreed that the number was too dangerous to leave in a living head, and they had her laid down in a bed. Her bed was the next one in the row. Two of the three officials were dead within the decade, by accident; the third was the first Matron of the Dormitory, and the number has passed from Matron to Matron since, whispered once at the bedside of the dying.

## Rule, Law and Politics

The Vigil is governed by the Watch of Watches, a council of some three hundred Eye-line nobles who sit, or rather climb, in three shifts around the clock on the Nodding Stair. It has not adjourned in two hundred years. Its members are lidless by law, attended by eye-wetters, and watched by one another, since in the Vigil the oldest political truth is that anyone may blink. At its head is the Warden-Prime, always an Eye-line, always lidless, watched in turn by attendants who wet the royal eyes with a brush every half minute.

Beneath the Watch sit the Offices: the Apothecary-General (rouse), the Lamplighter-General (oil and light), the Hourkeeper (time and bells), the Matron (the Dormitory), the Registrar of the Seeming (the public record), and the Captaincy of the Wakers (everything else).

### The Law of the Lid

The law is simple. Lying down is a crime. Closing your eyes for longer than a breath is a crime. Letting someone near you nod off is negligence, punishable as manslaughter if they go down. Selling sleep is a crime. Singing *The Lamb and the Lid* is a crime. Possession of down, feathers, wool batting or any soft stuffing in a quantity greater than will fill a shoe is a crime. Speaking of the room in a soothing manner is incitement.

The police, the Wakers, carry rattles and long needles, and they are entitled to needle any citizen who appears drowsy, without warrant and without apology. A citizen who objects to being needled is presumed to be drowsy.

Punishment is wakefulness. Minor offenders are sentenced to hours at the Rattlehouse, kept on their feet by Wakers with needles. Serious offenders are sentenced to the Lantern: an iron cage hung from a pole in a public square, open on every side, lit by its own lamp, in which the condemned is kept awake by any passing citizen with a pin, for as long as the sentence runs. Long sentences in the Lantern produce Seeming so violent that the condemned believe they are being eaten by the crowd, and sometimes the crowd believes it too.

Until 590 the capital sentence was Bedding. The condemned were led to the Dormitory in a white gown, given warm milk and poppy, laid in a bed and allowed to sleep, while a crowd outside sang the forbidden lullaby, the one day in the year it was permitted. It was considered the most merciful execution on the Table, and envied. It was suspended when the Matron reported that beds were scarce. Since then, the worst criminals have been held in the Rattlehouse awaiting a sentence that cannot be carried out, and the Rattlehouse is very full.

### The Three Parties

The deepest political question is the Dormitory, and the Watch is split three ways over it.

The **Builders** want more beds. They are the oldest and largest party, the party of the Architect and the Matron's office, and their position is that the number can be postponed by construction, because the Counted must be housed somewhere and every bed is a delay. Their critics observe that a bed does not change the number, and that building more beds is only building a bigger waiting room.

The **Removalists** want the Counted moved out of the country: carted down the Pilgrim Road, laid along the Rim, sold to Sallowreach or the Fatlands, buried, sunk, anything to get them off the Lid. Their theory is that the count is local. The Sleepers' Mile, they point out, has been beyond the border for three centuries and the eye has not opened. Their opponents point out that nobody knows whether the Sleepers' Mile is counted or not.

And one party, very quietly, wants the number reduced. They do not have a name in the public record. Among themselves they call themselves the Lessening, and their position is arithmetic: if the eye opens when the Counted reach a certain number, then the Counted must not reach it, and since the Counted cannot be woken, some of them must stop being Counted. The Lessening does not use the word murder. It uses the words *reduction*, *thinning* and *relief*. It has drawn up lists.

## Culture and Daily Life

### No Beds

There is not a bed, couch or cushion in the city. Floors slope, a little, so that a person who lies on them slides. Chairs have one leg, and must be balanced on; a person who dozes on a one-legged chair falls off it. Walls are studded with iron knobs at shoulder height, so that no one can lean. Doorways have no frames to rest against. Tables are chest-high. The Vigil eats, works, talks and loves standing up, and its people have the thick calves, the bad backs and the swollen ankles to show for it.

### Nudging

Everyone has a nudger. A nudger is the person whose job is to keep you awake: a spouse, a sibling, a friend, a hired child. Nudgers watch for the drooping lid, the slackened jaw, the long pause; they pinch, slap, shout, prick and shake. In a family, everyone nudges everyone, in a ring, so that no one is ever unwatched. A person without a nudger is called *unwatched*, and it is the loneliest word in the language.

Marriage is the promise to keep each other awake, and the vow is two words: *Pinch me.* Weddings are loud, bright, short, and held at the Bonfires. The couple pinch each other on the inside of the wrist until they bleed, and the guests throw bells. Love between spouses is tender and fierce and frightened, because every intimacy is a danger; what comes after love, in a body, is drowsiness, and the Vigil's lovers keep a rattle by them and a lamp at full wick, and their tenderness is shot through with watchfulness. *The little death is the big one here*, the rouse-house joke goes, and nobody laughs.

### Rouse

Rouse is a bitter black stimulant, ground and roasted from the dried caps of staremoss, a grey lichen that grows only on the cliff-faces of the Lid, mixed with Bitterhouse salts whose composition is a state secret. It is sold as pressed cakes, as powder, and as a thick black syrup, and it is taken hourly, chewed, sipped or packed against the gum. It tastes of burnt sugar, iron and bile. It makes the heart race, the hands shake and the pupils widen. Teeth go first: they blacken and loosen and drop out, and a Vigilant over forty with a full set is either rich, lidless or lying. Then the heart. The ordinary death of an ordinary Vigilant is a rouse-heart: a burst of thudding, a grey face, a hand to the chest, and the long-awaited lying down on the floor.

The dose has risen every decade since 390. A modest citizen spends two lacks a day on rouse. On the Slope it is cut to stretch it, and the cuts are worse than the drug.

### Pain as Courtesy

A slap is a favour. Guests are offered a pin on arrival, as other lands offer bread and salt, and to refuse it is an insult. Clothes are made of burr-cloth, a coarse wool woven with dried thistle-heads; the rich wear galled silk, laced with fine wire that pricks at every movement. Shoes are cobbled with a pebble in the heel. Collars are set with pins. A Vigilant's skin is a map of small courtesies: the scabs on the wrists, the bruises on the upper arm, the neat rows of needle-scars along the forearm. To bear them is respectability. To have unmarked skin is suspicious.

### Food

A full belly is a bed. Meals in the Vigil are small, frequent, cold and sour: pickled roots, hard bread, salt meat out of Fatlands barrels, vinegar, sorrel, smoked cliff-goat. Warm milk is illegal. Sugar is rationed. Soup is served lukewarm, by law. Rich households eat from vessels with sharp rims. The grace is the common one, *Lack keep away*, said with the eyes open, and followed in the Vigil by a slap on the table.

### The Seeming

After enough waking, everyone hallucinates. A Vigilant hears voices in the hiss of the lamps, sees insects walking across the plaster, watches faces rise up in the patterns of the cobbles, sees a dead mother at the end of a lane. These private visions are a nuisance and are ignored. But sometimes a whole street sees the same thing. A woman made of moths walking down Gutter Lane. A rain of teeth over the Pinchmarket. A door in the side of the Nodding Stair that was not there before. When that happens, the Vigil has made it a civic sense. Witnesses report it to the Registrar of the Seeming, the Seemers record it, and when enough unconnected people have seen the same thing, it is entered in the public record as news, read aloud at the Bonfires, and printed in the bell-sheets. A Seeming entered in the record is, for legal purposes, true. Contracts have been voided because of it. Houses have been condemned because the street agreed they were on fire.

The power to enter a Seeming, or refuse it, is the power to decide what happened. The Watch knows this very well.

### Children

Infants born in the Vigil do not sleep. They come into the world wide-eyed and quiet, and for their first years they lie awake in their mothers' arms staring at the lamps, never crying for rest, because they do not yet need it. Vigilants call them *starers* and consider them a mercy of the god. The weight comes later, at about four years old: the Coming-Heavy, when a child first yawns, first rubs its eyes, first asks, in a small cross voice, why it cannot lie down. That is when the Vigil loses its children. A child cannot be told what the room is. A child cannot be made to understand why the warm, the soft, the dim, the quiet are deadly. There is a wing of the Dormitory called the Small Rows, where the beds are short, and the mothers who visit them do not speak to each other.

Children of the Slope who survive the Coming-Heavy are named for the bell they were born at, and many of them become nudgers by six: Pip Eleven-Bell, Rook Quarter-Bell. It is cheap work and they are good at it, sharp-eyed and quick-fingered. They do not last long on rouse.

### Death

People still die in the Vigil, of rouse-heart, fever, accident and violence. A corpse is the only thing in the city allowed to lie down, and the body is laid out flat on a board in the street for a day so that the neighbours may come and look at it, and touch its closed eyes, and envy it. The mourners are allowed to sit, for one hour, on the kerb. Then the body is carried to Lampwrights' Row and burned in the furnaces, and its light goes out into the lamps. The rich pay to have a particular lamp lit from their ashes. The poor go to the general flame.

### Festivals and Days

The Night of Lying Down, on the anniversary of the First Night, is the city's great mourning: every family that has a sleeper in the Dormitory goes to stand at the foot of the bed for an hour, and the Dormitory's doors are open from bell to bell. The Shouting Day remembers the Grey Ninth. The Pinching Fair, at midsummer, is a riot of bells, needle-dances and wake-games. And on Tablenight, the longest night of Lack, the city puts out every lamp for one minute, to prove it still can. People stand in the dark in the streets holding hands and counting aloud together, all of them, so that no one can drift off unnoticed, sixty seconds, the only darkness they will ever see. Every year a few hands go slack in the dark. Every year, when the lamps come back, someone is still counting into silence.

### Crime

Theft and violence are common and treated as ordinary. The great crimes are the crimes of rest. Selling sleep; harbouring a sleeper; failing to report one; lying down; singing the song; and the newest, which the law has not yet decided what to call: the blink-murder, the killing done by someone who was not there.

The Vigil swears on its sleepers. *By my mother's bed* is a common oath, and the bed's number is spoken after it. The heaviest oath in the city is sworn at the foot of Bed One with a hand on the rail: *by the first of us, who lay down for all of us.* A Vigilant who breaks an oath sworn on a sleeper's bed is held to have broken faith with the Counted, and no nudger will watch them again.

[box: The Lain-Down]
Every year, hundreds of Vigilants lie down on purpose. The law calls it *voluntary rest*, prosecutes the nudger for negligence, and fines the family. The city calls it *going to the room* and does not talk about it. Those left behind carry it for the rest of their lives: they go to the Dormitory and stand at the foot of the bed, and the person they loved is there, warm, breathing, unchanged, smiling, unreachable, and will be there unchanged long after they themselves have grown old and gone onto the Row. There is no grave to leave, no body to bury, no end to the grief, only a bed with a number and a face that is still, horribly, at peace. GMs should play the Lain-Down as tragedy and as pressure, never as a solution: a contact who has started talking about how warm it must be; a nudger who has stopped pinching quite so hard; a widow who visits the same bed every day and has begun to bring a stool. The god's pull is gentle, reasonable and kind. That is the horror of it.

## Factions

### The Watch of Watches

The lidless government: sleepless, paranoid, and correct to be. Its members believe that the Vigil survives only because someone is always looking, and that the someone must be a person who cannot look away. They want to hold the city together until the number can be outwitted, and they are prepared to do anything that does not require them to admit how close it is. A PC joins as an aide, eye-wetter or clerk of the Stair; only the lidless may hold a vote. Enemies: the Bedded, the Tuckers, anyone who says the number aloud. Allies: the Wakers, the Rim Road Company, the Second Table, which several Watchers secretly belong to.

### The Wakers

The police. Their job is to hurt people for their own good, and after three centuries they are very skilled. Every Waker is trained at the Rattlehouse in the Atlas of the Quick, a manual of the body's points of pain, and in the use of the rattle and the long needle: a nine-inch steel spike, ground fine, driven into the places where pain is sharpest and harm least. They believe that pain is love made practical. They want to keep the count from rising, one drowsy citizen at a time. A PC joins by apprenticeship at the Rattlehouse and a year's probation in the Slope. Enemies: the Tuckers, the Bedded, the Lessening (which they do not know exists). Allies: the Watch, the Lamplighters, every frightened household.

### The Lamplighters' Guild

The keepers of the light. They trim the ten thousand public lamps, stoke the Bonfires, polish the roof-mirrors, and burn the dead on Lampwrights' Row. Oil is the Vigil's largest import, by Rim Road from Brinehollow whale-yards and Fatlands rendering-houses, and the Guild knows exactly how many days of it remain. They believe that darkness is death, and they are right. They want oil, at any price, from any source. The source has lately become a matter for the inner Guild alone, and the tallow vats behind the furnaces on the Row are bigger than they were. A PC joins as a wick-boy or mirror-climber. Enemies: anyone who hoards oil. Allies: the Wakers, the Renderers' Union of the Fatlands, Lukas Marre, oddly, whose cell they keep brightest of all.

### The Tuckers

A criminal mercy. For a price, the Tuckers will guard you while you sleep for sixty seconds, then haul you back: they lay you on a straw pallet in a cellar, count to sixty, and drag you up by a hook-and-harness that they will not let customers examine. The method works more often than not. Their failures are delivered to the Dormitory steps before the next bell, washed and wrapped and labelled with a number from the Tuckers' own register. They believe sleep is a human right; they also believe it is a month's wages. A PC joins as a counter, the Tucker who keeps time over the sleeper. Enemies: the Wakers, the Watch. Allies: the Bedded, rouse dealers, a surprising number of Watchers who have taken their minute.

### The Lessening

## The Vigil Now

Everything is running out at once. Rouse is failing: the dose that held a generation ago now barely holds an hour, and the Apothecary-General knows the year when the dose that keeps a person awake will be the dose that stops their heart. The Dormitory has fewer than two thousand empty beds. The Lamplighter-General has ninety days of oil. The hours themselves seem to be lengthening; the Hourkeeper's pendulum swings a hair slower every week, and the city's watches feel longer than they did, and nobody can prove that they are not imagining it, because everybody is always imagining something.

The Warden-Prime has sent an envoy to Sallowreach with a proposal too terrible to put in writing: the dead do not sleep. The Lessening has drawn up its lists. The Seeming has shown eleven districts a tall figure laying a table in the street. Rouse cut with Brinehollow brine is sending the Slope's addicts walking west in their sleepless trances, toward a sea four hundred miles away. Forty-one people have killed someone they loved in a sleep of two breaths, and the inspector on the case has started to notice what the victims had in common. And in Bed One, the first sleeper, who has not stirred in three hundred years, has begun to move her lips.

### Adventure Hooks

- **The Fourth Pillow.** The Matron hires the party, off the record, to sit the night rows of the Dormitory's east wing, where three Counted have been found smothered in a month. The Matron suspects the Lessening. The Lessening suspects the Matron. The party, sitting in the breathing dark for a whole watch with the air rising and falling around them, must stay awake to catch a killer, and each time a sleeper dies, someone in the city blinks and does not come back.
- **The Brine Walkers.** Rook Quarter-Bell's customers are walking west in a shuffling column, eyes open, down the Pilgrim Road past the Sleepers' Mile. A Waker sergeant with a brother among them hires the party to bring them back before they reach the Rim, where the Company's porters have orders to stop them by any means. Somewhere on the road, the column stops, turns as one, and points.
- **The Deadwatch.** The Watch sends the party north as escort to Envoy Castellan Dray, with a chest of platters, a dozen nudgers and orders to ensure the Regent of Sallowreach says yes. On the road the nudgers begin to blink. In Lastgate, Dray asks the party privately whether they would volunteer to be the first.
- **Forty-One.** Inspector Grimmer needs outsiders. Every blink-murder victim, he has found, was a secret member of the Lessening. Somebody, or something, is defending the Counted. Grimmer wants to know what, and he wants the party to blink deliberately, under guard, to find out what it is like to be used.
- **What Aveline Says.** The Matron's notebook of Bed One's lip-words has been stolen. The thief is selling pages in the Pinchmarket. The pages contain names, and the people named on them are going to the room, one a day, in order.

## The People of the Vigil

### Warden-Prime Ilvane Stroud, the Unblinking

Ruler of the Vigil, fifty-eight years old, born lidless, and she has not shut her eyes once since birth. ### Lady Oriel Stroud

The Warden's daughter, twenty-three, born with eyelids, which in the highest Eye-line is a deformity and an embarrassment of state. She has been kept hidden in a suite of bright rooms at the top of the Stair since birth, attended by deaf nudgers. She is small, soft-voiced, dark-haired, with ordinary hazel eyes that she keeps fixed open in company until they stream, because her mother cannot bear to see her blink. Alone in her rooms, she closes her eyes for whole seconds at a time, and nothing has ever felt so good. She wants to be allowed to be ordinary, and she wants her mother to look at her once without that face. She fears she will be married off to a lidless cousin and never close her eyes again. Her secret: when she closes them, she does not see darkness. She sees the long room, from the far end, through a great grey lid, and every sleeper in it turning their head to look at her.

### Maud Sexton, the Hourkeeper

Mistress of the city's clocks, sixty, stooped, with a jeweller's loupe screwed into one eye socket and a pocket full of brass escapements that she fidgets with like rosary beads. She sets the time for every bell in the Vigil from the great pendulum in the Pendulum Hall. Over eleven years of measurements she has proved, by pendulum, sand, candle and the pulse of her own wrist, that each hour is a little longer than the last. Not by much: a breath per watch, now two. Something is stretching the night the Vigil will not admit it is having. Her report was suppressed. She has written it again, smaller, in the margins of the bell-schedules. She wants someone with authority to stand in the Pendulum Hall for an hour and feel it. Her secret: she has run the curve forward. On a night not long from now, the hour will stop ending.

### Dr. Aldous Crane, Apothecary-General

### Garron Ashlock, Lamplighter-General

### Inspector Hale Grimmer

He investigates blink-murders, killings done by ordinary people during a two-second sleep, with no memory after. He has forty-one cases. The method is the same in each: the victim laid flat on the floor, a folded cloth beneath the head, hands crossed on the chest, eyes closed under the killer's thumbs, and the killer's palms laid gently over mouth and nose until the breathing stops. Every killer comes back with no memory and the victim's teeth in their palm. Grimmer is fifty, ragged, chain-chewing rouse, with a mind that goes on working long after his body has stopped wanting to. He wants to know who is doing it. He fears that he will be next, in one direction or the other. His secret is what he found last week: all forty-one victims were members of the Lessening, down to Tobin Coyle, the Slope rope-twister, who copied Dormitory registers for them at night for beer money. Something is defending the Counted.

She visits bed 40,112 daily, and stands at its foot for an hour, and talks. She is thirty-eight, rouse-thin and quiet, with the scabbed wrists of a woman who has had no nudger but herself. The Counted do not age, but her boy has grown. He is the size of a man now, long and broad under the sheet, with a man's hands and a man's jaw and a beard coming in, and he is smiling. She wants him back. She fears what will come back. Her secret: when she leans close, he smells of bread, and his lips move along with hers when she counts.

### Benedikt Lour, the Architect

He designed the modern city: the sloped floors, the one-legged chairs, the studded walls, the endless Stair. He is seventy, frail, bent, with the elegant scrawl of a man whose hands shake for everything but a pen, and he is proud of every discomfort he has ever invented. He is now drawing a Second Dormitory. He has been told to plan for the whole population. He has drafted it on the Pillow, the only open ground left, and the drawings show four hundred thousand beds, in rows, radiating outward from the hollow like the rays of an eye. He wants to finish before he dies. He fears that the Watch will not wait. His secret: the Second Dormitory has no doors that lock from the inside, and its ventilation is designed to carry warm air.

### Rook Quarter-Bell

A rouse dealer on the Slope, twenty-two, lean, quick, wolfish, his teeth already gone, gums packed black with his own product. He cuts what he sells with bottled Brinehollow brine, bought by the cask from Dry Jonah's agents, because it is cheap and it works. His customers stay awake longer than anyone else's. They have also started bailing salt water every dawn and dusk, and walking west. There are two hundred of them now, in a slow eyes-open column. Rook wants to stop selling it and cannot, because nothing else holds. His secret: he has been taking it himself. He tastes salt every morning, and he has started standing at windows facing west.

### Envoy Castellan Dray

The Warden's ambassador to Sallowreach, fifty-five, a tall immaculate Eye-line courtier with a lidless stare and a chest of documents he never lets out of sight, travelling with twelve paid nudgers and a cask of Bitterhouse rouse. His commission is to buy the secret of Keeping, so that the Vigil's citizens can be killed and kept upright: a city of the dead, who never need to sleep. He has been in Lastgate for two months, negotiating with the Regent. He wants to save his city. He fears that the price will be paid in its people. His secret: he has learned that the Kept cannot leave Sallowreach, and that the Vigil would have to move there, all of it, to die; and he has not yet told the Warden, because he thinks she will say yes.

### Aveline Morne, the Sleeper in Bed One

A baker's daughter, nineteen years old forever, who lay down on the First Night in 341 and was the first not to rise. She has lain unmoving for three hundred years under a white sheet in a bed with a brass rail, a plump fair girl with flour still in the creases of her knuckles, cheeks pink, lips slightly parted, breathing with the rows. Last month her lips began to shape words. The Matron has started writing them down. Most of them are names, one a day, and each person named has gone to the room the next day. One, last week, was a sentence: *There is someone at the door who has not been fed. Set another place.*

## Playing a Vigilant

> You will never rest. Not once, not for a breath, not until you are on the Row. Now go and do something with it.
>> — what Slope mothers say to a child on the morning of the Coming-Heavy

A Vigilant has been awake since the age of four. Every hour of every day of their life is still behind their eyes. They are sharp-sighted, suspicious, quick to see a lie and quicker to see a yawn, and they are carrying a weight that no one from any other land can imagine. They have a nudger, or they had one. They know someone in the Dormitory. They do not trust their own memory, and they are right not to.

**Attribute bonus:** +1 EYE.

### Weariness

Every character in the Vigil, native or foreign, tracks **Weariness** from 0 to 10. A foreigner who has slept well arrives at 0. A Vigilant has never slept and can never fall below **2**: that is the floor, the silt of a lifetime.

**Gaining Weariness.** +1 for each watch (eight hours) spent without a dose of rouse. +1 for each scene of comfort: warmth, softness, a full meal, rain on a roof, quiet, a long dull wait, a lullaby, a sermon. Yawn days double all gains. A contagious yawn in earshot calls for a Caul + Resolve roll (Routine, or Hard from a Glass House yawner); failure adds +1. A Tooth that bites back (see below) adds +1.

**Shedding Weariness.** A dose of rouse removes 2 Weariness and prevents gains from the watch's passing for that watch; a day's rouse costs 2 lacks on the Height and half that, cut, on the Slope. A second dose in the same watch requires a Gut + Endure roll (Hard; Grim for a third); failure costs 2 Flesh, and a Lack is a rouse-heart: the character suffers Ruin. A pin, slap or pinch from a nudger removes 1 Weariness, once a scene. A Waker's long needle removes 2 and costs 1 Flesh. Foreigners sleeping outside the Vigil's borders clear Weariness to 0. Vigilants have nowhere to sleep.

| Weariness | State | Effect |
| 0–2 | Fresh | As fresh as anyone on the Lid. |
| 3–4 | Heavy | −1 on all Eye and Lung rolls. |
| 5–6 | Drifting | −2 on Eye and Lung rolls. The Seeming begins. Blink checks on triggers. |
| 7–8 | Grey | −2 on all rolls. Blink check every scene, and on triggers. Dread checks one step harder. |
| 9 | Going Under | Blink check every few minutes of play. |
| 10 | Asleep | The character falls asleep (see below). |

**Falling asleep.** At Weariness 10 the character sleeps, and goes to the room. A nudger has one breath to fetch them: a Hand + Stitching or Tongue + Intimidate roll at Hard (a Waker's needle succeeds automatically if it is already drawn). If that fails, the nudger may try once more, at Grim, in the next breath. After that the character is Counted. There is no further roll. Foreigners and Vigilants alike are carried to the Dormitory and given a number.

### Blinks

A **blink check** is Caul + Resolve against a Difficulty set by Weariness: Routine at 5–6, Hard at 7–8, Grim at 9. Characters in the Appetite or beyond (Regrowth 4+) make blink checks on triggers even below Weariness 5, at Routine. Triggers are anything that would make a tired person drift: warmth, softness, silence, monotony, a yawn, lying down, a voice that murmurs, watching the Dormitory breathe.

On a failure the character blinks, and the GM rolls on the table below. Each blink also adds 1 Fray, for the lost time, whether or not the character learns what happened. On a Lack, the GM rolls twice and uses both results, and if the character sees what they did, they make a Dread check at the rating the GM judges right (usually 2 or 3).

| d10 | The Blink |
| 1–4 | A second gone. You lose the last thing said, and drop what you were holding. |
| 5–6 | Moved. You are up to ten paces from where you stood, facing another way. |
| 7 | You said something. The GM speaks one line in your voice, often a true thing you wanted kept. |
| 8 | Your hands did something small: a lamp put out, a door unbarred, a word written. |
| 9 | The room held you longer: d6 minutes, standing. You remember the warmth and nothing else. |
| 10 | Tucked. The most vulnerable person near you is lying down, eyes closed, and your hands are on them. Roll d6: on 1–4 you came back in time. |

[box: GM Advice — Did I Blink?]
Blinks are a tool for gaslighting the players, gently and fairly. Once a character is Drifting or worse, the GM may tell a player that something has changed, a door now open, a companion now across the room, a cup now empty, and decline to say whether it is a blink or a Seeming or the truth. A character may make an Eye + Reckoning roll (Routine at Drifting, Hard at Grey) to trust a memory; on a failure, they cannot be sure, and NPCs who say otherwise are as likely to be right. Never use this to cheat players out of earned success. Use it to make them look at one another and ask, quietly, *did you see me move?*
[/box]

### The Seeming

At Weariness 5 and above, the GM gives characters private hallucinations: voices in the lamps, insects in the plaster, the dead in the lane. Shared Seemings, which a whole street sees, are real enough for law in the Vigil whether or not they are real at all. An Eye + Search roll at Hard tells a character whether a Seeming leaves traces (warm cobbles, a crumb, a place card). A character who Breaks in the Vigil most often takes a Derangement of the Seeming: that a loved one has been replaced by a sleeper's dream of them; that they themselves are asleep in a bed and dreaming all of this; that the room is real and the city is the dream.

### The Sop: the Lull

Iss is quieted by rest, and in the Vigil rest is a crime. The **Lull** is the act of closing your eyes on purpose for a count of ten while a nudger holds your hand. It removes 1d6 Hunger. It feeds the Want to lie down. The character must immediately make a blink check at Hard, whatever their Weariness; on a failure they are in the room for the count, and their nudger must fetch them back as if they had fallen asleep. Every Vigilant knows someone who took the Lull once too often.

### The Tooth

When a Vigilant Partakes, their pupils blow wide and black, the world slows and softens at its edges, sounds come as if through a wall, and for a heartbeat they see the scene as if from very high up and very far away, every detail at once. Everyone nearby yawns. The air smells of clean linen. When a Tooth shows a 1 and the god bites back, the character takes the extra Hunger and +1 Weariness as well, and their next blink check is one step harder.

### Gifts

**The Taste (Regrowth 1–3).** Choose one. *The Catching Yawn:* Partake and yawn deliberately; everyone in earshot with Weariness 3+ makes a Caul + Resolve roll at Hard or gains 2 Weariness and loses their next action to drowsiness. You gain 1 Weariness yourself. *Heavy-Eyed:* you always know when someone in sight is about to blink, gaining +2 to Instinct and Search against blinkers, and you know when someone lies about having blinked.

**The Appetite (Regrowth 4–6).** Choose one. *Lost Seconds:* Partake and blink on purpose; for 1d6 seconds you are gone, and you reappear up to ten paces away anywhere you could have walked. Nobody present remembers seeing you move. Roll on the Blink table afterwards, rerolling results of 1–6. *The Long Look:* blink under a nudger's guard and ask the GM one question about a Counted sleeper or anyone currently blinking in the city; gain 2 Weariness and 1 Fray.

**The Course (Regrowth 7–9).** *Lidless.* Your lids shrink and are gone. You gain +2 to Search and can never be surprised by anything in your line of sight. You remember everything you have seen. Dread checks against anything before your eyes are one step harder, because you cannot look away. Your eyes must be wetted every half minute; without it you lose 1 Flesh an hour and are blind within a day. And the great privilege of the Eye-lines is yours: a lidless character who reaches Weariness 10 does not sleep. Instead they Break, at once, and their Weariness drops to 7. This is not mercy. It is how the Eye-lines rule.

**The Brink (Regrowth 10–11).** *Lay Them Down.* Partake and speak softly to one person with Weariness 5+ who can hear you; they make a Caul + Resolve roll at Grim or fall asleep, and go to the room. Each use forces a Dread 3 check on you. While you are at the Brink, everyone who spends a scene in the same room as you gains 1 Weariness, and restless sleepers turn their faces toward you.

### The Wants

- **To lie down.** Triggered by beds, softness, warmth, a body lying peacefully, the Pillow. Resist with Caul + Resolve at Hard; on a failure, the character presses against the comfort for a minute and makes a blink check.
- **To count.** Triggered by rows, crowds, sleepers and fear. Resist at Routine; on a failure, the character must count the thing aloud to the end before acting.
- **To watch.** Triggered by a sleeping or drowsing person. Resist at Hard; on a failure, the character cannot leave that person unwatched for the scene.
- **To tuck in** (the Brink only). Triggered by a weeping, wounded or frightened person. Resist at Grim; on a failure, the character tries to make them lie down and rest.

### The Seating

At Regrowth 12 the character falls asleep, one last time, smiling, and no nudger on the Table can fetch them. They are carried to the Dormitory and given a number. Their player should take a moment with that. Later, perhaps much later, the GM may tell the surviving characters that a sleeper in a certain bed has become restless. Lips first. The god has been furnishing the character from the inside, and when it speaks, it will use their voice, and it will know their friends' names, and it will be very glad to see them.

### Background Concepts

- **Slope Nudger.** Born at a bell, pinching strangers for crumbs by six, you know every rouse-cut on the Slope and every face that is about to go down. You have outlived three clients.
- **Washed-Out Waker.** You did your week in Sister Pinch's standing-stall, and your year on the beat, and then you put the needle down one night and could not pick it up. You still know the Atlas. You still hear the screams as thanks.
- **Eye-line Spare.** A younger child of a lidless house, born with eyelids, or born lidless and too far down the line to matter. You have been educated, watched, and kept out of sight, and you have never once been allowed to look away.
