RECORD OF A LAWFUL DISSOLUTION, kept by the undersigned clerk of the Bench of Clauses, in the Ninth Room beneath the Ledger, on the eleventh day of Carving, 640 A.G. The condemned: Aubin Sele, cooper, convicted of selling the same cask twice under oath. Present: the condemned, the Breaker Jude Crake, the clerk (bitted, writing only), one Surety-witness (bitted). The hour-glass is turned at the first word.
CRAKE: Good evening, Master Sele. No, sit, the chair is for you. I brought figs. You needn't eat them. I only thought the room wanted something sweet in it.
SELE: (says nothing for the space of nine grains)
CRAKE: You've been told what I am. That's all right. People always have. I'm not going to touch you, and nothing in this room is sharp, and the door is not locked. You may check it. (The condemned does not check it.) I'm told you were a cooper. My father bent staves. I remember the smell of the steaming shed, the oak going soft. Is it true you can hear a bad cask?
SELE: If it's dry. If you knock it. (He stops himself. He has said nothing binding. The clerk notes his relief, visible in the shoulders.)
CRAKE: That's lovely. A ringing note, I'd guess, and a dead one where the split is. (Some minutes on staves, pitch, his wife's cooking, the late rain. The condemned laughs once, at a story concerning a goat. The clerk notes that it is a good story.)
CRAKE: May I ask you something small? Your daughter. Wenna, isn't it? She's to be fostered with your sister in Low Dray. Would you like her told anything?
SELE: Tell her (stops) Tell her, if you're able, that her father thought of her.
CRAKE: That's beautifully put. The "if you're able" is mine, not yours, so it costs you nothing. You're careful. I admire careful. (The glass is at its quarter.) You're shaking. Are you cold?
SELE: No.
(The clerk notes that the condemned is shaking, and that the room is warm, and that "No" in answer to a question of present fact is an assertion. A small sound, like a knuckle cracking, from the condemned's jaw. A thin line of blood at the corner of the mouth. The condemned understands. He weeps. He does not cry out, which would be foolish.)
CRAKE: Oh, Aubin. I'm so sorry. It's only a little lie. Everyone tells it. Here, the cloth. You'll want to hold it under. Let's not talk about cold. Let's talk about Wenna. She'll be frightened of me when she's older, I expect. They'll tell her stories. Will you do one thing for me, so I can tell her you were brave?
SELE: (nothing for a long time; the glass is at its half) What thing.
CRAKE: Only stop crying before the glass runs out. That's all. So I can tell her.
SELE: I (stops) I'll try.
CRAKE: That will do.
(The clerk notes that "I'll try" is an oath of effort, the lightest there is, and that it is kept by trying. The condemned tries. He breathes slowly. He looks at the figs. The clerk notes that at the three-quarter mark he has very nearly stopped weeping, and that the Breaker is telling him, softly, about a girl he once saw in Low Dray who had red ribbon on her bit, and that perhaps it was Wenna, and that she looked well, and that she was holding the hand of a man who was not her uncle. The condemned asks what man. The Breaker says he didn't see the face. The condemned asks again. The Breaker says it was probably no one. The condemned begins to weep again.)
CRAKE: Aubin. Aubin. You promised you'd try.
SELE: I am trying, I am, I swear I am
(The clerk records that the condemned has sworn to a present fact, which was true, and that the true oath holds. The clerk records that the condemned then stopped trying, because he was thinking only of the hand that was not his daughter's uncle's, and that a man cannot try and not try in the same breath, and that the god heard which one it was.)
(At this point the record becomes difficult to keep. The clerk sets down only what is required. The first Breaking was the tongue's, for the lie about the cold, and it worsened, the jaw separating at its hinge with the sound of a green branch. The second was apt to "I'll try" and opened him along the effort: the long muscles of both arms and the back, which split lengthwise like staves without a hoop. The third was apt to "I swear I am": the chest, at the breastbone, so that the clerk could see the condemned's heart moving and observe that it was trying very hard. None of these was fatal. The condemned remained aware throughout. The Breaker held his hand.)
(The glass ran out at the forty-first grain past the hour's mark. The condemned had ceased by then. It is the Breaker's first hour to exceed the hour. He asked that this be noted. He asked that it be noted also that he did not see any man in Low Dray, and that Wenna Sele is well, and that he lied about it, and that the lie cost him the nail of the smallest finger of his left hand, which came away in the cloth, and which he left with the figs.)
Sealed in silence. Witnessed. Filed.

## The Meal

### What Tolm Was

Before the Gorging, Tolm, the Witness, sat at the head of the Gullet, the deepest gorge in the country, and listened. It was the size of a hill, shaped like a seated person drawn by someone who had only heard one described, with a bowed head as long as a barn and ears without number: great folded ones like bracket fungus on a dead oak, and small ones down its throat and the insides of its arms, opening and closing like the gills of fish. Its mouth was small and always closed. Its tongue, the chronicles say, was the color of raw liver and as long as a street.

Tolm did not command and did not bless. It heard. A vow spoken in its presence became a fact the way a stone dropped becomes a fall. Marriages were made at its knee; boundaries were walked in its hearing; a liar standing before it found the lie had been true all along, or found himself face down in the red dust with blood coming out of his ears. The people of the canyons loved it the way a court loves a scrupulous judge: with fear, with gratitude, and with the steady low resentment of those who can never quite be off the record.

### The Silent Supper

In the ninth winter of the Lack, the canyon people came to the Gullet with knives, and they came in silence, because they knew exactly what would happen if they did not. Anything said in front of the Witness would count. A cry of pain would become a sentence. A prayer would become a contract. "Forgive us" would become a debt. So they did not speak. They had spent a month practising, and the practice had a cost: a generation of hand-signs for butchery, a set of whistles agreed and then forbidden, the dry-throated discipline of a whole people learning to hold their tongues.

They began at dusk. The old account, carved on the Ledger in the year 12 A.G., records that Tolm did not move, but that every ear on its body turned toward the knives as they came, the great ones and the little ones, a sound like ten thousand pages turning at once. The first incisions were made at the throat, where the flesh was softest. What came out was not blood but something thick and clear that smelled of wet slate and tasted, those who caught it on their lips said afterward in handtalk, of the moment after you have said a thing you cannot take back.

They worked for three nights. The god's skin was tough as harness leather, lined with a fat that mouthed the shapes of words when cut. Butchers wore wax in their ears and still came away with nosebleeds. The ears were taken first, because no one could bear to cut while they listened, and laid in rows on the canyon floor, where they went on twitching toward every footstep for a day and a half. Someone stepped on one. The sound it made was the sound of the person who had stepped on it, and it went up the canyon walls and came back, and that was the first echo anyone in Oathen was afraid of.

On the second night a woman named Deverel Ashe slipped in the slick and laid her forearm open to the bone; she knelt with her teeth clamped on her sleeve until her jaw cracked, and made no sound. Near the fires, a small child woke in its mother's arms and began to laugh at the flames. The carving says only that the mother held the child close for a long time, until it was quiet; that those nearest saw and did not stop her; that she sat with the bundle in her lap until dawn and then went back to work. Her name is not on the Ledger. Her line is: the Hushwell Tongue-line, among the highest in the land, whose crest is a closed hand over an open mouth.

The tongue was taken on the third night. It took forty people to draw it out from behind the teeth, hand over hand like a hawser, and as it came Tolm's mouth opened for the first and last time anyone remembered. The god spoke once. Every person present heard it in a different voice, the voice of whoever had first taught them to talk, and every account gives the same two sentences: "Go on. I am listening."

They went on. The families who ate the tongue, raw, in slices that kept curling in their hands, became the Tongue-lines, the nobility of Oathen. The ears were boiled and eaten by the canyon clans of the upper gorges, whose descendants are said to hear too well; the great slow heart went to the Rain-keepers of the high basin; the scraps and the clear fluid went to the poor, who licked it off the stone and were Scraplings ever after. The bones were rolled into the Gullet and are there still, under a floor of red sand, and the canyon above them is the quietest place in a quiet country.

In the morning the Lack ended, as it ended everywhere. In Oathen, the first thing anyone said aloud was said by a herdsman's son who found green shoots on the canyon rim and shouted, "It's over, I swear it's over!" It was. The boy's word was the first oath of the Long Grace, and it was kept. He lived to be ninety and never said anything again with quite that much confidence.

[box: The Two Sentences]
Theologians on the Bench of Clauses have spent six centuries arguing the grammar of Tolm's last words. "Go on" is permission, or command, or encouragement, or a sigh. "I am listening" is a statement of present fact, and the god of true speech could not make a false one, which means that at the moment it was being eaten it was listening, and the question that has never been settled is when, if ever, it stopped. The Theft doctrine reads it as a sentence: we are watched until we pay. The Gift doctrine reads it as forgiveness: go on, eat, live. The Bait doctrine, illegal in Oathen since 290 A.G., reads it the plainest way of all. The god said it was listening. It has not said that it has stopped.
[/box]

## The Rule of the Land

### Words Bind Flesh

In Oathen, a promise is a physical fact. Keep it and nothing happens. Break it and the body breaks to match: at once, with a sound, in a shape that fits the word. A man who swore to stand by his brother and ran had both legs fold under him backwards, the knees bending the wrong way like a heron's, and he lay in the road with his brother walking on without him. A woman who promised her heart to one man and gave it to another was found with her chest opened like a cupboard, the ribs swung outward on their cartilage like doors, the heart inside beating, entirely visible, entirely hers.

The Breaking is always apt and never by itself fatal. Tolm was a witness, not an executioner. What the god does to the forsworn is not punishment so much as testimony: it makes the body tell the truth that the mouth failed to. The broken live on, often for decades, as the Forsworn, and you can read a person's moral history from across the street. A merchant with no hands cheated someone. A woman whose lips have split back to the ears told a lie that mattered. A soldier with a spine bent like a hook swore to bear something and set it down. The canyons are full of these histories walking.

It does not have to be solemn. "I'll be back before dark" is an oath. "I'd die for you" is an oath. "I'll never forgive you" is an oath, and the man who swore it to his father and then, at the old man's deathbed, wept and took his hand, had his own hand turn to the consistency of wet bread in his father's grip. A child's "I promise" binds exactly as hard as a king's. The god does not weigh station. It weighs meaning.

### What Counts

The Bench of Clauses has spent six hundred years defining what Tolm counts, and its definitions fill the four miles of the Ledger and a library besides. In practice, the canyon folk know the rule in their bones, because the ones who did not know it are no longer whole:

- A promise binds. Any statement that commits the speaker to a future act, a future state, or an abstention ("I will", "I won't", "I'll be there", "never", "always", "you can count on me").
- A lie breaks at once. A spoken assertion the speaker believes to be false is a broken oath the instant it is finished. The Breaking for a lie is nearly always the mouth: the lips, the tongue, the jaw, the teeth.
- A boast is a promise. "I could carry that alone" is heard as "I will carry it if asked." "Nothing could frighten me" is heard as a vow of courage, and the next thing that frightens the speaker will cost them.
- Meaning outweighs grammar. A sentence built to sound like a question but meant as a promise binds as a promise. Tolm heard what people meant, and it still does.
- Ignorance is no defense. An honest mistake about a fact (you said the well was dry, and you believed it, and it was not) is not a lie, but it is still a statement, and how the god will weigh it is a matter of furious debate. Most Oatheners do not risk it. They say "I believe the well is dry," which is true about them, rather than "the well is dry," which is a wager about the world.

### How Oatheners Speak

So Oatheners do not chat. They speak rarely, slowly and in conditionals, and they never, ever exaggerate. A polite sentence is mostly exits: "If able." "As I now intend." "Barring death, illness, or weather." "Unless the Bench rules otherwise." "In so far as I can see." A shopkeeper asked whether the bread is fresh says, "It was baked this morning, I am told." A mother asked by her child if she will come back says, "That is my wish," and the child, who is bitted and cannot reply, learns from the sentence everything the mother cannot promise.

Foreigners on the Rim Road find Oatheners cold and evasive. Oatheners find foreigners obscene: people who say "I'll kill you" in jest and "I'll love you forever" at the end of an evening, and walk away whole. To an Oathener a talkative stranger is a man juggling knives in a nursery.

[box: GM Advice — Running the Rule at the Table]
Oathen changes how players talk, and that is the point. Make it clear before play that in-character speech in Oathen is mechanically live: what a character says aloud is what the character has said. You are not trying to trap the players with grammar; you are letting them feel what it is to live in a country where every sentence might be a contract. Be generous in the first session. When a player says something binding by accident, pause and ask, "Is that what your character says, in those words?" Once they have felt the first Breaking land on someone else, stop asking. The best Oathen sessions are quiet ones, with long pauses before anyone answers a question, and players writing their character's sentences down before they say them.
[/box]

### The Witness Is Inside

Tolm was the god who listened. It is still listening, from the inside: from the blood of those who ate it, from the rock over its bones, from canyons that throw every word back. The Rule is not a law of nature but the attention of a god, and attention can sharpen and wander. It has begun to count things it never used to count: handtalk, a scribe's chisel, perhaps, for the highest bloodlines, the silent words a person tells themselves at night. And because it is listening, it can be spoken to, which is what makes blasphemy in Oathen so dangerous (see Crime and Punishment).

And because it is listening, it can be spoken to. Blasphemy in Oathen is not an abstract offence. To curse Tolm aloud is to address a god that is present in your own throat, and the Bench's records hold four hundred years of cases of what happens to people who do. Most of them went quiet afterward. Not all of them by choice.

## The Regrowth in Oathen

Tolm is growing back, and what grows back is a witness: an ear that hears too well, a tongue that says too truly. In the Tongue-lines it grows fastest. The Bench of Clauses is the most godsign-ridden body of people in the country, and the more powerful a family, the more carefully it chooses who is allowed to speak.

### The Taste

It begins with the echo. You say something and, half a second later, you hear it again in your own voice, close behind your ear, slightly flatter, like someone reading it back for the record. At first it happens only in quiet rooms. Then everywhere. Children at the Taste whisper into cupped hands to hear whether it comes back. Adults learn to wait for it, and to listen to what they said with the god's attention, and to wince.

Other tells follow: a taste of wet slate when a promise is made nearby; the ears ringing faintly whenever someone in the room lies; a habit of mouthing other people's words along with them. Tongue-line children at the Taste are bitted early and watched closely, because the Taste is not dangerous, and the Appetite is.

### The Appetite

The compulsion to swear. It comes on like a sneeze: a pressure in the throat, a heat behind the teeth, and then the words are out, vows on nothing, on everything. "I will walk to the sea." "I will never eat bread again." "I will have your eyes." Sufferers wake in the night having sworn in their sleep and lie rigid, trying to remember what they said, while the one who shares their bed lies equally rigid, trying not to have heard it.

The Appetite-struck must be gagged for their own safety, and in Oathen this is no metaphor. The iron bit of the Appetite is a heavy thing, hinged at the jaw, with a flat plate that presses the tongue down and a small grille for breath and broth. Families keep their swearers at home, bitted, hands bound when the urge is strong, because a determined swearer will try to sign or scratch vows into the plaster. The Murmuring House in Tacit holds three hundred of them, in rows, and the sound the place makes, three hundred people trying to promise through iron, is where it gets its name.

### The Course

At the Course, speech stops needing to be a promise to bind. Plain statements begin to come true. "The door is shut," said of an open door, shuts it, or shuts something. "It's cold," said on a warm day, brings frost to the speaker's lashes and nobody else's. Figures of speech become fatal: a man who said he was starving was dead by evening, in a full pantry, his stomach shrunk to the size of a walnut. A Tongue-line girl who told her sister "you look like death" was a mourner within the week.

The body changes with it. The tongue thickens, darkens and lengthens until it rests against the bit even in repose, mottled the color of raw liver. A second voice develops beneath the first, a half-step lower, and sometimes it says the words a moment before the speaker does. The small ears come: puckers in the skin of the neck and shoulders and the inner arm, at first like scars from a pox, then opening, pink and folded and perfectly formed, turning toward sound. Course-sufferers cannot bear crowds. Too much is said in them, and all of it is heard.

### The Brink

At the Brink, the gap between saying and being narrows to nothing. A Brink-sufferer's ordinary speech reshapes the room they stand in: a careless "of course" makes a thing inevitable; a sigh of "everything's falling apart" has, on record, brought down a house. The bit is no longer optional. Brink Tongue-lines are taken from their families by the Bench and fitted with silver bits, and their hands are kept gloved and occupied, and they are not permitted pen or chisel. They go mad, often, because a mind full of things it may not say is a mind with no door out, and in Oathen a Break at the Brink is a public danger: a madman whose delusions are binding.

### The Seating: the Sayers

A Tongue-line in the last stage does not describe the world. What they say, is. They are called the Sayers, and they are the closest thing the canyons have to Tolm walking again: not the god's ear but its mouth, the place where its witness turns into its word. The first was a girl in the Hushwell house who said "I wish it would stop" during a storm in 598 A.G. It stopped. So did the wind, the river, the birds, and the hearts of everything in the valley of Low Shale, which is still there, intact, with nothing in it moving.

The few Sayers alive are kept bitted in gold, which is the one metal Tolm's mouth will not bite through, and locked in the Gilded Cells beneath the Ledger. They are unmuzzled rarely, on the Bench's vote, to say a single sentence the Clausewrights have drafted for months. The Bench calls this governance. The Sayers do not call it anything. Their faces, behind the gold, have grown long and smooth, and their eyes have closed over with skin, and the small ears have covered them like moss.

[box: The Sayer's Gold]
A Sayer's bit is a work of terrible craft: soft gold over a core of lead, cast to the exact shape of the mouth, with a tongue-plate that has to be resized every few months as the tongue grows. The Bitmaker Garl Tome fits them himself. The gold must be pure. A bit cut with copper was found, in 611 A.G., to have been bitten clean through overnight, and the Sayer it held had said one word into the dark of the Cells before the guards reached her. No one knows what the word was. The Gilded Cells were walled off for six weeks afterward, and the guard who came out first had no ears, and smiled.
[/box]

## Land and Weather

Oathen is dry red canyon country, deep and narrow, under a hard white sky. The plateau above is scrub and scoured stone; the life is below, in the cracks. Rivers run in the canyon floors, thin and green and fast, and the towns are cut into the cliff walls in tiers: dwellings, granaries, shrines and tombs, connected by ladders, rope-lifts and narrow stairs that wind up the rock like the thread of a screw. It is hot, still and extremely quiet. Sandals are soled with felt. Doors are hung on leather. Cart wheels are wrapped in rag. You can stand on a balcony in Tacit and hear a woman across the canyon draw water.

The great danger is echo. An oath repeated is an oath renewed, and a canyon will repeat anything. A man who swore in the narrows of the Sheath to pay a debt by the new Plate heard it come back nine times, and when he failed, he broke nine times, each Breaking landing on the last. Whole gorges are posted as silent, with stone hands carved at their mouths, palm out. Wind is feared for the same reason: a vow overheard is still a vow, and when the dry Carving wind called the Tattler comes up from the south, families sit in their inner rooms in silence until it drops.

It rains once a year. Before it died, Tolm promised the canyons rain on the first day of Grace, and a god's word outlives the god. The Kept Rain has fallen on that day for six hundred and forty years: a single day's downpour, warm and heavy, that fills the cisterns, floods the canyon floors, and drowns the careless. The whole country lives on that day's water. This year it came three days late.

### Places in Oathen

#### Tacit

The capital, carved into both walls of the deepest canyon. Half a million people, and you can hear a sandal drop. The Sunward and Shadeward Walls face each other across two hundred rope bridges; the Tongue-lines live high on the Sunward side, and the Bench sits at its heart in the Hall of Conditions, a felt-lined chamber floored with sand so that nothing said there can echo. Markets run on handtalk and tally-sticks. The city smells of hot stone, goat, and the cedar oil the rich rub into their bits.

#### The Breakdowns

The slum of the Forsworn, on the canyon floor below Tacit, where the river runs green and the sun reaches for two hours a day. Here live the broken: men without hands, women with mouths opened back to the ear, people walking on legs that bend the wrong way, people whose ribs stand open under oiled cloth. It is the loudest place in Oathen, because the people in it have nothing left to lose. They swear constantly, defiantly, on broken parts that cannot break again. They sing. They mock the Walls overhead. Their lanes are named for the oaths their founders broke: Fair Weight Row, Back-Before-Dark, the Never Alley.

#### Sworn Gorge

A sealed ravine east of Tacit where an oath shouted in 419 A.G. has never stopped echoing. Anyone who hears it is bound by it. Nobody knows any longer what it says. The mouth of the gorge is walled with dressed stone forty feet high, and the wall is packed behind with wool, and still, on the stillest nights, a man pressing his ear to it can hear a murmur in the rock like a crowd a mile off, rising and falling. The Echo-Wardens keep a post at the wall. They wear wax and lead plugs in their ears and communicate by touch, and they rotate every nine days, because the murmur gets into you anyway.

#### The Ledger

A cliff face four miles long into which every vow of public record has been carved: treaties, marriages, sentences, Rim Road contracts, the oaths of kings. The oldest carvings at its western end are worn nearly smooth; the newest, at the eastern end, are still pale with fresh dust. Scaffolds hang from the cliff top, and the carvers work on them in all weathers, in silence. Behind the face, inside the rock, lies a warren of record-rooms and copying halls, and below that, the Ninth Room, where executions are conducted, and below that, the Gilded Cells.

#### The Gilded Cells

Where the Sayers are kept: a vault of nine chambers cut deep under the Ledger and lined in beaten lead and felt, with doors of gold-faced iron. Three of the cells are occupied. The guards are bitted and deafened by surgery. A Clausewright of the Bench visits once a week to inspect the bits and does not stay long.

#### The Gullet (new)

The gorge where Tolm was eaten, at the head of the river system west of Tacit. Its floor is deep red sand over the god's bones, and it is the quietest place in Oathen: sound does not carry here, but sinks, as if into a pillow. Pilgrims walk the Gullet barefoot on Tablenight eve. Those who put their ear to the sand hear, they say, a slow sound like breathing; the Bench rules that what they hear is the river. The sand is hot all year, and it is getting warmer.

#### The Unmade Road (new)

A switchbacked track down from Wending Pass, in the high eastern canyons, where Captain Renn Hold's regiment came apart in 627 A.G. Four hundred soldiers swore to hold the pass to the death, obeyed an order to retreat, and broke on the road behind their captain. Their remains were never cleared. The bone along the road is still sorted, roughly, into the shapes of men lying where they fell, and the drovers who use the road walk their animals in single file down the center of it. Renn Hold is still at the top of the pass. He has to be.

#### The Murmuring House (new)

A hospital and asylum on the Shadeward Wall of Tacit for the Appetite-struck and the Brink-mad who have no family to keep them. Three hundred patients, bitted in iron, in long low wards. The nurses work with wax in their ears and feed their charges thin broth through the grille of the bit. The sound of the place is a ceaseless, thick, wet humming: three hundred vows attempted and not finished.

#### The Witnessing Vaults (new)

The catacombs beneath Tacit, where the dead stand upright in niches, faces outward, to witness. Families come here to swear on the dead, and the Vaults' keepers, the Keeners, are the only people in Oathen who have heard the dead speak (see Oaths on the Dead).

#### Quillhouse (new)

The Rim Road Company's notarial house on the Oathen stretch of the Rim, three days' ride north of Tacit. A fortress of pale stone, gated, garrisoned by Company guards, and home to Jessamy Quill. Every Company contract on the Table is carried here by courier, read aloud before her, and sworn. The waiting-hall is always full: Fatlands renderers, Brinehollow admirals' factors, Vigil oil-buyers, all made to sit in silence in a long felted room while an Oathen clerk explains, in handtalk and on slate, that anything they say from here on counts.

#### Low Shale (new)

The valley the First Sayer stopped in 598 A.G. Everything in it stopped: water standing in the riverbed in a single hard green ribbon that will not run if you cup it, birds hanging in the air a foot above the ground, a herdsman mid-stride with his crook raised, his eyes wet and open after forty-three years. Nothing rots. Nothing moves. The air is warm and has no smell at all. The Bench has posted it as silent and forbids entry. Pilgrims of the Bait doctrine sneak in to leave written prayers at the herdsman's feet. The herdsman's eyes have been seen to follow them.

### The Honest Age

For two centuries after the Supper, Oathen was the conscience of the Table. Other lands discovered they had swallowed monstrous gifts; Oathen discovered it had swallowed honesty, and sold it. A treaty sworn in Tacit could not be broken without the signatories' bodies breaking too; a merchant house that swore its weights at the Ledger could be trusted to the crumb. Oathen grew rich on notarizing the world. The canyon cities filled with foreign envoys learning to keep quiet.

It was not a kind age. Children broke constantly on promises they did not understand, and the cemeteries of the period are full of small, oddly shaped bones. In 215 A.G. the Bench passed the Muzzling, and the canyons have been quiet in a new way since: the silence of the nursery.

### The Mute Succession

The old kings spoke, and every word bound them. By 330 A.G. King Orrel the Fourth had sworn so many coronation oaths and treaty-clauses that he ruled his last years from a litter, his body folded into what his physicians called an exact map of his reign's contradictions. Thereafter the crown passed only to children who had never spoken. Nine such kings have reigned, and all died with their silence intact.

### The Oath in Sworn Gorge

In the drought summer of 419 A.G., something was shouted in a narrow ravine east of Tacit, and the ravine has never stopped saying it. The records of that year are incomplete: a page is cut from the Bench's minutes and a stretch of the Ledger has been chiselled smooth, very carefully, in a way that took weeks. What is known is that the people of the hamlet of Sowe, at the gorge's mouth, were all found broken the following spring, every one of them, in the same way, and that the shape of the Breaking was so terrible that the Echo-Wardens' charter forbids its description. The gorge was walled. The murmur goes on.

### Wending Pass

In 627 A.G. Reckoners out of the Fast came through the eastern canyons, and the four hundred soldiers of the Fourth Regiment of the Canyon Watch swore, in the old form, to hold Wending Pass to the death. Captain Renn Hold looked at the raiders and at his men, most of them nineteen, and ordered them to fall back. He kept his own oath by staying. They broke theirs by obeying. The sound came down the road like a rockfall, four hundred Breakings in a breath, each soldier opening at every joint by which he had bound himself to the place. They did not die of it, not at once. The Reckoners found the captain alone at the top with his sword drawn, looked at the road below him, and went back the way they had come.

## Rule, Law and Politics

Oathen is ruled by the Mute Kings, chosen in infancy, bitted for life, and raised to rule by gesture. The present king, Ket the Ninth, has never made a sound. Beneath him sits the Bench of Clauses, forty-one lawyer-priests called Clausewrights, whose art is the drafting of promises that can survive contact with reality. In theory the Bench interprets the king. In practice the king nods, and the Bench spends a month in forty-page conditionals deciding what the nod meant, and then does what it would have done anyway.

There are no prisons. There is no need. A convicted person is made to swear the terms of their own sentence: "I will not leave the district of Low Dray for nine years." "I will pay the widow one lack a day until my death." "I will not speak again." Breach the sentence and the body enforces it. The Bench's chief tool of government is therefore not the sword but the clause, and its great fear is the sentence badly drafted, which leaves a convict free, or the sentence too well drafted, which kills him in a way the court did not intend. For the gravest crimes there is the Breaker, Jude Crake, who carries out death sentences with nothing but conversation.

Trials are simple. In a Breaking trial, presided over by Judge Thane Urrow, there is no evidence and no testimony. The accused repeats the promise they are alleged to have broken, aloud, in its original words, and the court watches. If they kept it, nothing happens. If they broke it, it breaks them again, now, in front of everyone. For crimes that are not oaths (theft, murder, arson), the accused is asked a single question: "Did you do it?" and must answer. A Forsworn who has nothing left to break in the mouth is a problem the Bench has never solved, which is why the Breakdowns are, to Tacit's lasting horror, the one place in Oathen where a person can lie.

Oathen's power abroad is trust. Every other government sends its treaties east to be made real, and the Rim Road Company swears every contract before its notary, Jessamy Quill. Oathen charges a tithe on every sworn page, and has never needed an army larger than its notaries. It has one anyway, the Canyon Watch, small and very well disciplined, because a soldier who has sworn to obey cannot do otherwise. Since Wending Pass, the Watch's oath has been redrafted. It now runs to eleven pages, and the phrase "to the death" has been removed.

## Culture and Daily Life

### Speech and Silence

- Handtalk. A sign language used for everything that matters little: prices, directions, the weather, gossip, jokes, flirtation. It is held not to bind. That belief is being tested, and fingers have started breaking.
- The bit. Children wear a soft leather muzzle until twelve, decorated by their parents with beads, ribbons and family marks. Having it removed is the coming of age, the Unbitting, and the first words spoken afterward are chosen with a Clausewright present. They are usually a family motto, or "I am here," which is safe.
- Conditional speech. "If able." "As I now intend." "Barring death, illness, or weather." A polite sentence is mostly exits.
- Proverbs. Oathen proverbs are short and verbless where possible: "Fewer words, more fingers." "A full mouth, an empty hand." "The canyon keeps the change." "Listen twice." "Say it to the dead, pay it to the living." "Better bitted than broken."

### Food, Drink and Dress

Oatheners eat goat, canyon lizard, flatbread baked on hot stones, dried figs, and a sour green cheese aged in the river caves. Water is life and law: every household's cistern-share is sworn yearly at the Rain Shrine, and stealing water is the only theft the Bench treats as a crime against the god. Meals are eaten in silence; the grace "Lack keep away" is signed, not said, unless there are foreigners at the table, in which case it is said very slowly with three exits attached. Wine is rare and feared, because drunkenness makes promises.

Dress is plain and close-fitting, in dust-red and ochre wool, with long sleeves to hide what broke. The Tongue-lines wear high collars and veils across the mouth, white for the unwed, black for those whose vows are many. Rings are worn on the thumbs as reminders of oaths outstanding: a ring per vow, so that a man's hands tell you how much he owes. The Forsworn go bare where they broke, as a point of pride.

### Marriage and Kin

Marriage vows are negotiated for months and run to forty pages, covering fidelity, property, illness, absence, the care of each other's dead, and what counts as "cherishing." Divorce does not exist: a marriage vow cannot be unsworn, only broken, and its Breaking is the chest opened to the air. What the faithless do, and what the vows forbid in their thicket of clauses, the canyon decently does not discuss aloud. The Tongue-lines marry cousin to cousin to keep the god's tongue in the family, and some old houses have married so close for so long that their children are born with mouths sealed over with skin, and must be cut open before they can feed.

### Children

Children are bitted from the first tooth until twelve. They grow up in handtalk, which they speak faster and more inventively than adults, with a whole private slang of nursery signs. The bit is soft and does not hurt, but it shapes the face: Oathen adults have a groove worn at the corners of the mouth, and some of the old bits, family heirlooms, have been worn by six generations of children and taste of all of them. Every parent in the country carries one fear above all others: that their child will say something before twelve, in the instant the bit is off for cleaning. Some do. The Bench records the Breakings of children only by number. The number this year is higher than last.

### Death and the Dead

The dead are entombed upright in the Witnessing Vaults, faces outward, so that they may witness the oaths of the living. Before a body is set in its niche, the family gathers and speaks every promise they made to the dead person in life, and the Keener asks the corpse, formally, whether each was kept. The dead do not answer. Usually. A death in Oathen also releases the dead person's oaths, which is why heirs sometimes wait at a bedside very patiently, and why the Bench has drafted a clause, standard in all wills, binding the heirs to have hoped for recovery.

Oaths sworn on the dead are the heaviest in the land: a hand on the bones, the dead one's name, the vow said into the niche. They are used for blood feuds, for marriages across feuding families, for debts too large for any Surety. When such an oath breaks, the dead person answers for Tolm, and what the dead do then is the stuff of every Oathen ghost story, and all of them are true.

### Festivals

- The Kept Rain (first day of Grace). The whole country stands in the open, faces up, mouths bitted, and the rain comes. Children take their first swim. Lovers make the year's vows in the downpour, when the noise of it hides the words from the canyon. This year, for three days, the whole country stood and waited, and the sky stayed white.
- The Unbitting (Plenty). Twelve-year-olds have their bits removed in public, in the Hall of Conditions, and speak their first chosen words. Families weep, silently.
- The Carving of the Year (Carving). The new vows of public record are cut into the Ledger. Carvers work through the night by lamplight, and the town watches from the canyon floor.
- Tablenight (longest night of Lack). Twenty-four hours in which no one in the country makes a sound. Shops close. Bells are tied. Babies are nursed constantly. It is the only day Oatheners feel safe, and the happiest day of the year.

### Crime and Punishment

Every crime in Oathen is either an oath broken or a question waiting to be asked: a convict swears a sentence, a debtor a schedule, and a murderer, if the crime is grave, receives Jude Crake. Blasphemy, the cursing of Tolm aloud, is not punished by the Bench at all; it is merely recorded, as Tolm will see to it. Of forty-one blasphemers entered in the record since the Muzzling, thirty-nine were found the next morning with their tongues gone, not cut but missing, as if never grown, and their mouths full of fine red sand. The other two were Sayers. No one has entered their sentences in the record.

## Factions

### The Bench of Clauses

The Clausewrights: rulers in practice, and the finest contract lawyers who have ever lived. They train for twenty years in the drafting of vows, in the reading of Tolm's past judgments on the Ledger, and in the art of speaking a whole day without committing to anything. They believe that the Rule is law and that law can be perfected: that somewhere there is a sentence so well drafted that it binds exactly what is intended and nothing more. They want order, the Rain explained, the Sayers kept, and their own power left untouched. They fear the Forsworn, the widening of what counts, and their own Tongue-line blood, which is the most advanced in the land.

### The Forsworn

The broken. They live in the Breakdowns, organized, angry, and with very little left to lose. A Forsworn who broke their hands cannot be broken in the hands again; a Forsworn with an opened mouth can lie without further cost. The Breakdowns have made a politics of this. Led by Brakk, the Forsworn swear loudly and freely on what is already broken, take jobs no whole Oathener can, and run the canyon's smuggling, lying and loud-mouthed trades. They want the Breakdowns made a free city, the end of public shaming, and a share of the Rim Road tithe. Some want much worse: they want everyone in Tacit broken, so that no one is better than anyone.

### The Sureties

Professionals who take on other people's oaths for a fee and carry the risk in their own bodies. A Surety swears, before the principal's oath is made, to stand behind it: if the principal breaks, the Surety breaks in their place. Sureties are licensed by the Compact of 148, tattooed with the oaths they carry in a dense fine script, and among the richest commoners in the land. They are also, inevitably, ruins. A Surety in the trade for twenty years has usually paid on a dozen defaults and carries them all. They believe in the arithmetic of risk; they want fees, fair principals, and a quiet death before the next default. They fear one bad season.

### The Quiet

An ascetic order that has given up speech entirely, some by vow and some by knife. Founded in 240 A.G. by Abbess Hollin Mure, the Quiet live in cliff-cells along the upper Gullet and teach, in writing, that speech is the god's fishing line, and every word a hook. They want silence: first for themselves, then for Oathen, and in their secret texts, for the whole Table. Sister Verity Amn has brought a terrible new teaching: that for the highest bloodlines, even thought now binds, and that the knife is not enough.

### The Echo-Wardens

Rangers who police the silent gorges and hunt down old sworn words still bouncing in the deep rock. An oath can live in a canyon for decades, flying from wall to wall, binding everyone who walks through it; the Wardens track such echoes by ear, map them, and kill them with felt baffles, wool dams, sand-falls and, for the worst, controlled rockfall. They are deaf in one ear by tradition (the right, cut at their initiation) and listen with the other like hunting dogs. They want the canyons clean of old words. They fear Sworn Gorge.

### Powers from Abroad

The Rim Road Company is the great foreign power in Oathen, operating from Quillhouse under Jessamy Quill. The Cutwrights' College keeps a small, sharp-eyed office in Tacit and knows exactly which Tongue-lines are nearest the Brink. The Purgation preaches among the Forsworn that a broken body is a body vomiting up the god. The Second Table has agents in at least three Tongue-line houses, and an interest in Sayers that needs no explaining. The Reckoners have not come through the canyons since Wending Pass; they know Renn Hold is still at the top.

## Oathen Now

The rain was late, which means a god's promise has slipped, which should not be possible. Handtalkers are starting to break. A scribe has found evidence that written words now bind. If the Regrowth is widening what counts as speech, soon there will be nothing an Oathener can safely say, sign, write, or perhaps think. And the Bench is debating, in forty-page conditionals, whether to take the bit out of a Sayer and have her fix it.

Meanwhile the Forsworn are counting. The Breakdowns have doubled in five years, swollen by handtalkers with broken fingers and scribes with split palms, and Brakk now speaks for a fifth of the capital. The Company has tripled the guard on Quillhouse. Lady Imre Dath, in her gold, is writing, and no one is quite sure on what.

### Adventure Hooks

- The Late Rain. Yusra Thole, the Rainwarden, has not spoken in a month, because she cannot lie about the rain and will not tell the truth. The Bench hires the PCs to find out what she knows without asking her a question. The answer lies in the Gullet, where the sand over Tolm's bones has grown hot enough to blister, and something beneath it has begun, very slowly, to clear its throat.
- The Surety's Last Default. Ona Fell can survive one more default, and one of her two hundred and eleven principals is about to break: a Tongue-line merchant who swore a Company contract he cannot meet. The PCs must find which one before it happens, and the merchant has hired them too, to make sure Ona breaks first.
- The Hold at Wending. A Company caravan must cross Wending Pass, and the man at the top will not let anyone through who has not sworn to hold it. Renn Hold is not mad; he is bound. Getting past him means understanding what he swore, and the four hundred on the road below have opinions too.
- Sworn Gorge Has Gone Quiet. For the first time in two hundred years the murmur behind the wall has stopped. Halvar Stane believes the echo has found a way out. Three herders in the eastern canyons have broken in the same way as the hamlet of Sowe, and the PCs must track a word through the rock before it reaches Tacit.
- The Notary's Hand. Someone is trying to make Jessamy Quill break. Agents of the Second Table want the Company's contracts loosed so that the Rim Road falls into chaos before Tablenight; the PCs are hired as her bodyguards, and every conversation in Quillhouse is a battlefield.

## Playing an Oathener

An Oathener is a person raised to weigh every word, in a country where the god in their blood is a witness that never stops writing things down. They make careful allies, terrible liars and devastating negotiators. They are also, the moment they open their mouths, at risk.

**Attribute bonus:** +1 TONGUE.

### Oaths in Oathen

These rules apply to anyone within Oathen's borders, native or not, and to Oatheners abroad when their Regrowth reaches the Course.

#### Oath Weight

Every binding statement has a **Weight** from 1 to 5, set by the GM from what was meant and how it was said.

| Weight | Kind | Examples |
| 1 | Idle | "I'll be back by dark." A small lie. A boast. |
| 2 | Plain | A deliberate promise to a person. "You can count on me." |
| 3 | Sworn | "I swear." Any witnessed vow, or a lie that matters. |
| 4 | Solemn | Marriage, Company contract, sworn sentence, vow at the Ledger. |
| 5 | Grave | An oath sworn on the dead, or on Tolm by name. |

An oath sworn in an echoing place renews once per echo the speaker hears (usually 1–3; a canyon narrows can manage 9). Each renewal is a separate oath of the same Weight, broken at the same moment. Handtalk and writing currently count at half Weight, rounded down; at Weight 0, the GM rolls a d10 anyway, and on a 1–2 the god counts it at Weight 1. The widening is happening.

#### Conditional Speech

A character can speak carefully. When they deliberately draft an oath or a sensitive answer, roll **Tongue + Clause**. The difficulty is Routine 10 at leisure, Hard 14 in a pressed negotiation, Grim 18 in combat, interrogation or under a Dread check. Every Helping adds an **Exit**: a condition the player names ("barring illness", "if the river allows", "as I now intend") under which breach is not breaking. A Grace makes the oath **Clean**: the GM must honor any reasonable reading of it. A failure means the sentence carries no Exits; a Lack means the speaker said more than they meant, and the GM names one extra Weight 1–2 promise in the words.

When a character speaks without care (in anger, grief, fear or play), the GM may call for the same roll to see whether anything binding slipped out.

#### When an Oath Is Breached

1. **Check the Exits.** If an Exit genuinely fits the circumstance, nothing happens.
2. **Argue the reading.** If the case is ambiguous, the character may plead to the god in the moment: roll **Eye + Clause** vs 10 + (2 × Weight). Success: Tolm accepts the reading. Failure: the oath breaks.
3. **The Breaking.** The body breaks at once, with a sound. The GM chooses the most apt rupture from the Breaking table (or rolls a d20 when the subject is unclear). The victim loses **2 × Weight** Flesh, which cannot reduce them below 1: the Breaking is never fatal by itself, though bleeding, exposure and further harm may be. At Weight 3 or more the rupture is permanent: a **Forsworn mark**, with a lasting penalty of −1 per Weight above 2 to rolls using the broken part. At Weight 5 the part is lost entirely.
4. **Dread.** The broken make a Dread check of the oath's Weight (maximum 4). Witnesses make one at Weight − 1 (minimum 1).

**Broken Ground.** A part already lost to a Weight 5 Breaking cannot break again. Oaths whose apt Breaking would land there may be sworn freely; if broken, the GM shifts the Breaking to the next nearest apt part, at −1 Weight. This is how the Forsworn of the Breakdowns can swear.

**Lies.** A spoken lie is an oath broken the instant it is finished. Small lies are Weight 1, lies that matter Weight 3, lies under the god's name Weight 5. Their Breaking is the mouth: lips, teeth, tongue, jaw.

#### Sureties

A **Surety** swears, before the principal's oath is made, "I stand for this word." From then on, if the principal breaks, the Surety breaks in their place, taking the full Breaking (the principal suffers only the Dread check, as a witness). A Surety may roll **Gut + Endure** vs 10 + (2 × Weight) to halve the Flesh lost; the mark is taken regardless. A principal who knowingly defaults on a Surety is called **sold** and no Surety in the land will stand for them again.

| Weight | Standard fee (lacks) | Notes |
| 1 | 2 | Bondlings will take it |
| 2 | 10 | Often bundled for merchants |
| 3 | 50 | Underwriter required |
| 4 | 200 | Marriages, Company contracts |
| 5 | 1,000 | Few will take it; oaths on the dead never |

A PC may be a Surety; each bond carried is tattooed on the body in script. Carrying more bonds than Gut + Endure means one more Fray each new Lack season, as the weight of other people's word wears the mind.

#### Oaths on the Dead

To swear on the dead, the swearer lays a hand on the bones or the tomb, speaks the dead one's name, and says the vow into the niche. The oath gains +1 Weight (to a maximum of 5), cannot be carried by a Surety, and adds a second Witness to Tolm. When it breaks, the dead **Testify**: roll a d6.

| d6 | The dead Testify |
| 1 | The corpse's jaw opens in its niche and speaks the oath aloud, in its own voice, loud enough to be heard for a mile through rock. Everyone who hears it knows who broke it. |
| 2 | The oathbreaker's Breaking takes the shape of the dead one's own death-wound, in addition to its apt shape. |
| 3 | The dead one's bones are found in the oathbreaker's bed at dawn, arranged lying beside them. |
| 4 | The oathbreaker speaks only in the dead one's voice for a month. Dread 2 for all who knew the dead. |
| 5 | Every promise the oathbreaker ever made to the dead in life is re-weighed. Each one not kept breaks now, at Weight 1. |
| 6 | The dead one's name begins to replace the oathbreaker's own. Friends forget which they are speaking to. Each Lack season, 1 Fray, until the oath is kept or the name is lost. |

### The Regrowth of Tolm

**The Sop: Swearing.** Make an unconditional oath, aloud, of Weight 2 or more, before a witness, with no Exits. Remove 1d6 Hunger. The oath binds as normal.

**The Tooth.** When an Oathener Partakes, they hear their own last words come back to them, half a second late, in a voice that is not quite theirs, and taste wet slate. If any Tooth shows a 1, the god bit back and took the character at their word: their last spoken sentence becomes a Weight 1 oath, read in its most literal sense.

**The Wants** (resisted with Caul + Resolve):
- **To Swear.** Triggered by silence held too long, by being doubted, or by a direct question. Failure: the character vows something aloud (GM's choice, Weight 1–3).
- **To Hold Others to Their Words.** Triggered when someone nearby breaks a promise. Failure: the character must confront them and demand they repeat it.
- **To Correct.** Triggered by hearing a falsehood. Failure: the character states the truth aloud, whatever it costs.
- **To Be Heard.** Triggered in echoing places. Failure: the character speaks, to hear it come back.

#### Gifts

- **The Taste: Echo-Ear.** You hear your own words repeated a half-second late. You may roll **Eye + Instinct** (Hard 14) to know whether a statement spoken in your hearing was a lie or bound its speaker, and at what Weight. Cost: in echoing places, Dread 1 checks whenever you speak.
- **The Appetite: Hold to It.** When someone makes you a promise, you may Partake to make it heavier: +1 Weight. You feel, as a pressure in your teeth, whenever they come near to breaking it. Cost: you cannot release them; you must hold them to it, and the Want To Hold Others to Their Words triggers automatically.
- **The Course: Say So.** Once per scene, state a plain fact about something you can see. Roll **Tongue + Godsign**: Hard 14 for a small thing ("That rope holds"), Grim 18 for a significant one ("The door is locked"), Dire 22 for a great one ("He cannot see us"). Success: it is so for the scene. Cost: +2 Hunger, and for the rest of the day the GM may make any figure of speech you utter come true. Flesh change: the tongue darkens and lengthens; small ears open on the neck and inner arms; a second voice speaks under yours.
- **The Brink: Make It So.** Once per session, say one sentence that becomes true, at any scale the GM judges a single sentence can carry. No roll. Cost: Regrowth +1, and until the session ends, every sentence you speak binds as Say So with no roll and no limit. Brink characters must be bitted, by law and by sense.

**The Seating.** A Seated Oathener becomes a Sayer: the face smoothing, the eyes skinned over, the mouth a door the god speaks through. The character is no longer playable. The player writes the character's last sentence, the one they would have said, and the GM decides whether it was spoken before the gold went in.

### Backgrounds

- **Junior Clausewright.** A Slate-Clerk with ink on the fingers and a head full of Exits. Clause 2, Lore 1, Reckoning 1. Starts with a slate, a bit for emergencies, and a debt to a Bencher.
- **Forsworn of the Breakdowns.** Broken at Weight 5 once, young, and proud of it. Choose one Broken Ground. Intimidate 2, Brawl 1, Deceive 1. Starts with a cleaver, an oiled cloth, and friends who lie for a living.
- **Bondling Surety.** Carrying three small bonds and the fees for them. Endure 2, Clause 1, Resolve 1. Starts with 60 lacks and three tattoos.
