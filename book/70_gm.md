# The Carver's Knife

> At a feast on the Table, the one who carves decides who eats first, who gets the best of it, and who is served the part that nobody wants. It is a position of honor. It is never a position of love.
>> — Dame Suet Marrable, to a new Carver at the Unending Dinner

[fiction]
**From the commonplace book of a Rim Road storyteller, found at Thimble Cross after the snows.**
They always ask me for the one about the god that smiled. I tell it the same way every time. I lower the lamp. I let the fire burn down until their faces are only the parts the coals can reach: noses, wet eyes, the shine on the lips. I wait until somebody coughs, and then I wait for them to be ashamed of having coughed.
The trick is not the monster. Anyone can describe a monster. The trick is to describe the kitchen first. The bread on the board, the knife beside it, the knot in the wood under the bread that looks like an eye. The child asleep in the corner with her thumb in her mouth. You make them love the kitchen. Then, when the knife moves on its own, they do not scream because of the knife. They scream because of the bread, and the knot, and the child.
I have told that story four hundred times. Last winter, on Tablenight, I told it in this very room, and when I got to the end there was a place laid at the back table that nobody had laid, and a chair pulled out, and a card with my name on it.
I am writing this down because I have decided not to tell it again.
[/fiction]

This chapter is for the Carver: the player who runs the game. It explains how to run horror on the Table, how to use the rules without letting them get in the way, how to build a campaign around the Regrowth, and how to make sure everyone at the table leaves wanting to come back, even though their characters will not.

## The Carver's Duties

You have five jobs. Everything else is technique.

- **Describe the world** so the Guests can see, smell and taste it.
- **Play everyone else**: the Regent and the beggar, the god and the dog.
- **Apply the rules** fairly, and know when to put them away.
- **Keep the table safe**, with Lines, Veils and the Grace, and with your own attention.
- **Make the world move.** The Grace is ending. The gods are growing back whether the Guests act or not. Show them the clock.

## Running Horror on the Table

### Make Them Love the Kitchen

Horror is the violation of something cared for. If the Guests do not care about anything, nothing can be violated. Spend the first half of every story making them care: a warm inn, a Kept grandmother who tells filthy jokes through her stitched jaw, a Fatlands cook who sings while she skims the drip from the stockpot, a Netwatch sergeant who calls his patrol "my ducks". Let the Guests eat, rest, joke and plan. Let Who Waits at Home write them letters. Then take it away.

### All Five Senses, and Then One More

The Table is a world of bodies. Describe it through the body. For every horror you run, ask:

- **What does it smell like?** The sweet wrongness of the Breath in Sallowreach; the rendering-yard stink of the Belch; salt and kelp and the cold-mineral smell of a drowned man's lungs; lamp-smoke and sour rouse-sweat in the Vigil; iron, wet clay and birth-water in Cradlewrack; the hot stone and dry silence of Oathen; and, in the Fast, bread, always fresh bread, from nowhere.
- **What does it sound like?** Flies in fronts. Teeth in the wheat. Bailing coughs at dawn. Bells every quarter hour. The pang, felt through the floor. The crack of a body breaking to match a broken word.
- **What does it feel like?** Warm ground that rises and falls. Cold grey fingers with one joint too many. A floor that slopes so you cannot rest.
- **What does it taste like?** Everything on the Table is food, or was. Make them taste it.
- **What does it look like?** Last. Sight is the least frightening sense, because it is the one we trust.
- **And the sixth sense, the god:** what does the Guest's own blood do, in the presence of this? Does their Hunger stir? Does their Tooth itch? Does the thing in front of them seem, horribly, like family?

### Gore With a Purpose

This book is graphic, and your table may want it graphic. That is allowed and it can be powerful. The rule is: **gore should always carry meaning.** A body opened on the Table is never just a body. It tells the Guests what the god wants, what the land costs, what was done and by whom. A Mangling is a story in a wound. When you describe the bowel sliding free of a Netwatch officer's belly, make sure the Guests also see the wedding knot tied into his anchoring rope.

When the table's appetite is satisfied, cut away. Restraint is a tool too. The door closing on the Cullmaster's dusk shift, and the sound of the wheat moving in a field where there is no wind, can be worse than anything you say.

### Tragedy, Not Spectacle

Some of this book's horrors are deliberately human: suicide, contagious despair, kin-slaying, the murder of the helpless. Run them as tragedy. That means:

- The camera stays on the people left behind: the cut rope on the bed, the extra place at the table, the note in a careful hand, the neighbors who stop calling names through the wall.
- Nobody is a punchline, and nobody's death is a reward.
- The god benefits. Every loss is a little more of it in the world. That is the horror, more than the act.
- You never describe *how*. You describe *after*.

### The Clock Is Always Running

The Grace is ending. Use clocks: the Cradlewrack interval shortening, the Vigil's empty beds, the Pantry's four months of grain, the Brinehollow waterline, the Sallowreach Hushes spreading toward the Hand-line estates. Write each one on a card where the Guests can see it. Advance them between sessions whether the Guests act or not. Horror is helplessness, and the slow tick of a number going down is helplessness made visible.

### No Rescue, but Many Mercies

The Table cannot be saved. That is the premise. But individual people can be: for a night, a season, a life. A Guest who cannot stop the Reaping can still get one family across the border before the dusk shift. A Guest who cannot cure the Vigil can still sit up with a dying nudger through her last hour. Those small mercies are the only victories the Long Grace offers, and they should feel enormous. Do not take them away cheaply. Take them away expensively, much later, when the Guests have forgotten to guard them.

## Using the Rules

### When to Call for Dread

Call for a Dread check the first time the Guests encounter a horror, and whenever something they love is harmed. Do not call for Dread so often that Fray becomes accounting. If a Guest is a Seamer, corpses do not frighten her; what frightens her is a corpse that thanks her. Calibrate to the character.

### Pacing Hunger

The Tooth is the most important choice a player makes. Make it tempting: put the Guests in positions where +3.5 is the difference between saving someone and failing them. Make it visible: describe the god coming forward every time they Partake. Make it count: when Hunger fills and Regrowth rises, stop the game for a moment and describe the change together. A Guest who reaches the Course should feel the weight of it. In a typical campaign of twenty sessions, a Guest who Partakes freely will reach the Course; one who Partakes recklessly will reach the Brink.

### When the Seating Comes

Treat a Guest's Seating as the finale of their story, not as a failure. Talk with the player beforehand. Give them a last scene: a last speech, a last kindness, a last act of terrible power. Then let the god have them. Bring them back later as part of the world, and let the other Guests visit what is left. The Dowager Hills are full of grandmothers who were once someone's companions on the road.

### Fudging and Fairness

Roll in the open whenever you can. The Table is a cruel place, and the dice are the most honest thing in it. If a Guest dies to a bad roll in a meaningless fight, that is a sign you should not have run a meaningless fight. Make fights matter, warn players when they are walking into death, and let the dice fall.

### Saying Yes to the God

Players will look for loopholes: Kept characters who use their undying bodies as shields; Oathen Guests who swear things into existence; Brinehollowers who try to bargain with their Tenants. Let them. The gods are not interested in rules-lawyering, but they are very interested in being invited in. Every loophole a Guest finds is a door the god can walk through. Say yes, and then show them who else came through it.

## Building a Campaign

### Choose a Center

Campaigns on the Table work best when they are anchored to one place and one thread, at least to start. Choose:

- **A land.** Begin in one land, and let the Guests learn its rules intimately before they travel.
- **A thread.** Pick one of the *Threads Between the Lands*: the Deadwatch, the Host on the Road, Brine in the Rouse, the Missing Hand, the Sayer's Sentence, Eight Chairs. It will pull the Guests across borders when they are ready.
- **A clock.** The land's own crisis, ticking.
- **A patron.** Someone who pays the Guests or protects them, and who will betray them.

### Campaign Frames

**The Caravan.** The Guests are the crew of a Rim Road Company caravan: Factors, Wardens, a cook, a Seamer, a Cutwright on assignment. Each story is a waystation, a cargo, a border. The Company's contracts are sworn in Oathen and cannot be broken; it is only a matter of time until one of them requires something unthinkable.

**The Survey.** The Guests work for the Cutwrights' College, mapping where each god is regrowing fastest. They travel the lands, read bloodlines, and slowly discover that their own ledgers are being used by the Second Table to plan a menu.

**The Purge.** The Guests are a Purgation cell. At first they fast and bleed and pray. Then they are asked to burn a house with a family inside it. What do they believe, and how far will it take them?

**The Watch.** The Guests are Nightwardens in one city: the Netwatch of Lowmark, the Wakers of the Vigil, the Loft-Wardens of Lastgate. Each story is a case. The cases get worse. The city's secret, which the Guests are paid to protect, is the worst of all.

**The Household.** The Guests serve or belong to a single noble family in the last stages of the Regrowth: a Hand-line house where the hands have started to grow; a Gut-line estate where the matriarch is putting down roots; a Caul-line household where the heir has been carried for forty years. Domestic, claustrophobic, unbearable.

**The Pilgrimage.** The Guests are travelers bound for the Fast, for reasons of their own: to find the Host, to find a cure, to find someone who was invited and never came home. The Rim Road is the journey. Hallowboard is the end.

### Escalation

A campaign on the Table should move through four movements, like the Regrowth itself:

| Movement | Sessions | What Happens |
| The Taste | 1–4 | The Guests learn one land. Small horrors. Personal stakes. The god in their blood barely stirs. |
| The Appetite | 5–10 | A thread pulls them across a border. They discover the scale of the Regrowth. They begin to Partake to survive. |
| The Course | 11–18 | The powers of the Table notice them. A Guest is changed beyond hiding. Someone they love is lost. A land's crisis breaks. |
| The Seating | 19+ | The end of the Grace in one land, or all of them. The Secrets of the Table come into play. Somebody sits down. |

### Making the Guests Matter

The Guests cannot save the world. They can decide who sits at the table when it ends. Let their choices shape the shape of the catastrophe: whether the Vigil buys the secret of Keeping, whether the Assemblers' figure gets its hand, whether Lady Imre Dath is unbitted and what she is told to say. A campaign on the Table is a story about which awful thing happens, and who is to blame for it.

## The Carver's Tools

### Read-Aloud Text

Read-aloud passages in this book are set in the same style as in-world documents. Read them slowly. Pause before the last line. Then stop, and let the silence do the work. Do not explain. The Guests will ask.

### Handouts

The Table is a world of documents: death licenses, Cutwright ledgers, contracts forty pages long, place cards, the Tally, the Long Count, a seemer's report. Give your players paper. A handwritten place card with a Guest's name on it, set at their seat before the session starts, will do more than any monster.

### Names

Every land has its naming customs, listed in its chapter. When you need a name in a hurry: Sallowreach names are soft, mournful and old (Bettany, Osric, Henna, Tam); Fatlands names are hearty and edible (Suet, Brisket, Tansy, Hob); Brinehollow names are wet and coastal (Kessa, Sennet, Hask, Tolly); Vigil names ring like bells (Ilvane, Maud, Pip, Rook); Cradlewrack names are rustic and beginning (Orla, Lark, Juniper, Hinge); Oathen names are clipped and few-syllabled (Ket, Brakk, Rue, Ona); the Unfed name their children for the Refusal (Forbear, Abstain, Decline, Stint, Tarry, Nay, Want, Spare).

## Safety, Again

You have read the content warning. You have discussed Lines and Veils. You have explained the Grace. Now do one more thing: watch the people at your table, not just their characters. If someone has gone quiet, check in at the break. If someone laughs too hard at the wrong moment, it may be how they are coping. If a scene you planned starts to land on something real in someone's life, stop it, and do not make them explain.

The Table is a world without rescue. Your table is not. Be the Carver who makes sure everyone gets fed.
