# Secrets of the Table

> Eight gods. Seven peoples. Six meals and one refusal. The arithmetic does not close.
>> — marginal note in the Master Concordance at Quire, in the Archprovost's hand

[box: Players, Stop Here]
This chapter is for the Game Master alone. It answers the questions the rest of the book refuses to: who the Eighth was, why the Providers held still, what the Regrowth is for, what Orrum is laying its tables for, and how the Long Grace ends. If you are a player, close the book here. Nothing in this chapter will help your character survive, and everything in it will spoil the slow, horrible pleasure of finding out at the table. The people of the Table have spent six hundred and forty-one years chiseling this answer out of the world. Do them the courtesy.
[/box]

## How to Use This Chapter

What follows is the default truth of the setting: one coherent answer to every mystery the other chapters raise. It is built to be discovered piece by piece over a long campaign, through the Eight Chairs thread and the clues scattered through every land. After it come three alternate truths, one for each of the Three Doctrines, for GMs who prefer a different answer or whose players have read too much. Last comes How the Grace Ends: five apocalypses, any of which can serve as a campaign's finale.

Whichever truth you choose, hold to one rule. The answer must never be a solution. Knowing why the world is ending should change what the characters fear, what they choose and whom they forgive. It must never give them a way to stop it. The Long Grace is a grace, said before a meal. The meal is coming.

## The Default Truth: The Guest

### What the Eighth Was

The eighth figure, the one chiseled out of every carving older than the Gorging, was not a Provider. It provided nothing. Its oldest name, recoverable from the chisel-shadows on four hundred carvings by anyone patient enough to make rubbings and lay them over one another (as Archprovost Hesper Lune has done), is **the Guest**.

The Guest was the god of hunger itself. Not appetite, which is a body's wanting, but hunger: the pure, sourceless, unsatisfiable fact of a mouth. Its people were every people. It had no land, because it had a chair at every table. In every house on the old Table, at every meal, there was a place for it, and that place was the reason meals were eaten at all, because without the Guest no one would ever have needed to eat. The other seven gods were each the god of something. The Guest was the god of *needing* something.

The old carvings show eight figures around a table. Seven face inward. The eighth is the one who sits. The seven Providers are shown standing, because they were not guests at that table. They were the hosts and the servers; Orrum pulls out the chairs, and the others bring the dishes. The dishes, in the oldest carvings of all, which the College has never found intact, were the world's increase: seed, catch, calf, child, word, sleep, ending, poured upward through each god in turn and set before the one who sat.

That is what worship was. In Table-speech the words for *worship* and *eat* share a root, *orr-*, and scholars insist they always did. They are right. The giving back that every people made to its Provider was not eaten by the Provider. It was passed along. The gods were the Guest's stewards, and the peoples fed it through them, a little, every day, and in return it stayed in its chair and did not come into the kitchen.

### The Grace

*Lack keep away.*

Every people on the Table says it before eating with a stranger. It is the only thing they all share besides the coin and the road. It is older than the Gorging, older than the Lack that gave it its modern name, older than the Rim. It is the last surviving prayer to the Guest. Its oldest form, which a few fen grandmothers and a few Oathen Clausewrights still know, is longer, and in Table-speech it means: *Guest, you have been fed; keep to your chair.*

Every stranger at a table is a little bit the Guest. That is why the grace is said with strangers and not with family. It is a ward. It is the reason, for ten thousand years, the Guest never stood up.

The Rim Road custom of setting one extra chair at every inn table on Tablenight and turning it to face the wall is another ward, and a very old one, and the innkeepers who keep it have no idea what it is for. A chair turned to the wall is a place laid for someone who has been asked to leave.

### The Long Lack

Ten years before the Gorging, the Guest stopped staying in its chair.

Nobody knows why. The GM may decide, or leave it unknown; the people of the Table never learned, and that absence is the oldest wound in the world. Perhaps the giving back had grown thin, in an age of plenty when people forgot to say the long grace. Perhaps it was simply time, as winter is time. Perhaps (and this is the answer the seabed table suggests) it had done this before, many times, and the world was only due.

What happened is that the Guest sat down at every table at once. It took its place in every kitchen, every granary, every womb, every net, invisible, and ate. It ate the world's increase before the world could have it. That is what the Long Lack was. Seed did not rot in the ground; it was eaten in the ground, the beginning sucked out of it, and what was left rotted. The ash bread was bread the Guest had eaten in the oven, leaving only the shape. The nets came up empty because what swam into them was eaten on the way up. Women did not conceive because the Guest was sitting in that chair too. For nine years, nothing began, because the Guest ate beginnings.

And it ate gods. The Guest's proper food is not the world's increase but what the increase passes through. As the Lack went on, it turned to the stewards themselves. The Providers grew thin not from shared hunger but because they were being eaten, slowly, from the inside, by the thing they had served since before there were people. They stopped speaking because it had eaten their voices first. They lay down because it had eaten their strength.

### The Eldest Walk, Revisited

The priests who met at Hallowboard in the eighth year read the carvings on the table's eight sides and found a line of small figures walking toward the table. They read it as a tithe owed to the Providers. They were not wrong that it was a tithe. They were wrong about who it was owed to and what the figures were. The walking figures in the oldest carvings are not offerings. They are the courses, carried in.

The Eldest Walk changed nothing. Sixty thousand firstborn opened their throats at the feet of gods who could not accept them, because the gods were no longer anyone's to accept; they were food. The Guest did not take the eldest either. The Guest eats increase, beginnings, gods. The dead are none of these. The eldest lay where they were laid until their families came back for them with sacks.

Play this straight and let it hurt. It is the most useless act in the history of the world, and it is also the only reason the world survived, because it is the act that turned the knives. Without the Walk, the peoples would have gone on hoping until they died. The Walk proved that the gods would save no one. The fury of that proof is what made the Gorging possible. The Providers could not ask to be eaten; their voices were gone. They needed their peoples to want to.

### Why the Providers Held Still

They held still because being eaten was the only place left to hide.

The Guest eats gods, and it finds them by their wholeness. A god is a single great thing, a mountain of meaning, and it cannot be overlooked. But a god cut into ten thousand portions and swallowed by ten thousand mouths is no longer a single thing. It is a trace in the blood of a people, a little salt in the lungs, a little grey in the hands, scattered through bodies that each have their own small hungers. The Guest, coming to the table and finding the dishes gone, could not tell god from eater. A coin hidden in a sack of coins.

So when the knives came, the Providers lay down and held still and let themselves be divided. The Cuts were not merely the shape of the meal; they were the shape of the hiding. Some portions of each god had to be large enough to keep the god's nature whole somewhere, in a few bloodlines, and those families became the nobility. The rest was spread as thin as it could be spread. Ossel smiled. Ummer watched with kind eyes. Iss never woke, because a sleeping god is quieter. Vey kept beginning again until it could be swallowed small. Tolm listened, and what it heard was that its people were not speaking, and that was so: a meal with no words, in front of a god whose nature was to make speech true, was the quietest possible place to disappear into.

Then the Guest stood up from the table. The food was gone. On the morning after the Gorging, it left. And the world exhaled. That is why the crops rose in a week: not a miracle, but nine years of withheld increase coming back all at once, the world's held breath let out. *This was not grown,* said Tallis Brome in the Fatlands. *This was poured.* She was right. It was poured back.

### Orrum, and Meek Garrity's Seating Plan

Orrum the Host was the only one of the seven who did not need to hide on its own account, because of what it was. A Host is not food; a Host is the one who serves. The Guest cannot eat its Host, any more than a guest at a feast can eat the house. That is the oldest law of the table, older than the Providers, and it binds the Guest as it binds every guest who has ever sat down.

But Orrum had a duty. A Host must feed whoever comes. So it did the only thing a Host can do when a terrible guest arrives: it arranged the meal. Meek Garrity has found its seating plan, and he has misread it, as the Unfed would.

The plan is in a hand that is not human. It is a list of the seven peoples and the seven Providers, which people eats which god, in what order, on which night of a single Plate, so that every god would be hidden before the Guest noticed the dishes disappearing. Orrum drew it up and sent it, by dream and by carving and by the hands of its priests, to every land. It is the reason the Night of Seven Tables happened everywhere at once without anyone agreeing to it. It is the reason the Cuts follow the same pattern in every land. Orrum seated the world.

It put its own people last, on the tenth day, because a Host eats last. It meant, perhaps, to lie down on Hallowboard at the very end and be eaten by the heartland and hidden too, the final course, after everyone else was safe. The plan's last line, in Orrum's hand, reads: *After the tenth day, the table is cleared.* It is not a record. It is a warning. After the tenth day, it would be too late.

The heartland was the largest of the lands, and its people were the weakest, and the snow lay deep. They arrived on the tenth day. The Guest had already risen from the table at dawn. Orrum lay on Hallowboard and heard them coming and opened its eyes and looked toward the eighth side, where the Guest had sat, and the Guest was not there. The meal was over. If the Unfed had eaten Orrum then, they would have been the only people on the Table carrying a god the Guest had already noticed, and it would have come back for them, and for everyone. So Orrum got up. Its people never refused. They were late, and the others had left them nothing. Nine in ten of them died with a meal in front of them that it was no longer safe to eat.

The survivors made a virtue of it. They called it the Refusal. It is the founding lie of the only innocent people in the world, and Meek Garrity is about to take it away from them.

### Why the Lack Never Left the Fast

When the Guest stood up, it marked its place. A diner who means to return leaves something on the chair. The Fast is the Guest's napkin. The one land that did not eat is the one land the Guest has kept its mouth on: the Lack never lifted there because the Guest never entirely left. Seed still rots in the Fast because it is still being eaten in the ground. The heartland's people live inside the last, quiet, continuing remnant of the Long Lack, and have for six hundred and forty-one years, and believe it is their reward for virtue.

The single green sprout in Yield Hartsell's field is not a sign the Lack is ending. It is a sign that the Guest has stood up from that chair, too. It is on its way somewhere else.

### The Regrowth

The Regrowth is the Providers coming back out of hiding, and they are doing it because the Guest is returning.

Where the Guest went when it left the Table, nobody knows. Beyond the edges of the world there is sea and wasteland and salt, and nobody who has gone looking has come back with anything worth the trip. It may have other tables. It may make rounds. The College's graph, which shows every land's Regrowth bending upward at once around 600 A.G., marks the moment it turned back toward the Table. Every Regrowth is speeding up because the Guest is getting closer.

The gods are not rising out of malice or out of justice. They are being flushed out. A god hidden in a people is a god in a trap: if the Guest comes and cannot find the god, it will eat the people to get at it, the way a man who has lost a coin in the bread eats the loaf. The hidden gods know this, as much as they know anything now. Every instinct they have left says *get out*. So they are trying to gather themselves back together, out of ten thousand bodies, into something whole that can run. Every Seating is a god trying to stand up.

The tragedy is that a god that gathers itself back together is a god the Guest can find. The gods are fleeing straight into the open. They cannot help it. The hunger in the blood, which the rules call Hunger and which every character feels when they Partake, is the Guest's approach felt from inside the food. When a character's Hunger fills and their Regrowth rises, that is a god flinching toward the door.

### What the Seatings Will Do

A full Seating, in any land, is a god whole again, wearing its people or its ground. Each one is a dish carried back to the table. Three lands are close.

- **Sallowreach.** The Hushes are Ossel's body re-forming in the fen, a piece at a time, wherever Hand-line blood is thickest. When they join, the fen will be one great Hush, and Ossel will lie in it, whole, grey, with its hands open, as it lay on the first night. The Kept will finish, all at once. Their silence will be the first course.
- **The Vigil.** The Counted are Iss, gathered into sixty thousand sleeping bodies in one shared dream. The number Matron Dorm keeps is the number of sleepers required to hold the whole god. When the last bed fills, the eye in the long room opens. Iss has never been awake. The first thing it will ever see is the Guest's chair.
- **Cradlewrack.** The land is in labor with Vey. Every Part is a piece of the god born early, and the Assemblers' figure is an attempt to deliver it by hand. When the pangs close to nothing, the Dilation will open fully and Vey will be born whole out of the ground, the size of the highlands.
- **Brinehollow.** The sea is moving into the Tenanted because Dromm is gathering itself back together in their bodies and in the Trench, pulling its element home. When the last of the sea has gone in, Dromm will rise from the Trench as it was dragged from it, and the coast will be dry to the horizon.
- **The Fatlands.** Ummer is regrowing in the soil and in the Laden. The Reaping is the god gathering its portions out of the people who carry them. When enough of the plains are Seated, the land will stand up and walk, as Ummer walked, and the fields will bow toward it.
- **Oathen.** Tolm is regrowing in its Sayers, and the widening of what binds (handtalk, writing, thought) is the god's attention spreading back out through its people. When a Sayer fully Seats, Tolm will speak with one voice again, and what it says will be so. Its last promise, the Kept Rain, slipped because the god keeping it is moving.

When all six are Seated, the Table will look as it did on the eve of the Gorging: six gods, whole, in their lands, gaunt and frightened, with nowhere left to hide. And at Hallowboard, the Host will have laid the table.

### What Orrum Is Laying the Tables For

Orrum is a Host. A Host must feed whoever comes. It knows the Guest is coming back, and it is laying a place for the Guest. That is the whole of it, and the cruelty is that Orrum cannot do otherwise.

Every table Orrum lays has one chair without a place card. That chair is the Guest's. Every table at a crossroads in another land marks the road the Guest will walk, the way a household sets lamps along the drive on the night of a great dinner. The Long Table in the valley, nine miles of the Seated, is the high table. And when the Guest sits, the Host must serve what the Guest asks for, and the Guest asks for gods. Orrum will have to bring them in. It has been the steward of the Guest's table since before there were people, and every Seating that brings a god back together brings it closer to Orrum's hands.

But there is a second thing Orrum is doing, and it is the closest thing to love in this book. The law that binds the Guest says it cannot eat its Host, and it cannot eat its Host's other guests. Anyone seated at a Host's table as a guest is under the Host's protection. The Seated are not food. They are sheltered. Orrum has spent six hundred and forty-one years trying to seat its own people, the Unfed, where the Guest cannot touch them, and they have spent six hundred and forty-one years saying *No, thank you.* The place cards with names, the crusts replaced with something better on Abby Rooke's windowsill, the week Little Fain spent being fed and the one who cried when it was time to go: all of it is the Host pleading.

It is no rescue. The Seated waste at the table for decades, eating air, praising empty plates, alive and smiling and gone. To be sheltered by Orrum is to stop living in every way except the last. And Orrum is now laying tables outside the Fast, in every land, and the names on the cards are not only Unfed names. It is trying to seat as many as it can before the Guest arrives. It will not be enough. It knows that too. Spare Tolland of the Hostwatch thinks it is lonely. It is. It is a host on the night before the worst dinner in the world, setting places for the children in the kitchen, knowing the guest of honor eats children.

Welcome Ardent, the first Unfed ever to accept, was offered a position because the Host needed a steward who could walk among people and lay tables without frightening them. He has spent six centuries doing it. He knows whose chair the empty one is. He would like very much to be relieved.

### Grandfather Peat's Smiling God

Grandfather Peat is the boy at the back of the crowd on the night Sallowreach ate Ossel, the peat-cutter's son who never ate and who stared past the god at the empty bank on the far side of the fire. He drowned that week, and because he drowned in Sallowreach in the first week of the Gorging, he was among the very first of the Kept, sealed in bog and brine and found centuries later, still aware.

He says the god was smiling. He is right, and the full account, which he will give to anyone who learns his dialect and asks the right question, is this. Sallowreach ate last of all the peoples, slowest, because no one wanted to begin. By the time the fen-folk were halfway through Ossel, every other god on the Table had been swallowed. The Guest had come to the fen, to the last dish still on the table. It sat on the empty bank across the fire. Peat saw it, because he was the only one not looking at the food. He describes it as *the shape the fog makes around someone who isn't there*, very tall, sitting, with its hands in its lap and its face turned toward the god. It was waiting for the fen-folk to finish so it could have what was left.

And Ossel, half eaten, its hands still lifting to close the eyes of dying children, turned its great grey face toward the Guest across the fire and smiled at it. It was the smile of a hunted thing that has reached cover just in time and knows it. It was the smile of a god of endings who had just understood how this one ended. The Guest looked at Ossel, and then it looked at Peat, who was looking at it. Peat ran. The fen took him. He has been underwater, more or less, for six hundred years, and he has had time to think about what the Guest's face did when it smiled back.

### Cray Lobbett's Eight-Chair Table

The stone table on the far Uncovered is older than the Gorging. It is older than the Providers. It is older than the sea that covered it. It is a table from a world before this one, and the Guest ate at it.

The seven chairs pushed in belonged to seven gods who came before the Providers, in an age nobody remembers, and who did not find a place to hide. When a diner has finished, the chair is pushed in. Those seven are finished. The Providers grew, in the long ages after, out of what fell from that table: crumbs, the way gods are born when a world has been eaten nearly clean. That is how the Providers knew what to do when the Long Lack came. They had been crumbs on the floor at the last one.

The eighth chair is pulled out because the Guest has never pushed it in. It does not finish. It goes away and comes back. The sea's retreat is Dromm gathering itself, but its effect is to uncover the table, and the Tenanted who sing at the lowest tide are singing, in a language Ilse Marl has been writing down as a chart, the directions to it.

Cray Lobbett cannot stop going back because the chair is inviting him. Each time he visits, it is pulled out a little further, toward him. He has not sat down. He has measured the distance with a knotted cord, and by his reckoning he has three visits left.

### Meek Garrity, Peat and Lobbett Together

Put the three together, as the Eight Chairs thread invites, and the characters have the whole of the default truth: an old table where the Guest ate the gods before; a fen where it sat to watch the last of the Providers disappear and was smiled at; a seating plan in the Host's hand that arranged the hiding of the world and missed its own people by a day. What they do with it is their business. Knowing it changes nothing, except this: the characters will understand that every Seating they help along, every Regrowth they hasten, every god they summon out of its hiding to cure or kill or fix something, is a dish carried to the table.

### What the Second Table's Second Meal Would Cause

The Second Table's creed is *It worked once*. It did. The diners do not understand why.

The first meal worked because the gods chose it, held still for it, and were scattered through ten thousand mouths so thinly that the Guest could not find them. The Second Table plans the opposite. A few dozen diners, all of them High Cuts, intend to eat six whole gods between them. A god eaten by a few is not hidden; it is concentrated. It is plated. Each diner of the second meal would become a single, dense, unmistakable vessel of a god, sitting at a single table, ready to serve. The Second Table is not preparing a second Gorging. It is preparing the Guest's dinner, by hand, and seating itself in the dishes. The blank eighth line at the foot of their menus is not a missing course. It is the line for the diner, and it is not them.

And then there is Wont Bellamy's proposal: to begin with the one god still whole. If the Second Table eats Orrum, there is no Host. The law that binds the Guest is a law of hospitality: a guest eats what is set before it, and only that, and spares the house and the household. Kill the Host and there is no house, and no household, and no law. The Guest would arrive at a Table with no one to lay it, no chair to keep it in, no grace that means anything, and it would do what hunger does when no one is serving it. It would eat the Table. Not the gods in it; the Table itself, every field, every body, every road, every season, every beginning, until there was nothing to begin again from. The College calls this, in the one ledger it has written about it, *the Unhosting*.

The Head of the Table, who presides from an empty chair at every Second Table dinner and has never been seen, is no one. The diners each believe one of the others knows who it is. The chair is pulled out at every dinner because the first Carvers, centuries ago, found that it would not stay pushed in.

## The Alternate Truths

The default truth is the Guest. A GM may prefer a world in which one of the Three Doctrines is simply correct. Each of the following replaces the default answers in full. Clues already planted in the other chapters (the chiseled figure, the eighth chair, the seating plan, the smiling god, the seabed table) still work; they only point somewhere else.

### The Theft: The Bill

*We murdered them. The Regrowth is justice, and we deserve it.*

**The Eighth.** The Eighth was the god of increase: not harvest, which was Ummer's, but *more*, the simple fact that there could be more tomorrow than today. It lived among all the peoples, invisibly, as the surplus in every granary. In the generation before the Lack, in an age of fat and pride, the peoples found it, and caught it, and ate it, a little in every land, in secret, to have more than their neighbors. It was the first Gorging, and nobody ever spoke of it. The chisel marks dated before the Gorging are the guilt of the first eaters, cutting their victim out of every carving.

**The Lack.** The Lack was simply the world without increase. Nothing began because the god of beginning-again was in the peoples' bellies. The Providers starved because there was no longer any more of anything.

**Why the Providers held still.** Each was bound by its own nature, and could not resist without ceasing to be itself. Ossel could not refuse an ending. Ummer could not refuse to feed. Dromm held still because it was the god of keeping things down. Iss could not wake. Vey could not refuse to begin again. Tolm heard the peoples' silence and could not make it untrue. They held still in grief and contempt, and they let themselves be eaten so that the peoples would carry, in their blood, a debt that could be collected.

**The Regrowth.** It is the bill. The gods are growing back to take what is owed, with six centuries of interest, and the Seatings are the gods standing up in their courtroom. **Orrum** lays its tables as an accusation: every table has a place for the Eighth, the first one eaten, the one that was never mourned, and the Seated are those who have accepted their sentence. **Peat's smiling god** was smiling the way a creditor smiles. **Garrity's seating plan** is a list of debts. **The seabed table** is where the Eighth was eaten. **The Second Table's meal** would be a second murder with the bailiff already in the room, and the gods would rise in each diner at once.

**How this changes play.** The Theft is a world of guilt. Nothing that happens is unfair. Characters who seek to escape the Regrowth are bilking a creditor, and the creditor is in their blood. Atonement is possible in small ways; it does not reduce the bill.

### The Gift: The Love That Does Not Fit

*They offered themselves out of love. The Regrowth is a tragedy neither side wanted.*

**The Eighth.** The Eighth was the god of the shared table itself, the bond between the peoples: the stranger whose arrival makes a meal a feast, the reason anyone sets an extra place. It was old and tired, and when the Lack came (a natural disaster, a winter of the world, no one's fault) it was the first to give itself. It lay down and asked the other seven to eat it, so that they could go on feeding their peoples a little longer. They did. The Long Lack's middle years, when the gods grew thin but did not die, were the years they lived on it. The peoples, in grief and incomprehension, chiseled its figure out of every carving, because they could not bear to look at the place where it had been.

**Why the Providers held still.** Love, as the Thankers say. When the Eighth was gone and the Lack went on, the seven did what it had done, and gave themselves.

**The Regrowth.** It is the gods dying a second time, slowly, inside their peoples, because love that large does not fit in a body. It hurts them as it hurts their hosts. The Seatings are the gods trying, with their last strength, to give again: Ossel offering endings, Iss offering rest, Vey offering new beginnings, all of it too much, all of it ruinous. **Orrum** is the only one that was not allowed to give itself; its tables are the gift the Unfed refused, still being offered, and the Seated are happy because they accepted. **Peat's smiling god** smiled because it loved the boy at the back and was glad he had not eaten. **Garrity's plan** is a list of who would be fed first. **The seabed table** is where the Eighth gave itself to the seven; its chair is pulled out in memory. **The Second Table's meal** would be accepted, gladly, by gods who cannot refuse to give, and would kill the diners with kindness in a single night.

**How this changes play.** The Gift is a world without villains, which is worse. Every horror is an act of love, and every act of love is a horror. There is nothing to fight. There is only grief, and the knowledge that the gods would stop if they could.

### The Bait: The Brood

*They wanted to be eaten. The Lack was theirs.*

**The Eighth.** The Eighth was the parent. The seven Providers were its brood, and the eighth place in the carvings was the mother's place, at the head of the table, and the peoples cut it out because the brood, in their blood, told them to. The Eighth died long before the Gorging, or went away, as such things do when their young are grown.

**The Lack.** The Lack was the Providers' doing, exactly as the Baiters preach. They withheld the world's increase, together, for nine years, to bring their peoples to the edge of starvation, because a god cannot breed on its own. It must be eaten. The Eldest Walk was useless because the gods did not want people. They wanted to be wanted.

**Why the Providers held still.** A seed does not fight the ground.

**The Regrowth.** It is a hatching. Every High Cut body is an egg, and each will open, not into one god but into thousands, one per noble vessel, a brood of godlings for every land. The Seatings are the first hatchlings, and they are hungry, and their first meal will be the peoples that carried them. **Orrum** is the one that failed to breed. Its people refused, or were late, and it has been desperate for six centuries to be eaten by someone, anyone. Its tables are invitations. Eventually, the courses on them will include the Host. **Peat's smiling god** smiled the way a parent smiles at a christening. **Garrity's plan** is a planting chart. **The seabed table** is the nursery of the last brood, which hatched and ate its world and left only that. **The Second Table's meal** would be a second sowing: the gods, eaten again by a few, would breed again in those few, and the next generation of gods would be born from the bodies of the Table's nobility, in their beds, all at once.

**How this changes play.** The Bait is a world of parasitism, and its horror is biological. Everyone is incubating something. The doctrine's joyous cults are right, and the Second Helping's feasts are the most honest thing on the Table.

## How the Grace Ends

These are five endings for the world, built as campaign finales. Each can be reached through the threads in the previous chapter; each has signs, a last act, and a final image. None of them offers real rescue. Characters can choose where to stand, whom to hold, what to say, and how much of the world goes down with dignity; they cannot stop the meal. Choose one in advance or let the campaign's threads decide. The Long Grace is a grace. When it ends, someone eats.

### The Guest Sits Down

*The default ending.*

**Signs.** The eighth chair at every Rim inn, turned to the wall on Tablenight, is found turned to face the table in the morning, and cannot be turned back. Hunger rises in every godsigned character by 1 each week, with no Partaking. Seed begins to rot unsprouted again in the Fast's border villages, then in the Fatlands' outer fields. Three Seatings complete within a season: a Hush swallows Lastgate's eastern quarter, the Dormitory's last bed is filled, the Dilation opens. Welcome Ardent asks to be relieved and nobody answers.

**The last act.** On Tablenight, every table on the Table is laid at once, in every house, by no one. Every chair has a card but one. The grace is said everywhere, and for the first time in ten thousand years it does not work. The Guest arrives at Hallowboard and sits. Orrum, weeping, begins to serve. The gods are brought in from their lands one by one, and each land goes with its god: Sallowreach closed like a book, the Vigil asleep forever, the highlands born and swallowed, the coast dried to the bone, the plains eaten like a cut of meat, the canyons silenced mid-word.

**What the characters can do.** Choose a table. Characters who accept a place at one of Orrum's tables are Seated, sheltered, and will waste forever, smiling, in a world being eaten around them. Characters who refuse can spend the last night doing whatever matters most to them before their land's god is carried in. A character with a high Cut can try to keep their portion of the god from being gathered, and hold the Guest's attention on themselves for one hour, which is long enough for someone they love to reach Orrum's table.

**The last image.** The Long Lack, again, everywhere, for nine years in a single night. At dawn there is nothing left on the Table but the Fast, which is full of tables, and at every table a person, eating air, praising the food, and the Host walking among them, refilling glasses that are already full.

### The Last Seating

*The gods come back.*

**Signs.** The Seatings accelerate past any curve the College has drawn. Nobody needs the Guest for this ending; it may come or not, later, it does not matter. The gods simply finish regrowing first. Every godsigned character's Regrowth rises by 1 each season.

**The last act.** Six gods stand up out of six lands in the same year. Ossel lies in a fen that is one silent Hush, and the Kept finish all at once, and the silence spreads south. Ummer rises out of the Fatlands, a living country on legs, with its people still growing on its back, and begins to walk. Dromm comes up out of the Trench with the sea inside it, and the Tenanted turn toward it as one. Iss opens its eye in the long room and every sleeper in the Dormitory sits up together. Vey is born out of the highlands. Tolm speaks through every Sayer at once. They have been in the dark for six hundred and forty-one years, cut up and swallowed and digested and held, and they remember exactly who ate them.

**What the characters can do.** Bargain, briefly, with a god that is only half itself and very hungry. Try to reach the Fast, the one land with no god to come home to. Stand between a god and its people and be the first thing it notices.

**The last image.** The gods walking the Rim Road, slowly, in the way they used to walk among their peoples, and the peoples of the Table lying in rows at their feet, as the eldest once lay, and this time the gods take them.

### It Is Fixed

*The Sayer's Sentence, spoken.*

**Signs.** The Sayer's Sentence thread reaches its fourth stage. The Bench has drafted, every land has bid, and the text runs to four hundred conditions. Its first and last line, the only one that matters, reads: *It is fixed.*

**The last act.** Lady Imre Dath's golden bit is removed in the Gilded Cells before the Bench, the envoys and the Mute King. She says it. What a Sayer says, is. Every Regrowth on the Table stops, at once, exactly where it stands. So does everything else. *Fixed* in Table-speech means mended, and it also means set fast, and Tolm has always taken words at their plainest. Nothing on the Table can change from that moment. The Kept do not rot further. The hungry are not fed. The sleepers do not wake and the waking do not sleep. Labor in Cradlewrack stops between one pang and the next and does not resume. The sea stands where it is. The rain will not come, and will not fail to come. Characters stop between one breath and the next.

**What the characters can do.** If a character is in the room, they get the time it takes for the sentence to leave her mouth: a single action, chosen before the first word. They might reach a hand toward someone. They might close their eyes. Whatever they choose, they will be doing it forever.

**The last image.** The whole Table as a still life, a painted meal: every person exactly as they were, aware, unable to blink, under a sun that does not move. And at Hallowboard, a Host frozen mid-stoop over a table it was laying, one hand on the back of the eighth chair, which it had been about to pull out.

### The Long Table

*Everyone accepts.*

**Signs.** Orrum's tables spread beyond counting. The Host on the Road thread reaches its fourth stage. The Calling-Cards appear in every land with every name. Pilgrims stream into the Fast. The Fatlands' starving go first, then the Vigil's sleepless, then the Kept, who can sit at Orrum's tables only on the fen side of the Drop and do. The Unfed go last. They hold out for the longest. Then the Abstinent, starving, meet in the Pantry, and Elder Forbear Wend says *Yes, please*, once, and everyone hears it.

**The last act.** There is no last act. There is only sitting down. One by one, then by villages, then by lands, the people of the Table take their places at the Host's tables, where the Guest cannot touch them, and eat from empty plates, and praise the food, and smile, and do not get up. The Guest arrives to a world where every person is someone else's guest and there is nothing it is permitted to eat, and so it sits in its chair at Hallowboard, the only diner at the Table without a plate, and waits. It is very patient. It is hunger.

**What the characters can do.** Sit, or be the last ones standing. A character who refuses to the very end inherits the world: empty fields, empty roads, tables in every house, and somewhere in the middle of it a Guest that is waiting, and is eating the only food the Host is not protecting.

**The last image.** A table nine hundred miles long, around the whole Table, along the Rim, with everyone who ever lived on it seated, wasting, delighted. The grace said and said again, forever, over a meal that never comes. *Lack keep away. Lack keep away.* The Long Grace, kept.

### The Unhosting

*The Second Table eats.*

**Signs.** The Second Table thread reaches its fourth stage. Wont Bellamy's motion passes, or is not needed. On Tablenight the Second Table gathers at Hallowboard with eight knives of grey bone, and the Host lies down on the eight-sided table, as it lay down once before, and looks at them with the look of someone who has heard the door.

**The last act.** They eat Orrum. It does not fight. Diners from every land, every one of them a High Cut, cut and chew and swallow the god of the shared table, and as they do, every hearth on the Table goes out at once, and every table in every house is, suddenly, only furniture. There is no longer any such thing as a host. There is no longer any such thing as a guest, in the old sense, bound by the rules of a house. There is only hunger, and a world with nobody to keep it in its chair. The Guest comes. It does not sit. There is nowhere for it to sit. It eats the Second Table first, as an appetizer, because they are full of gods; then it eats the gods in their lands; then it eats the lands themselves: every field, road, body and season, every beginning, until there is nothing for anything to begin again from.

**What the characters can do.** Be at Hallowboard, and try to stop it, and fail. Take a knife and eat, and be among the first eaten. Or go as far from the middle as the Table allows, to the edge of the sea or the salt, and watch it come.

**The last image.** Not a ruin. Nothing so solid. A plate licked clean, under a Plate licked clean: a round bare place where the Table was, with an eight-sided stone in the middle, wiped spotless, and beside it, pushed in, eight chairs.
