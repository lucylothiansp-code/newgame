# The Long Grace

> We were hungry. Say it plainly. We were hungry, and they were there.
>> — first line of every history written on the Table

## Prologue: The Night of Seven Tables

Nobody agrees whether it was one night or a whole black season of nights. Every people remembers it as one. These are seven of the memories, set down in the words of the people who keep them, and arranged in the order the Rim Road Company's chroniclers prefer, which is clockwise, ending at the middle, because that is where every road on the Table ends whether it means to or not.

### I. The Fens

[fiction]
Nobody wanted to be first. That is the whole of it, and the fen-folk tell it as if it were a virtue.

Ossel lay where it had lain down, half in the black water and half on the reed-bank below the place that would one day be Lastgate. It was grey the way fog is grey, long the way a road is long, and its hands lay open on the peat, palms up, each one wide enough to cup a cottage. We stood around it with knives we had ground from the shoulder-blades of cattle that had died in the fifth year. There were thousands of us. You could hear stomachs. You could hear the fog dripping off the reeds.

Every so often, while we stood there, one of the children at the back of the crowd died. That was ordinary then. You heard a mother make the sound and then stop making it. And each time, one of those great hands lifted, slow as a heron, reached over our heads without touching anyone, and closed the child's eyes with a fingertip the size of a loaf. Then it settled back onto the peat and lay open again.

It did that eleven times before anyone moved.

The one who moved was Agna Corse, who was ninety and had buried four husbands and nine children and was dying where she stood. She did not cut the god. She asked her grandson to. She said, I will not last till morning and I would like to have eaten something. He took the smallest finger of the left hand, the last joint, and it came away like the stalk of a mushroom, and inside it was cold and pale and wet and smelled of cellars and turned earth and the inside of a closed box. She ate it raw, with her gums. She did not die that night. She did not die the next night either. She is, by some accounts, still upright in a Lastgate loft, and asks after her grandson, who has been sunk in the Sump for five hundred years.

After her it went easier. It always does. We ate it slowly, because it was cold and because we were ashamed, and because every time a knife went in the hand nearest would lift a little, as if to close our eyes, and then think better of it and lie down. The families who took the hands sat apart and ate them in silence, joint by joint, cracking the knuckles with stones. Their lips went grey by morning. We bowed to them for it.

There was one boy, they say, a peat-cutter's son, who stood at the back the whole night and never ate. He was staring past the god, over its shoulder, at the empty bank on the far side of the fire, where nobody stood because there was nothing there to stand near. Just before dawn he made a small sound, and turned, and ran out into the fen in the dark, and we heard the water take him. Nobody went after him. We were eating.
[/fiction]

### II. The Plains

[fiction]
We were fastest. Say that too. Say it proudly; we do.

Ummer the Laden came in off the stubble on the last evening of the ninth Carving and lay down on the great threshing floor at Brawn Tilth like an ox that has decided. It was the size of a barn and it smelled the way an oven smells when you open it, and the starving went toward that smell before anyone had spoken a word, on their hands and knees, the old ones crawling, the young ones running on legs like rake-handles.

There was no ceremony. There was no first cut, because there were a thousand first cuts. People bit it. I want you to understand: before the knives came out, people had their faces in its side and were biting, and the skin gave like the crust of a loaf, and under the crust it was fat, yellow, a hand's depth of it, warm as a body, and under the fat it was meat that bled gravy. Children were pushed in to the shoulder. Men pulled ribs apart with their arms and handed the meat back over their heads to whoever was behind. A woman sat in the hollow of its flank with her baby and chewed and put the chewed meat in the baby's mouth with her tongue, chewed and gave, chewed and gave, crying the whole time, and the baby lived, and that baby's grandchildren's grandchildren sit on the Board of Plenty today.

The Gut-line families got there first because they were nearest the belly. They opened it and grain came out. That is what is remembered: the belly opened and the threshing floor was knee-deep in grain in a heartbeat, a wave of it, hot, smelling of summer, and in that grain were small white things like teeth, which we took for husks. We ate those too. Nobody asked.

By midnight the big bones were bare. By the second hour we were cracking them on the floor-stones for the marrow, and the noise of that was like a forest coming down. By dawn there was nothing on the threshing floor but grease and grain and people asleep in it, curled up, fat-faced, with their mouths still moving. The god had not made a sound. It had not stopped us. Old women say its eyes were open the whole time, and kind, and that it watched us eat it the way a mother watches.

The hunger did not stop. We noticed that around noon. We were full to the throat and the hunger had not stopped. It still has not.
[/fiction]

### III. Longslab

[fiction]
Dromm would not come up on its own. The others lay down; the Fathom had to be fetched.

It took ten thousand hooks. Every family on the coast brought every hook it owned and hammered more from door-hinges and pot-handles, and we rowed them out over the deep place in boats with nothing in their holds, and we set them, and we hauled. The ropes sang. Men's hands opened to the bone on them and they wrapped the bone in sailcloth and hauled again. It came up out of the dark slowly over three days, a shape like a reef that breathed, and it did not fight us, but the sea did: the water around it thickened until boats could not row through it, and the pressure of it pressed our ears until they bled in threads down our jaws.

We dragged it onto the shingle at Longslab on the third evening. It was grey and slick and vast and its skin moved in long slow ripples like the surface of the water it had left. It had no face you could find. It had an enormous slow breathing, a sound like surf turned inside out, and every breath it took pulled the tide a little further out from under the boats.

It took four days to stop moving. We did not wait for it to stop. We were too hungry for that. The families who would be the Lung-lines cut their way in under the ribs on the second day and found the lungs, which were the size of chapels, folded and grey and full of cold water, and they ate them in the water, standing in the god, chest-deep in the brine that poured out of it. One girl put her whole head inside a lobe to drink and came out and said she had heard the sea from the inside, and that it was talking. She walked into the water a week later, the first of all the Called, though nobody had a name for it then.

The beach has never dried. Stand on Longslab in the hottest week of Plenty and the shingle is wet under your soles and the wet is warm. On the fourth night, when it finally stopped moving, every one of us woke at the same moment coughing salt water. We thought it was grief.
[/fiction]

### IV. Ludmere

[fiction]
Hush. That was the whole instruction. Hush, and do not wake it.

Iss the Lidded slept in the high valley above Ludmere, where it had always slept, curled on its side like a hound, as long as the valley, with its head on its paws and its great lids shut. It had never been seen awake. Its breathing was the weather of that country: slow, warm, regular, the grass along the valley floor lying down and rising up again with each breath. We had slept beside it all our lives, and dreamed well, and in the famine years we had dreamed of food, nightly, until the dreams were the only food we had.

So we came up the valley in the dark, in stocking feet, with our knives wrapped in wool so they would not clink. Mothers sang. That is what we kept from that night: the lullabies. A thousand women singing low, so the god would go on sleeping while we opened it. The knives went in to the singing. The blood came out warm and slow and smelled of linen and milk and the hollow of a lover's neck, and the people who drank it yawned, all of them, and had to be slapped awake to go on eating.

It never woke. Write that down and underline it. It never stirred, never flinched, never sighed. We took the flesh from the haunch and it went on breathing. We took the heart and it went on breathing for an hour without one. Near dawn the families who would be the Eye-lines climbed the face with ropes and cut out its eyes, still shut, lids and lashes and all, each one the size of a cartwheel, and lowered them down, and sliced them like bread, and ate them closed. The lids were the best part, they said after. Like cream.

Some of us dozed, toward the end, full and warm against the cooling side. And some of those who dozed dreamed of a long dim room, and woke weeping, and could not say why. We put it down to the occasion.

The breathing stopped at sunrise. The valley grass lay down flat, and has never quite stood up again.
[/fiction]

### V. The Red Hills

[fiction]
Vey was the smallest of them and it would not stay killed. Every midwife in the highlands was called up to the round hill to help, and the midwives tell it best, so this is how they tell it.

It was the size of a calf when it lay down, a soft red thing with a hundred small closed openings in its skin like buds, and its warmth came off it in waves you could see in the frost. A Caul-wife of Kest put her knife in under the breastbone, cleanly, the way you open a sheep, and it died. And then it opened. The wound became a mouth, and the mouth drew breath, and out of it slid Vey again, smaller, wet, and new, and lay down on the grass beside its own body and waited.

We killed it seven times that night. Each time we killed it, it began again out of the death, out of the cut, out of the place where the knife came out. By the fourth time we were eating as we killed, fast, so that what we had cut would be swallowed before it could begin. By the sixth the women were holding the openings shut with their hands while the men cut, and the openings pushed at their palms like a child's head pushing. The meat was hot and sweet and tasted of iron and new milk, and those who ate it felt their own bodies clench and loosen and clench in the belly, all of them, men and grandmothers, a pain like a door being tried from the other side.

The seventh time it was very small. The size of a hand. A woman named Harrow Bede, who had lost nine in the Lack and buried every one in the red clay with her own fingers, took it up in her palm and looked at it for a long while. Then she put the whole of it in her mouth and swallowed, and held her jaw shut with both hands until it stopped moving, and then she sat down in the clay and laughed until she was sick.

The families who ate the caul wore it out of the hill on their faces like veils, wet and red in the torchlight, and it did not come off in the morning. Behind us, on the empty hilltop, a door stood in a frame with no house around it, open a crack. Nobody had brought it there.
[/fiction]

### VI. The Canyons

[fiction]
...

Nothing.

They ate it without speaking, and the telling of it in Oathen is also without speaking, so this is set down by an outsider who was told it with hands.

Tolm the Witness lay at the bottom of the deepest canyon with its ear to the stone. It was long, and bare, and listening. It was always listening; everything said in front of it was so, and the people of the canyons had learned over ten thousand years to say little near it and to mean all of it. In the Lack they had come to its ear and said we are hungry, and it was so; they had said we will die, and that was so; and they had stopped coming.

On the night, the whole people came down the canyon walls on ropes in silence, carrying their children with leather straps over their mouths. They spread out along its length in silence. They raised the knives in silence. The only sound was the knives. The god heard the knives, and that was so.

They ate. Imagine a hundred thousand people chewing in a canyon that repeats everything. The echo of the eating went up the walls and came down again and went up again for the whole night, a wet enormous patience, and the god lay with its ear to the rock and heard it, and heard it, and heard it.

A boy, partway through, stopped chewing. He looked up at his mother with his mouth full and his eyes wide, and drew a breath to say something. She put her hand over his mouth. She did not take it away until dawn. When she did, the boy had chewed through the side of his own cheek trying to keep from speaking, and she held a strip of the god's tongue against the wound until it closed. The families who ate the tongue sat in a circle, cutting it into slivers no bigger than a fingernail, and laid each sliver on their own tongues, and closed their mouths, and let it dissolve. They say it was the only thing any of them ever tasted that had no taste at all.

Nobody spoke for a year afterward. The first word said aloud in the canyons after the Supper was a merchant's "I swear," three springs on, and you know what happened to his hands.
[/fiction]

### VII. Hallowboard

[fiction]
We are the people who said no. Teach it so. Our children learn it before their own names.

Orrum the Host lay on the table at Hallowboard, on the bare hill at the middle of the world, the eight-sided stone table that is always clean. It lay as the others lay, quiet, warm, its great stooped body folded along the board, and the board was the right size for it, as if it had been cut to measure. It smelled of bread. All of it. The whole hill smelled of bread fresh from the oven, and of woodsmoke and of the inside of a kitchen on a winter evening when everyone you love is home.

We stood around it and we did not eat. We stood in the cold with our children on our shoulders, skin over bone, and the god lay on its table and waited, and we said, as we say now, No, thank you. And again, No, thank you. And again. Nine in ten of us died where they stood that winter, or on the walk home, or in their houses with the smell of bread still in their noses. We did not eat. Remember it. We are the only clean people in the world.

Remember also, because the elders do not forbid it, how long the walk to Hallowboard was. The heartland is wide, and we were weak, and the snow lay deep on the old roads. Remember that when the first of us came over the last ridge, the fires on the far horizons, where the other peoples were, had already burned down to red. Remember that the god on the table opened its eyes when it heard us, and looked at us, and its look was the look of someone who has waited a very long time for guests and has just heard the door.

Remember that it turned its great head, once, and looked at the eighth side of the table, where none of us stood, where the snow had not settled, where nothing was. Remember that it looked there for a long while. Remember that when it looked back at us, it was afraid.

Then it got up. It got up off the board, slow and stooping, the way an old man gets up from a chair. It stood over us in the dark, warm, enormous, smelling of supper. And it began to lay the table.

No, thank you. No, thank you. No, thank you.
[/fiction]

## Why "the Long Grace"

On every table on the continent, before the first mouthful, someone says the words: *Lack keep away.* It is the shortest grace in the world and the only one every people shares. It is said quickly, with the eyes down, the way you touch a doorframe for luck. It is the pause between sitting down and eating.

The poets of the Rim Road began calling the age itself by that name around the third century, half in mockery: six peoples had sat down to the biggest meal in history, and somehow they had not yet finished saying grace over it. The joke was that the meal was eaten in a night, and the bill had not come. Priests took the phrase up a century later without the joke. To them it meant what it sounds like it means: that the six hundred and forty-one years since the Gorging are not an age at all but a held breath, a little ceremonial pause, and that the real eating has not yet begun.

Scholars of the Cutwrights' College use the term precisely. The Long Grace, in their ledgers, is the span between the swallowing of the Providers and the completion of their Regrowth: a finite period, with a measurable rate, approaching its end. Every land's chronicle in the last decade has started using the phrase in the past tense by accident. "In the Long Grace, my grandmother kept bees." Nobody corrects it. Everyone hears it.

In common speech, "the Grace is ending" is what you say when the milk turns, when the dog will not come in, when the neighbor's daughter starts bailing at the wrong hour. It is said lightly. It is meant the other way.

## The Providers

They were not distant. That is the first thing every child is told and the last thing any adult understands. The gods of the old world did not live on mountaintops or behind the sky. Each lived bodily among one people, immense and present, the way a mountain is present: you could walk to it, you could put your hand on it, you could smell it on the wind on a warm day. It had a temperature. It shed. It had moods you could read the way farmers read cloud.

There were eight. Seven are named in every carving older than the Gorging, standing or lying in a ring, facing inward, around something that is never shown. The eighth place in the ring is always there, and always empty, because the figure in it has been cut away.

### Living Beside a God

A Provider did not rule in any sense a magistrate would recognize. It did not make laws or demand prayers. It simply did what it was, enormously, and the people who lived in its shadow arranged their lives around the fact. Fen-folk did not have to bury their dead, because Ossel closed them, and what Ossel closed went quietly into the peat and was clean in a season. Plains-folk did not have to fear a bad harvest, because Ummer walked the fields. Canyon-folk did not need courts, because a thing said in Tolm's hearing was so, and liars learned to keep away from its ear.

The cost was intimacy. You lived with a god the way you live with weather in a small house. You heard it at night. You smelled it in your bread. Children were sent to sit in its shade and came home with its dust on their clothes. Sweethearts met in its lee. People died against its flank in old age, deliberately, the way an old dog crawls under the stove. Every people had words for the god's breathing, the god's stillness, the god turning over in the night. Every people believed, without needing to say so, that the god knew each of them by name.

Worship, in the old world, was mostly feeding. Every people brought their Provider a portion of what the Provider had given them: the first sheaf, the first catch, the first cry of a newborn, the first word of a child. It was called *the giving back*, and in Table-speech the verb for it is the same as the verb for eating. The gods ate what they were given, slowly, over days. Nobody remembers them ever asking for more. Nobody remembers them ever asking for anything at all.

### Ossel, the Closing Hand

God of death and endings. It lay in the northern fens for as long as anyone remembered, long and grey and still as a fallen ash, with hands that moved when nothing else did. Its iconography is two open hands, palms up, and in later carvings a single hand drawing a lid down over an eye. Fen-folk wore a grey thumbprint on the brow at funerals and pressed their own thumbs to the eyelids of the dead in imitation of it. When someone died, the family carried them to the god on a reed hurdle and laid them by a hand, and the hand closed their eyes and their mouth and folded their arms, and then the body was sunk in clean black water and was gone in a season, bones and all, leaving the water sweet. People who lived near Ossel were calm about everything. They knew how things went. An old fen saying, from before: *The Hand comes for the candle when the candle is done.* Nobody now alive in Sallowreach can say it without weeping.

### Ummer, the Laden

God of harvest and plenty. It walked, unlike most of the others: a vast, slow, broad-backed shape like a laden haywain made of earth and bark and pelt, ambling across the southern plains with its head down, and the fields bowed toward it as it passed and rose up heavier behind it. Its iconography is a full belly with a sheaf rising out of it, and a mouth, always a mouth, smiling. Plains-folk followed its track with seed-bags and sowed in its footprints, which never failed. They thanked it by name at every meal, as they still thank the fields, and the first loaf of every baking was set on the doorstep for it, and in the morning the loaf was gone and the doorstep was warm. It was the most loved of the gods and the most taken for granted. Children rode on it.

### Dromm, the Fathom

God of the deep sea, of pressure, of what is kept under. Nobody living saw more than its back, a long grey reef far out past the shelf that broke the surface on the stillest days and breathed. Its iconography is a closed circle with lines pressing in from every side, and the oldest coastal carvings show it as a hand holding something down beneath the waves. The coast gave it the first fish of every boat and the bodies of the drowned, rowed out with lanterns and let down on weighted ropes. In return the sea was full and the storms broke on its back before they reached the shore. Divers who went deep came up with nosebleeds and a feeling of being enormously, patiently watched. The old coast-folk said Dromm kept the sea's secrets for it, everything that had ever sunk, and would never give a single one of them up.

### Iss, the Lidded

God of sleep. It lay curled in the high valley above old Ludmere with its lids shut, and no one in recorded history ever saw its eyes open. Its iconography is a closed eye, two curved lines, sometimes with a row of small figures lying asleep along the lower lid. Worship in Ludmere was dreaming: oracles slept against the god's flank and woke with prophecy, and families brought their dreams to the temple-keepers each morning to be written down and offered. Sleep was holy and easy; the Ludmere bed was a famous luxury, exported up and down the old roads. People who lived near Iss were the best-rested and most reasonable people in the world. It is a small horror the Vigil does not dwell on that its ancestors were famous for their naps.

### Vey, the Opening

God of birth and beginnings, of doors and first breaths. The smallest Provider, the size of a heifer, soft and red and warm and always, somehow, new. It moved from house to house in the highlands when a birth was near and lay across the threshold. Its iconography is a doorway with something small just stepping through, and in the oldest stones a round opening ringed by petals or teeth. Highland women gave birth with one hand on the god. They gave it the caul, the cord, and the first breath of each child, which the midwife caught in her cupped hands and blew into Vey's nearest opening. Nothing was ever born wrong in the old highlands. Nothing was born too early. Doors stayed shut until they were opened, and when they were opened the god was usually standing on the other side.

### Tolm, the Witness

God of oaths and true speech. It lay along the floor of the deepest canyon in the east with one ear pressed to the stone, long and bare and grey-pink, listening. What it heard was so. Its iconography is an ear, sometimes an ear inside an open mouth, and in the canyon carvings a long figure with a finger to its own lips. Canyon-folk did not worship it so much as consult it: contracts were spoken into its ear, marriages were said there, and the guilty were brought to say their innocence in its hearing and see what happened. Parents warned children not to say *I could die* near the canyon floor. There was very little crime and almost no poetry. Visitors found the canyon peoples' honesty so frightening that the word for an Oathener in two other dialects still means *someone you cannot sit near*.

### Orrum, the Host

God of the hearth, of welcome, of the shared table. It lived in the rich heartland at the middle of the Table, very tall, stooping under doorways, warm, and it went from house to house. When it came to a door the hearth inside lit itself and the table was laid. Its iconography is a table with a single figure bent over it, laying it, and on the oldest stones an open hand holding out a bowl. The heartland was the richest country in the world because it was the most generous: every traveler was fed, every stranger seated, every quarrel settled over bread. Orrum was the god of the other seven too, in a way. The great carvings show all eight facing inward around a table, and the figure that pulls out the chairs is always Orrum.

### The Eighth

There is an eighth place in every carving older than the Gorging. In the ring of figures around the table, between the Host and the Closing Hand, there is a gap exactly the width of one of the others. Sometimes it is a smooth recess. Sometimes the stone is gouged and pitted. Sometimes the chisel marks are fine and tidy and sometimes they are frantic, a mess of strokes cut over strokes, as if whoever did it could not stop. In Brinehollow the stone was hacked with a boathook. In the Vigil it was ground out with a rasp so patiently that the recess shines. Analysis by the Cutwrights' College in 588 A.G. established that the removals were done by different tools, in different centuries, some of them before the Gorging and some of them after.

Nobody knows its name. Nobody knows its people. Nobody knows who ate it, or whether anyone did. No land claims it, and every land has, at some point in its history, made it a crime to ask. The question is called, on the Rim Road, *counting the chairs*. It is considered very bad luck.

A few things can still be noticed by those who look closely. In the carvings, the seven named figures are always shown facing inward toward the table, except that in perhaps one carving in ten, one or two of them have their heads very slightly turned toward the gap. On the oldest temple floors, in the places where worshipers would have stood, the stone in front of the eighth recess is unworn. In Table-speech there are two words for *guest*, and the older one has no plural, and no dictionary on the Table will print it.

## The Long Lack

> Nobody knows whether the Lack was a punishment, an accident, or a plan. That is the oldest wound in the world.
>> — preface to the College's *Concordance of the Nine Years*

For nine years, nothing began. That is the shortest true account. What follows is a longer one, assembled from the chronicles of all seven peoples, from the tally-sticks of granaries, from bone-pits, and from a great many songs that are no longer sung in company.

### The First Year: the Thin Harvest

It began in Grace, with seed. Across every land at once, the spring sowing did not come up. Not poorly: not at all. Farmers dug up their furrows in the second month and found the seed exactly where they had put it, swollen, soft, and rotting, with no shoot and no root. The second sowing did the same. The Providers were consulted and gave no answer, but that was not yet alarming; the Providers had never given answers. Ummer walked the fields as always and the fields did not bow. People said it was a bad year. The granaries were full from the year before. Nobody went hungry that winter, and the Tablenight feasts, which were then a celebration of the longest night, were held as usual.

### The Second Year: the Empty Nets

The nets came up empty along the whole western coast in the first week of Plenty and did not fill again. Hens stopped laying. Cows dried. Orchards flowered and the flowers fell and nothing set. The rationing began in the towns, and with it the first bread riots, and the first appearance of the phrase that would become the whole world's grace: *Lack keep away*, muttered over thinned porridge. In the north, Ossel's hands were seen to move restlessly at night. In the heartland, Orrum went from house to house as always, and the hearths lit, and the tables were laid, and there was less and less to put on them.

### The Third Year: the Barren Stalls

Livestock went barren. No calf was dropped on the whole Table that year, and no lamb, and no foal. Then the herds began to be eaten, against every law, because a cow that will not calve is only meat. The old breeds died out that winter, all of them, in a single season of slaughter. The Providers grew visibly thinner. Plains-folk measured Ummer's flank against a barn wall and marked it in chalk, and marked it again each month, and the marks moved inward. The great temples began demanding larger gifts, and got them, and the gods ate them slowly as always and grew no fatter.

### The Fourth Year: the Ash Bread

The flour went wrong. Grain that had been stored sound for years began to turn in the bins, and bread baked from it came out of the oven as grey ash in the shape of a loaf, which fell to dust when touched. Nobody has ever explained this. Bakers were hanged for it in three lands. Then the women stopped conceiving. It was noticed first in the highlands, where Vey lay across thresholds and no labor came; then everywhere. The last child born in the Lack is recorded in the Cradlewrack Tally as a girl delivered at Kest in the fourth Carving, who died on her third day. After her there were none for five years. The Providers stopped speaking that winter: not that they had ever spoken much, but the sounds stopped, the breathing grew shallow, the moods ceased to be readable. They lay still and grew thin.

### The Fifth Year: the Leather Winter

The fifth year was when people began to eat the things that are not food. Harness. Shoe leather. Boiled glue. Bark, then the inner bark, then the wood. Grass soup. Clay with a little salt. Lamp oil. In the cities, the rat-catchers became rich and then were eaten by the people they had sold to. The old and the very young began to die in numbers that overwhelmed every rite; in Sallowreach, Ossel's hands lay still and the dead piled at its side unclosed, and the fen-folk learned for the first time what a corpse does when no god tends it. In the Fatlands, people followed Ummer across the bare fields on their knees, licking its footprints. Ummer did not stop. It walked more slowly than anyone had ever seen it walk.

### The Sixth Year: the Kitchen Year

This is the year the songs leave out.

It began with the dead. A family would lose a grandmother in the night, and in the morning the grandmother would not be carried out. The pot would be put on. There are chronicles from all seven peoples that say the same thing in the same flat way: *in the sixth year the dead were kept at home*. The first laws against it were passed in every land, and none of them were enforced, because the magistrates had grandmothers too.

Then it went further. The College has assembled, from coroners' rolls and the confessions of the old taken down in the first decades after the Gorging, a picture it publishes only in cipher. Households drew lots. Children were sent to stay with cousins and the cousins sent theirs back, so that no one would have to eat their own. The phrase for this, still known in Fatlands dialect as a curse, is *trading tables*. In the Oathen canyons, where a word spoken could not be unsaid, families made solemn promises to one another that they would not, and then had to watch the promises take their hands off. In Brinehollow a man rowed his dead wife out to the deep place to give her to Dromm, as was proper, and could not let go of her, and came back with her, and the town watched him carry her up the shingle and did not say a word, and that night every chimney on the street smoked.

The smell of that year, survivors said, was the smell of a cooking-pot in a house where nothing has been cooked in months: so sudden and so rich that it brought the neighbors to the door. They would stand outside in the snow and not knock. They would just stand there, breathing it.

[fiction]
I will tell it once, and then I will have told it. We had Hessa three days after. Mother said it was what Hessa would have wanted and then she went out to the yard and was sick on the snow and came back in and stirred the pot. The fat came up yellow on the top. I remember the color. I remember that it smelled like supper and that I hated it for that more than anything. I remember that father said the grace first, Lack keep away, the way you say it over company, because she was company now. I ate. Everyone ate. We were alive in the spring. I have had children since, and they have had children, and I have never once let any of them see me eat.
[/fiction]
>> — deposition of Werra Hask, taken at Sated, 61 A.G.; the deponent was eighty-two

### The Seventh Year: the Bone Dole

Governments, such as they were, took over the dying. In every land, the dead were collected by the authorities and rendered in public kitchens, and the meat was given out by ration, and nobody asked whose it was. It was, the chronicles say, the most orderly year of the Lack. The first lacks were cut that winter: discs of salt-cured bone, stamped, each one good for a day's ration at the public kitchen. The bone was the bone of the dead. The coin has been made from other bone since, officially. Its value has never changed: one day's bread. People stopped fighting over food in the seventh year, because there was a system, and a queue, and a coin, and that is the year the old world truly ended.

The Providers lay down. All of them, everywhere, within days of one another. Ummer stopped walking at last in the middle of a bare field and folded its legs. Orrum, in the heartland, stopped going from house to house and sat on the bare hill at Hallowboard beside the eight-sided table, with its head bowed, as if waiting for someone to arrive.

### The Eighth Year: the Eldest Walk

In the spring of the eighth year, the priests of all seven peoples met at Hallowboard. It was the only time in history all seven sat together, and the only record of the meeting is a list of resolutions carved into the hill's foot-stone, worn almost smooth. They had come to read the carvings on the table's eight sides, which were older than any of them, and they found, or believed they found, a demand. The carvings showed, beneath the ring of figures, a line of smaller figures walking toward the table with their arms at their sides. The priests read it as a tithe: the first of every house. The gods had given each house its firstborn; the gods were starving; the gods wanted the first back.

By common counsel of all seven peoples, it was done. In the last month of Plenty, in the eighth year of the Lack, the firstborn adult of every hearth on the Table walked to the feet of their people's god.

They walked, mostly, of their own will. That is the part every chronicle insists on. They were sons and daughters of twenty, of forty, of sixty; they were the heirs of farms and shops and fishing boats; they kissed their younger siblings and handed over their tools and walked out of their villages in long lines, singing, with the whole village walking behind them to the boundary stone and stopping there. They walked for days. They were very thin. Many died on the road. The rest arrived.

The rite was the same everywhere because the priests had agreed it. At the god's feet, each firstborn knelt, and a priest opened their throat with a bronze knife, and laid them down against the god, as close as could be got. In the fens they were laid in rows between Ossel's open hands. In the plains they were laid along Ummer's folded legs until it was hidden to the knees. At Longslab they were rowed out to the deep place and let down on weighted ropes in their thousands. In the canyons each one whispered a vow into Tolm's ear before the knife: *I give myself, so that they may eat*, and that was so, or should have been. In the red hills they were laid across thresholds. At Ludmere they were laid along the sleeping flank like children put to bed. At Hallowboard, the heartland's firstborn were laid on the stone table, which was big enough for all of them, in a heap.

The count was never made. The College's best estimate is somewhere above sixty thousand.

Nothing happened.

The gods did not take them. The gods did not stir. Ossel's hands did not close a single pair of eyes; the dead lay in the fen staring upward until the birds that were left found them. Ummer did not eat. Tolm, which heard every vow, did not make one of them so. The bodies lay where they had been laid, through the end of Plenty and into Carving, against the flanks of gods who did not move, and nothing rose from the fields, and nothing came to the nets, and no woman quickened.

And in Carving, when it was clear, the people who had walked their eldest to the gods' feet came back for them with sacks, at night, and did not look one another in the face, and carried them home.

[box: The Eldest Walk in Play]
The Eldest Walk is the act no land discusses and every land remembers. It is why the Gorging happened. Before the Walk, the peoples of the Table believed the gods were starving with them and could be fed. After it, they knew that their gods would take nothing, give nothing, and save no one. Their most precious offering had been laid at the feet of every Provider in the world and been left to rot. The Gorging was not an act of hunger alone. It was an act of fury. Every land has a quiet name for it (the Tithe, the Long Line, the Giving of the First) and every land has, somewhere in its oldest families, a story of the eldest who did not go, or who went and came back. In the present day the Purgation preaches a second Walk, and the phrase *to walk the eldest* means, in every dialect, to make a sacrifice you know will be wasted.
[/box]

### The Ninth Year: the Turning

The last winter was the quietest. There was nothing left to fight over. The dead were not collected anymore, because the collectors were dead. In every land the survivors sat in their houses in the dark and looked at their gods out of the windows, and the gods lay where they had lain down, enormous, gaunt, still, with the bones of the eldest at their feet.

Nobody knows who said it first. Every people claims a different first speaker, and in every account the first speaker is a mother. What she said, in every version, comes to the same thing: *They have something left on them, and we have nothing. They would not eat what we gave. Let us see if they will be eaten.*

The knives were already ground. People had been grinding knives for nine years, out of habit, for want of anything else to do with their hands.

## The Gorging

Whether it happened on one night or across a season is disputed. The stories call it the Night of Seven Tables, and Tablenight is kept on the longest night of Lack in every land, but the chronicles do not agree. Brinehollow's dragging took three days and its god four more to die. Sallowreach ate last of all and slowest, and some fen accounts stretch its meal over a fortnight. Oathen's Silent Supper, because no one spoke of it, has no recorded date at all. The Cutwrights' College, which has tried to reconcile them for four centuries, settled in 501 A.G. on the formula *the ninth winter, within a single Plate*, meaning one cycle of the moon, and has quietly lost every argument about it since.

What is agreed is this. In every land, the people went to their god with knives, and their god did not fight. The Providers did not flee, did not cry out, did not strike, did not so much as turn over. They were enormous; a twitch of Ummer's flank would have crushed a village; a breath from Dromm drowned whole fleets in the old days. They held still. It is recorded everywhere and explained nowhere, and every argument in the world, sooner or later, becomes an argument about why.

Each god was butchered and shared out, and how it was shared became the shape of every society since. The families nearest, strongest, quickest, or most ruthless took the choicest parts: the hands, the gut, the lungs, the eyes, the caul, the tongue. Those behind them took the meat. Those behind them took what fell. Those at the very back licked the bones. Nobody planned it. Nobody needed to.

The famine ended the next morning.

Every land records this the same way, with the same awe. People woke on the morning after their meal to a green haze on the furrows. Hens laid. By midday the nets were full. Within the week crops stood shoulder high in fields that had been sown nine years earlier with seed that had rotted in the ground. Women quickened that month, all of them, all at once, the old and the very young and some who had long been past it. Cattle that had been barren for six years dropped calves within the season, which should not be possible. Trees that had been cut for firewood put up new shoots from the stumps overnight. The world, which had not begun anything for nine years, began everything at once.

That speed should have frightened people more than it did. Here and there it did. In the Fatlands, where the wheat stood shoulder high in seven days, an old woman named Tallis Brome is recorded as having walked out into the new field on the eighth morning, broken an ear, chewed it, and said to her grandson: *This was not grown. This was poured.* Nobody listened. Everyone was eating.

Only in the Fast did the Lack not lift. The heartland's people, who had not eaten their god, woke to the same bare soil, the same empty coops, the same rotten seed, and looked out over the Rim at their neighbors' green fields with an emotion that has shaped their entire history and that they have never once named correctly.

## The Three Doctrines

Every argument in the world eventually becomes an argument about why the gods held still. There are three answers, each with its temples, its martyrs and its hecklers, and most people on the Table believe a little of each depending on how the harvest went.

### The Theft

*We murdered them. The Regrowth is justice, and we deserve it.* The oldest and most common belief, held by the majority in Sallowreach and Oathen and by most of the poor everywhere. Its priests are called Confessors, and its rites are rites of penance: fasting on the anniversaries of the Meal, self-scourging in the Fatlands, the reading-aloud of ancestral guilt in Brinehollow. The Theft explains the Providers' stillness as shock, or contempt, or the paralysis of the betrayed. It offers no hope and asks for none. Its comfort is that the world makes sense.

[fiction]
You ask why it did not fight. I will tell you why a mother does not fight when her son comes for her with a knife. She is too surprised. She cannot believe it. She stands in her own kitchen with her hands open and looks at him, and she is still looking at him when she goes down. That is how they lay. That is how they looked at us. And now they are coming back up through us, through the gut and the lung and the eye, and you call it a curse? It is a bill. It is the only honest thing that has happened to this family in six hundred years. Pay it. Bow your head and pay it.
[/fiction]
>> — Confessor Abel Rennick, sermon at the Weeping Post, 627 A.G.

### The Gift

*They offered themselves out of love. The Regrowth is a tragedy neither side wanted.* The gentlest doctrine and the most popular among the comfortable: Gut-lines families in the Fatlands, Vigil merchants, the Rim Road's innkeepers. Its priests are called Thankers and its central rite is the Thanksgiving Meal, at which the worshipers eat in silence and then say, together, the names of all seven eaten gods. The Gift reads the Providers' stillness as consent and their Regrowth as an unwilling side effect, a grief shared by eater and eaten. It holds that the gods suffer in the blood as their hosts do, and that kindness to the godsigned eases the god. Its critics call it the doctrine of people who have never been hungry.

[fiction]
Children, think of it this way. Your grandmother is very old and there is no bread. She takes her portion and puts it on your plate when she thinks you are not looking. She says she is not hungry. That is what they did, all of them, on that night: they lay down and they put themselves on our plates and said, eat, we are not hungry. And now they are in us, and they are hurting, and we are hurting, because love that big does not fit inside a body. It was not supposed to come back. It is not their fault. It is not ours. Be gentle with the ones whose eyes do not close. Be gentle with the ones who must be gagged. They are carrying more of the gift than you.
[/fiction]
>> — Thanker Maudlin Ashe, children's homily, Lampwick Halt, 640 A.G.

### The Bait

*They wanted to be eaten. The Lack was theirs.* This is how gods breed: starve a people, be swallowed, and grow back through ten thousand bodies instead of one. The doctrine first appears in the record around 330 A.G., in a pamphlet nailed to a Rim Road toll-house, and has been illegal in four lands (Sallowreach, the Fatlands, the Vigil and Oathen) for most of the time since. It is gaining ground in all seven, because it is the only doctrine that explains the speed of the Week of Rising and the only one that matches the arithmetic of the Regrowth. Its preachers have no order and no title. They are called *Baiters* by their enemies and nothing at all by themselves. Some of them are terrified; some, like the Second Helping of the Fatlands, are joyous; all of them agree that the Gorging was not the end of the gods but their sowing.

[fiction]
Why did it not fight? Because a seed does not fight the ground. Look at your hands. Look at the joints on the Hand-line Regent's fingers, the eyes on the Vigil's lords, the cord on the Uncut, the fat on the Board. Did your great-great-grandmother eat a god, or did a god get into your great-great-grandmother? Nine years without a single child born, and then a whole world of children in one spring, every one of them with the god in the blood. You think that was mercy? It was planting. You are a furrow, friend. We all are. And the harvest is due.
[/fiction]
>> — anonymous Baiter, preaching on the Rim Road at Saltlick Cross shortly before being drowned by a crowd, 638 A.G.

Doctrine is a fine way to start fights between player characters who otherwise agree on everything. Ask each player, at character creation, which of the three their character believes and which their parents believed. The doctrines are not settled by the game; the Secrets chapter offers a default truth and three alternatives built on each doctrine, and the GM may choose. Until then, every sermon is equally true, and equally useless.

## The Regrowth

The return of an eaten god is called the Regrowth, and its marks on a person are called godsign. It moves through four stages, in a person, a family, or an entire country: the Taste, the Appetite, the Course, and the Seating. Each land's Regrowth obeys its own rules and is detailed in that land's chapter. What is shared is the shape, and the speed.

The Taste is small wrongnesses: dreams, cravings, a tell in the body that a lover notices first. The Appetite is compulsion; the body begins wanting what the god wants and will stop at nothing for it. The Course is flesh changing: the person becomes visibly, physically something else, grey-handed or loamy or lidless or veiled. The Seating is the god, in some real portion, present again, wearing a person or a place. Between the Course and the Seating there is a last threshold the College calls *the Brink*, when the person is still a person only by courtesy.

| Land | The Taste | The Appetite | The Course | The Seating |
| Sallowreach | Dread of open things | Compulsive closing | Grey hands, extra joints; touch finishes | A Hush |
| Fatlands | Hunger that wakes you | Eating without choosing | Swelling, rooting, loamy skin | A living hill |
| Brinehollow | Salt at waking | The pull westward | Grey skin, deep eyes, no breath | The Tenant takes the house |
| The Vigil | Heavy lids, spreading yawns | Blinks | The lids are gone | A sleeper begins to move |
| Cradlewrack | About to begin | False labor | True bearing | The land itself in labor |
| Oathen | Your words, echoed | The urge to swear | Plain speech binds | A Sayer |

No land has reached a full Seating of its god. Three are close. And every Regrowth is speeding up: signs that took generations now take years; the Hushes spread, the pangs shorten, the beds fill, the sea drops. The College's figures show that every land's curve bent upward at nearly the same time, in the decade after 600 A.G., which is impossible if the seven Regrowths are separate things. Each government is hiding its own numbers. The College holds all of them.

## Blood and Class: the Cuts

Who your ancestors were matters less than what part they ate. The families who took the choicest portions of a god carry the strongest godsign, and in every land they became the nobility, because in the first generations godsign looked like blessing. A Hand-line touch that could close a wound, a Gut-line appetite that never sickened, an Eye-line who could see in the dark: these were gifts, and their bearers were raised up. By the time the gifts curdled, the Cuts owned everything.

These bloodlines are called **Cuts**, and a person's Cut is the measure of god in their blood, from 0 to 5. The College grades them as follows, and its grades are used in law in every land.

| Cut | Name | Who |
| 0 | Blank | No godsign. Rare births; the Unfed of the Fast |
| 1 | Scrapling | Descended from those who licked the bones |
| 2 | Common | Those who ate the meat; most people alive |
| 3 | Prime | Old gentry; the hungry middle |
| 4–5 | High | Nobility of the choice Cut; the furthest along |

Each land prizes a different Cut: the Hand-lines of Sallowreach, the Gut-lines of the Fatlands, the Lung-lines of Brinehollow, the Eye-lines of the Vigil, the Caul-lines of Cradlewrack, the Tongue-lines of Oathen. Marriage between Cuts is arranged with ledgers. A high-Cut house that marries down dilutes its blood and its claim; a Scrapling who marries up buys rank and a faster death. In every land, the rulers are the most monstrous people in it, and the Regrowth advances fastest in palaces.

### Scraplings

The poor descend from those who got the scraps and licked the bones: the stragglers at the back of the crowd, the too-weak, the too-slow, the ones who arrived after the good parts were gone. They are called **Scraplings**, and they are the least changed people alive. A Scrapling of Sallowreach still cannot die, a Scrapling of the Vigil still cannot sleep, because the Rule of each land touches everyone in it; but their Regrowth crawls where a noble's gallops. Scraplings do the work of the Table: they haul, stitch, bail, nudge and render. They have always been told that their thin blood is the mark of their ancestors' weakness. They are only now, in the last generation, beginning to understand that it is the reason they may outlive their masters, and some of them are beginning to say so out loud.

### Blanks

Rarely, a child is born with no godsign at all. A Fatlands Blank feels no hunger. An Oathen Blank can lie. A Vigil Blank could sleep, if anyone let it. No one knows why they happen; the College's ledgers show no pattern of parentage, and Blanks have been born to High Cuts and to Scraplings alike. They are hunted in every land: as saints, as specimens, or as spare parts.

### The Blank Trade

The trade is old, quiet, and enormously profitable. Blank blood, transfused, is believed to slow the Regrowth for a season. Blank organs, sewn into a noble body by a skilled Seamer or a Fatlands surgeon, are believed to slow it for years. Blank marrow is boiled into a jelly sold in the Vigil as a sleep-cure and in Cradlewrack as a contraceptive. None of it works, or none of it works for long, and the price only rises. A Blank kidney sells on the Rim for about 3,000 lacks; a Blank heart, delivered beating in brine, for a sum the buyers do not write down.

Because the Unfed of the Fast are, in effect, a whole nation of Blanks, the trade's main source is the heartland. Poachers cross the Fast's border by night with nets and sedatives. Fatlanders like Kale Dragoman feed their catch generously on the road home, so the meat arrives in condition. Dr. Mercy Thrane of the Fast has traced vanished patients to surgeries in four countries. The ultimate buyers are, almost always, people of High Cut, and an increasing number of the largest purchases are made through intermediaries who never give a name.

## The Shape of the Table

People call the known world the Table. It is one continent, roughly round, about nine hundred miles across, and its geography is a ring around a hole. Beyond its edges there is sea to the west and wasteland, mountain and salt on the other sides, and nobody who has gone looking for anything beyond has come back with anything worth the trip.

- **The Fast** lies at the center: the rich old heartland, the best farmland on the Table, now almost empty, where the uneaten god still walks. It is always early Carving there.
- **Sallowreach** to the north: grey fen and fog, where nothing dies.
- **The Vigil** to the northeast: a lit plateau city that has not slept in three centuries.
- **Oathen** to the east: dry red canyons where words break bones.
- **The Fatlands** to the south: warm, wet plains of meat.
- **Brinehollow** to the west: a coast the sea is abandoning.
- **Cradlewrack** to the northwest: red clay highlands in labor.

Nobody crosses the middle. The old roads that once ran from every land to the heartland are still there, still beautifully kept by no one, and they are used by Crumb-Runners, Reckoners, poachers, pilgrims and fools. Everyone else goes around.

### The Rim Road

The Rim Road is a ring of highway, some two thousand four hundred miles long, that runs around the border of the Fast, touching every land in turn. It was built in the Thin Years after the Gorging on the foundations of the old heartland ring-roads, by caravaneers who wanted to carry the Fatlands' miraculous grain to every market on the Table without ever setting foot in the empty middle. A wagon caravan takes about a hundred days to make the full circuit; a fast rider changing horses at every post can do it in thirty.

The road is paved for most of its length in great fitted slabs of grey Oathen stone, cambered for drainage, with a ditch on the outer side and, on the inner side, facing the Fast, a low wall about knee-high. The wall is called the Hem. It does nothing; anyone can step over it. Nobody does. Every mile is marked by a milestone with the distance carved in both directions to the Company's headquarters, and every milestone bears the Company's mark, a wheel with seven spokes.

Every land's politics is shaped by needing something from a neighbor it finds obscene, and all of that need moves along the Rim. Fatlands meat goes everywhere. Oathen sells contracts and gold bits. The Vigil buys oil and brine and sells mirrors and rouse. Sallowreach exports wire, lacquer, embalming spirits and, illegally, Closings. Brinehollow sells salvage from the Uncovered. Cradlewrack exports soldiers, the Foundling Companies hiring out as caravan guards. The Fast sells nothing, and buys every mouthful it eats.

#### Tolls

The Rim Road Company owns the road, every inn and post on it, and every toll. Tolls are charged at every waystation, posted in Table-speech on a bone slate by the gate, and paid in lacks or Company platters. A standard schedule:

| Toll | Charged On | Rate |
| Foot | Each traveler on foot | 1 crumb a post |
| Wheel | Each wheel of a cart or wagon | 2 crumbs a post |
| Head | Each animal, or anything walked like one | 1 crumb a post |
| Weight | Cargo, by the hundredweight | 1 lack a post |
| Breath | Any traveler showing godsign at the Course | 5 lacks a post |
| Hush | Corpses, by the body | 1 lack a post |

The Breath Toll was introduced in 604 A.G. after a Course-stage Gut-line rooted himself in the middle of the road near the Chute and had to be dug around. The Hush Toll is so called because it is charged on the dead, who in every land but one are silent.

#### Rim Law

The Company's charter, sworn in Oathen in 71 A.G., grants it jurisdiction over the road surface and a bowshot either side. Within that strip, no land's law runs; Company law runs instead, and Company law has three articles, recited at every post at dawn: *Pay the toll. Keep the peace. Say the grace.* Murder on the road is punished by the Company's wardens with a rope at the nearest post. Theft of Company cargo is punished by being made cargo. Refusing the grace at a Company table, the universal insult, is punished with a fine of one lack and a night outside the gate.

The Rim's neutrality is its whole value. A Sallowreach Closer and a Fast Reckoner can share an inn table on the Rim, and each knows that if the other draws a blade the wardens will hang them before the blood is dry. They cannot share it anywhere else on earth.

### Waystations of the Rim

The Company keeps a post every twenty miles: a toll gate, a stable, a well and a bone-slate board of news. Every hundred miles or so there is a waystation proper, a walled inn-town with a market, a smithy, a wardens' barracks and the Company's factor. Clockwise from the north, the best known are these.

**Fogmouth.** The northern station, where the road from Lastgate meets the Rim. A painted line crosses the road outside the gate, red, renewed every Grace: the Drop. North of it, the Kept walk. South of it, they fall. Families from Sallowreach come to the line to say goodbye to living relatives going abroad, and stand on their side, and hold hands over it. The inn, the Long Farewell, serves vinegar-wine and has a back room where Tam Ruddock's clients wait to cross north. Rooms smell of cedar if you pay for it.

**Lampwick Halt.** At the Vigil's border on the plateau road. Never dark: the Company keeps a hundred lamps burning on the walls to accommodate Vigil merchants, who will not stop in a place they cannot see. Under Vigil custom there are no beds within sight of the plateau, so the Company built the Sleeping Sheds a quarter mile down the road on the Rim side of a stone marker, long low dormitories with shutters, where travelers bound for the Vigil take their last sleep for months. Rouse is sold at the gate. Many travelers lie awake in the Sheds all night anyway, out of nerves.

**Mumchance.** On the canyon road where the Rim enters Oathen. The quietest inn in the world. Orders are taken on slates; the bill is chalked; the innkeeper, a Forsworn woman whose jaw broke on a promise thirty years ago, nods. The walls are hung with felt so nothing echoes. A sign over the door, in four dialects, reads *Say Less*. Foreign travelers are given a printed card of safe phrases on arrival and a lecture by gesture. Every year someone ignores it and leaves on a stretcher.

**Treaty Stair.** A Company depot cut into a canyon wall a day east of Mumchance, at the head of the long stair down to Tacit. Here every Company contract is copied, sealed in wax and bone, and carried down to be sworn before the Company's notary, Jessamy Quill. The Stair is guarded more heavily than any treasury on the Table. Its clerks work in silence. Its archive holds, in theory, the binding text of half the commerce in the world.

**Saltlick Cross.** The southeastern crossroads where the dry country gives way to the Fatlands' first green. The old heartland road meets the Rim here, running straight in toward Hallowboard, and the crossroads is marked by four stone posts, each with a lamp. In 639 A.G. a table was found laid in the middle of the crossing at dawn, with eight places, steaming. The Company had it burned and the ground salted, and posted a warden on the spot every night since. The wardens rotate weekly. None will say what they see.

**The Groaning Board.** On the Fatlands stretch, two days north of the Chute. The Company's largest inn, with a common room that seats six hundred at a single table, built to impress Fatlands merchants and to feed them five times a day as the law requires. The floor slopes so that grease runs to a gutter. The smell reaches travelers a day out. The dishes are excellent, the portions vast, the meat unspecified. Foreign guests are advised to eat lightly and to watch the table's far end, where the Gut-line merchants sit, and to not be there when the wardens take one away at closing to be rolled out by six men.

**The Chute.** The Fatlands' Rim Road export depot, technically the Renderers' Union's yard and not the Company's, and therefore the one place on the ring where Company law runs only on sufferance. Barrels go out by the thousand a day with the origin stamped over twice. The Company keeps a factor and a weighhouse here and a standing order not to open anything.

**Brackwater Bridge.** On the southwestern stretch, a magnificent stone bridge of eleven arches carrying the Rim over the estuary of the river Slake, which ran into the sea. The sea left in 512 A.G. and the river followed it out across the Uncovered. The bridge now spans a dry bed of cracked mud and the hulls of barges, sitting upright. The Company still charges the bridge toll. The waystation inn, the Dry Anchor, is full of salvagers and Admiralty recruiters, and the cellars flood twice a day.

**Lowmark Spur.** Where the Rim meets the road down to Brinehollow's capital. Travelers coming up from the coast are checked here for the pull: wardens watch who stands too long at the west windows. The inn keeps a rack of bail-buckets for those who forgot theirs and a rope room where any guest may ask to be tied to the bed for the night, at a crumb a knot.

**Knocking Gate.** At the Cradlewrack border on the red-hill road, where Marshal Gideon Breech's men check every traveler leaving the highlands for signs of carrying. The Company's inn here, the Open Door, has no doors at all: every room has a curtain, by agreement with the Attendance, so that nothing can be accused of breaking in. Visitors knock on the frames out of politeness. Occasionally, something knocks back from inside an empty room.

**Wanting Post.** On the inner side of the eastern Rim, at the head of the road into the Fast's only town. The only Company station that faces the middle. The Crumb-Runners buy grain here at a markup the Company calls a risk premium. The Fast's envoy, Prudence Lowell, keeps an office in a bare stone room off the yard and receives all callers standing. Travelers who arrive after dark are asked, at the gate, whether anyone has invited them in on the road. It is not a joke.

**The Weeping Post.** On the northwestern stretch between Cradlewrack and Fogmouth, where weep-rain from Sallowreach meets the warm salt springs of the red hills, and the road is always wet and always tea-stained. A Confessors' chapel stands here, the oldest Theft shrine on the Rim, and a rotating congregation of travelers from both lands kneels in the brown water and asks forgiveness for the Meal. The inn is cheap and damp and everyone in it is sorry about something.

[box: The Rim Road in Play]
The Rim is the default campaign spine. Characters from different lands meet here, under Company law, and can travel together without being arrested at every border. Use the waystations as hubs: each one is a crossroads of factions, a source of jobs (escort, salvage, smuggling, auditing, finding someone who did not arrive), and a stage for the land next door to intrude. A circuit of the Rim is a hundred days; a campaign can be built as a single circuit, beginning and ending at the same gate, with the world measurably worse on the second visit.

## Time, Seasons, and Sky

The year is 641 A.G., After Gorging. Year 0 is the year of the Night of Seven Tables; the nine years of the Long Lack before it are numbered backward by scholars as the Lack Years, Lack 1 to Lack 9, and by everyone else not at all.

The year has four seasons named for a meal: **Grace** (spring), **Plenty** (summer), **Carving** (autumn) and **Lack** (winter). Each season has three months, and the months are named in common Rim usage by the Plate: First Plate, Middle Plate and Last Plate of Grace, and so on. Dates are given as day, Plate and season: "the ninth of Middle Plenty". The year turns on Tablenight.

The moon is called **the Plate**. A full moon is *a full Plate*; a waning one is *being cleared*; a new moon is *licked clean*, and travelers do not set out on licked-clean nights. Stars are called crumbs in most dialects; the Vigil, which cannot see them through its lamp-smoke, has no word for them.

Each land's seasons are bent by its Rule. Sallowreach has no real winter, because winter is an ending, and leaves stay yellow on the branch for decades. The Fast is always early Carving. The Fatlands' first frost is *Marbling*. Oathen has one rain a year, the Kept Rain, on the first day of Grace, and this year it came three days late. The calendar is shared, but the weather is not.

### Tablenight

Tablenight is the anniversary of the Gorging, held on the longest night of Lack. It was once the feast of the longest night, the happiest festival of the old world. Every land now keeps it, and every land keeps it differently.

| Land | Tablenight |
| Sallowreach | The living cook a banquet for their dead, who cannot taste it; everyone pretends |
| Fatlands | A one-day fast in penance; no village has ever reached sundown |
| Brinehollow | A bowl of seawater at the head of the table; the west door unbarred for whoever comes home |
| The Vigil | Every lamp put out for one minute; people hold hands and count aloud |
| Cradlewrack | Every door propped wide until dawn so nothing can be said to break in |
| Oathen | Twenty-four hours without a sound; the only day Oatheners feel safe |
| The Fast | Everyone stands outdoors, dusk to dawn, backs to every door; in every house, the table is laid |
| The Rim Road | Inns close their gates at dusk; one extra chair is set at every table, turned to face the wall |

Nobody knows when the Rim custom began. The Company's oldest inn ledgers record it as already old in 90 A.G. Innkeepers who are asked about it say the chair is *for the one who isn't coming*, and change the subject. Travelers who turn the chair back around in the night find it turned to the wall again in the morning.

## Things All Lands Share

### Table-speech

The common tongue, older than the Gorging, spoken in some form in every land: drawled and wet in the fens, clipped to almost nothing in Oathen, stretched and slurred in the Vigil. Its oldest root words are the ones the Providers' peoples shared, and among them is the root *orr-*. From it come *orren*, to eat; *orrel*, to worship or to give back; *orrish*, a shared meal; and *Orrum*, the name of the Host, meaning roughly *the one at whose board*. In Table-speech the words for "worship" and "eat" share a root. Scholars insist they always did. The Purgation insists this is the whole problem.

Table-speech is rich in food-words used for everything: a coward is *underdone*, a fool is *bread without salt*, death in most dialects is *being cleared*, and a person who has been ruined is *picked*. To ask someone's Cut, politely, is to ask *what portion* they come of. There are two words for *guest*. Everyone uses the newer.

### The Lack

The one coin honored everywhere: a disc of salt-cured bone, the width of a thumb-joint, with a hole through the middle for stringing, worth one day's bread. Ten **crumbs**, small bone chips, make a lack; twenty lacks make a **platter**, a sealed bone tablet issued by the Rim Road Company and sworn in Oathen as redeemable at any Company post. Lacks are cut at the Company's bonehouse near the Chute from Fatlands cattle-bone, salted for a year in vats, and stamped with a closed mouth. The first lacks, cut in the seventh year of the Lack for the public kitchens, were made from other bone, and a few still circulate. They are yellower, and smoother, and people who find one in their change tend to spend it quickly.

### The Grace

Nobody on the Table eats with a stranger without first saying *Lack keep away*. It is said with eyes lowered, hand flat on the table, before the first bite. Among family it is optional; among strangers, it is the law of every land and of the Rim. Skipping it is the universal insult: it says *I do not care if you starve*. Duels have been fought over it in Brinehollow, oaths broken over it in Oathen, and in the Fast, where nobody shares food at all, it is said anyway, to the empty air, before eating out of the hand.

### The Trade in Cures

Every land believes another land's curse might cure its own. Nothing has ever worked. The trade never slows.

Fast dust, which is earth from the Fast's roads, sold by the pinch to Fatlanders who believe it kills appetite. Cradlewrack cord, dried, worn as a belt by Sallowreach elders who want to be able to begin again. Most of it is fraud. Some of it does something, and what it does is worse.

## Powers That Cross Borders

Five institutions operate in every land and answer to none. Each has its own reasons for wanting player characters, and each has its own ways of using them up.

### The Rim Road Company

> Pay the toll. Keep the peace. Say the grace.
>> — the Three Articles, recited at every post at dawn

The merchant league that owns the ring road, its inns, its posts and its tolls. It is neutral, rich, and the only institution every government needs. It was founded in the Thin Years by seven caravan-houses carrying the Fatlands' miracle grain to every market, and its emblem is still a wheel with seven spokes, one for each founding house. In 71 A.G. it swore its Ring Charter in Oathen, and has sworn every contract there since, where they cannot be broken. Every Company platter, every inn-lease, every toll-farm, every bond of service is a promise sworn before the Company's notary, Jessamy Quill, and if broken the breaker's body breaks to match.

The Company is governed by the Wheel, seven Spokes descended by election from the seven founding houses, presided over by the First Spoke. It has no army, only the road-wardens, and no country, only the strip of stone a bowshot wide. It needs none. Every land would starve, go dark or come apart in a season without the Rim.

**Beliefs.** The Company believes in the road. It holds no doctrine about the gods, says the grace at every meal, and regards every land's horror as a pricing problem. Privately, its senior Spokes believe the Table has perhaps twenty years left, and are determined that the Company should be the last institution standing when it ends.

**What it wants now.** Oathen's rain was late. If a god's sworn word can slip, so can every Company contract, and the whole of the Rim's commerce rests on one notary's body. The Company is quietly auditing everything, pricing every land's collapse, and looking for something better than Oathen to swear by.

### The Cutwrights' College

> Blood is a ledger. We only read it aloud.
>> — motto over the gate at Quire

Genealogists who track every bloodline's Cut across six centuries. The College began among the tally-priests of the Bone Dole, who kept the lists of who had eaten what in the public kitchens of the seventh year, and among the scribes who recorded, on the morning after the Gorging, which family had carried home which portion of which god. By 140 A.G. those lists were the most valuable documents on the Table, and their keepers built a walled college-town at **Quire**, on the northeastern Rim between Fogmouth and Lampwick Halt, to keep them.

The College's ledgers decide marriages, inheritances and executions. A College certificate of Cut is accepted in law in every land; a College genealogy can make a bastard an heir, or prove that an heir is a Scrapling. Its Provosts sit in every capital. The northern Provost, Carrion Sedge, has proven that the Hushes follow Hand-lines blood exactly. Whoever holds the ledgers knows exactly where each god is regrowing fastest, and the College holds all of them, in the Master Concordance at Quire, in a vault cut into the hill under the library, kept at a constant cool by spring water.

**Beliefs.** The College is officially doctrine-neutral and in practice dominated by quiet Baiters. Its scholars have seen the curves. They know that every land's Regrowth bent upward at once after 600 A.G., which means that the seven Regrowths are one event, and they do not say so outside the walls.

**What it wants now.** To keep the ledgers secret and whole. To be the institution that understands the end when it comes. And, among the senior Cutwrights, to answer the question of the Eighth, which the College has been forbidden to ask by every land in turn and has been asking quietly since 588.

### The Purgation

> What goes down can come up.
>> — painted on the Purgation's altar-bowls

A cross-border movement that believes the gods can be thrown back up. It was born at the Weeping Post in 233 A.G., when a Fatlands physician named Hurlo Sweyn took a draught of his own devising before a congregation of Confessors and brought up, after six hours of retching, a hard white thing the size of a thumbnail which he swore was one of Ummer's teeth. The Great Retching spread up and down the Rim within a year. Its emblem is a bowl.

The Purgation's methods run from fasting and bleeding to the Black Draught, a purgative of bitter roots, salt and lamp-oil taken in public; to burning, on the theory that a god cooked out of the flesh cannot regrow; to massacre. Its militant wing, the Lancets, holds that a high-Cut house is a swelling in the world's gut and must be lanced. The Lancing of Brookhythe in 488 A.G., when a Lancet company bled out a Gut-line household of three hundred, servants and all, into the drains of their own manor, is its proudest and most infamous act.

**Beliefs.** The Purgation holds the Theft doctrine at its hardest: we stole the gods, the gods are inside us, and the only penance is to give them back by any opening available. Its preachers now call for a second Eldest Walk: the firstborn of every High Cut house, walked to the Rim and emptied, so that this time the giving will be done to the gods rather than for them.

### The Second Table

> It worked once.
>> — the whole of the Second Table's creed, engraved inside its members' signet rings

A hidden cult with one idea: when the gods finish regrowing, eat them again. Its members are mostly nobles, High Cuts of every land, and they are preparing. The first trace of it in any record is a menu found in the coat of a murdered Gut-line lordling in 412 A.G., listing seven courses, each named for a god, in a hand nobody could identify. The College has since collected forty more such menus. They are always seven courses. On the most recent ones, found in the last ten years, there is an eighth line at the bottom, left blank.

The Second Table meets in private dining rooms on the Rim and in the cellars of great houses, a dozen at a time, never more. Members address one another by course: the Fish, the Bread, the Sweet. They practice. They have eaten the fruit of Seated Laden hills, the grey fingertips of Course-stage Hand-lines, the lidless eyes of the Vigil's dead nobility, scraps of caul, and the quiet of a Tenanted heart. They keep notebooks of the effects. They have commissioned knives.

**Beliefs.** The Gorging ended the Lack. It made the eaters' ancestors into lords. If it is done again, by the right people, with preparation, the gods will be put down once more and the world given another six hundred years of grace, with the diners at its head. The first meal was eaten by a mob, shared too widely, and spread thin, which is why it did not hold; the second will be eaten by a few. Some members believe they will become gods. Most just believe they will be fed.

**What it wants now.** Blank organs, to make room. The College's ledgers, to know which gods will be ripe first. The Sayer's sentence, to make the meal certain. And, through the heretic Wont Bellamy of the Fast, the one god still whole: Orrum, the Host, as the first course.

**Joining.** By invitation only. A candidate is brought to a dinner and served a dish without being told what it is. They are told afterward. Those who finish their plate are sworn in Oathen to silence, on their own tongues. Those who do not finish their plate are served at the next dinner.

### The Reckoners

> Refusing was not enough.
>> — Reckoner watchword

Zealots out of the Fast who cross into other lands to kill the Godeaters' highest bloodlines. The first armed band crossed the Rim in 402 A.G.; they have not stopped since. Reckoners travel light, eat only what they carried, and carry nothing that can be traced. They select High Cut families from stolen College genealogies, cross the Hem at night, kill in beds, and are gone by dawn. The Rim Road Company hangs every Reckoner it catches on Company ground, and the Reckoners have never once killed on the road surface. Their full history, beliefs and their great captain Scant Hollis are described in the chapter on the Fast.

[box: Cross-Border Powers in a Campaign]
Every party should have a reason to be on the Rim, and the five powers supply most of them. The Company hires. The College asks questions and pays for answers. The Purgation offers absolution and then a spear. The Second Table invites the most successful characters to dinner. The Reckoners notice whoever has a high Cut and has become inconvenient. Let each power want something from the characters before the characters want anything from it, and let at least one of them be wrong about who the characters are.
